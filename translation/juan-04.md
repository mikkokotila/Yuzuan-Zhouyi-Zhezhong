## 23. Bo 剝 — Splitting Apart

<!-- Source: https://www.eee-learning.com/book/4576 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4576:001 -->

![Bo: Kun below, Gen above](../assets/eee-learning/015af4759f12-yi23b.png) **Bo 剝 — Splitting Apart** — Kun below; Gen above.[^j04-bo]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4576:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4576:002 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Concerning Bo, the *Sequence of the Hexagrams* says: “Adornment means embellishment. Once embellishment reaches its utmost, free passage is exhausted; therefore Adornment is followed by Splitting Apart.” When things reach the stage of patterned embellishment, their development has reached its limit. At the limit there must be a reversal; thus Adornment ends in Splitting Apart. The hexagram has five yin lines and one yang. Yin first arises below, gradually growing until its strength reaches the utmost; the many yin diminish and strip away yang. Hence Splitting Apart. In terms of the two constituent figures, the mountain clings to the earth. A mountain rises high above the earth, yet here it returns to cling to the earth: an image of collapse and disintegration.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4576:002 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4576:003 -->

### Splitting Apart: it is not beneficial to have somewhere to go.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4576:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4576:004 -->

**Original Meaning.** Bo means falling away. Five yin lines below are growing, while the single yang above is approaching exhaustion: yin grows strong and yang diminishes and falls away. This is the hexagram of the ninth month. Yin flourishes and yang declines; petty people are strong and the noble person is afflicted. Moreover, Kun within and Gen without give the image of complying with the time and coming to a stop. Thus one who receives this hexagram in divination must not undertake to go forward.[^j04-months]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4576:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4576:005 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Splitting Apart is the time when the many yin grow strong and diminish and strip away yang. The multitude of petty people undermine and destroy the noble person; therefore it is not beneficial for the noble person to go forward. One should only speak deferentially, conceal one's traces, and wax or wane with the time, to escape the harm of petty people.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4576:005 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4576:006 -->

### First Six: Splitting the bed at its legs. Steadfast rightness is destroyed: misfortune.[^j04-bed-punctuation]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4576:006 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4576:007 -->

**Original Meaning.** Splitting begins below. To destroy what is right brings misfortune; hence this prognostication. *Mie* means to destroy.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4576:007 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4576:008 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Yin's stripping away of yang proceeds from below upward. The bed is the image because it is where the body rests. Splitting from below gradually reaches the body itself. “Splitting the bed at its legs” means splitting the bed's legs. Since the splitting begins below, it begins at the legs. Yin advances from below and gradually destroys steadfast rightness: a way to misfortune. *Mie* means making something nonexistent—the disappearance of the correct Way. Yin strips away yang and the yielding transforms the firm: what is deviant encroaches on what is right, and petty people diminish the noble person. The misfortune is evident.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4576:008 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4576:009 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4576:009 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4576:010 -->

**Yu Yan:** Yin's diminishing of yang advances from below. The first line is at the bottom; hence the image of splitting the bed, beginning with its legs and destroying them below. In this time, when “it is not beneficial to have somewhere to go,” one should only comply with the time and stop. “Persistence: misfortune” warns the diviner that stubborn adherence without knowing how to change brings misfortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4576:010 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4576:011 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Master Yu's explanation takes the word *mie*, “destroy,” with the preceding clause. It evidently proceeds from “destroying what is below” in the *Commentary on the Images*. This, too, may be retained as an interpretation.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4576:011 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4576:012 -->

### Six in the Second Place: Splitting the bed at its frame. Steadfast rightness is destroyed: misfortune.[^j04-bed-frame]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4576:012 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4576:013 -->

**Original Meaning.** *Bian* is the bed's frame. The splitting has advanced upward.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4576:013 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4576:014 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Bian is what separates the upper from the lower part: the bed's frame. Yin gradually advances upward, and the splitting reaches the frame. What is right is destroyed still further, and the misfortune deepens.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4576:014 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4576:015 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4576:015 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4576:016 -->

**Yu Yan:** Having destroyed the first line's legs below, it now destroys the second line's frame in the middle: the splitting has advanced upward. If one who receives this prognostication still clings stubbornly without knowing how to change, misfortune is certain.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4576:016 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4576:017 -->

### Six in the Third Place: Parting from them. No blame.[^j04-parting]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4576:017 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4576:018 -->

**Original Meaning.** The many yin are stripping away yang, but this line alone responds to it. Leaving its own party and following what is right is the way to be without blame. If the diviner acts thus, there is no blame.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4576:018 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4576:019 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** While the many yin strip away yang, the third alone occupies a firm position and responds to the firm. It differs from the yin above and below. Its intention is to follow what is right, and in a time of Splitting Apart it is without blame. The third's conduct may be called good. Why, then, does the text not say “good fortune”? Because the many yin are stripping away yang and the multitude of petty people are harming the noble person. Although the third follows what is right, its situation is isolated and weak, and its correspondent occupies a place without official position. At such a time escape is difficult: how could there be good fortune? The meaning is merely that there is no blame. Saying “no blame” is a means of encouragement.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4576:019 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4576:020 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4576:020 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4576:021 -->

**Xun Shuang:** All the others strip away yang; the third alone responds to the top and has no intention of stripping away or harming it. Therefore there is no blame.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4576:021 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4576:022 -->

**Wang Bi:** It corresponds with the top. While the many yin strip away yang, I alone unite with it. Although situated in Splitting Apart, one can be without blame.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4576:022 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4576:023 -->

**Hu Bingwen:** The third line of Splitting Apart is the fourth of Return. Why is Return's fourth six not promised good fortune, while Splitting Apart's third six is promised freedom from blame? Return concerns the noble person: to illuminate the Way without calculating achievement may rightly receive no promise of good fortune. Splitting Apart concerns petty people. When someone among them alone recognizes the noble person, unless freedom from blame is promised, there is no way to open the door to correcting that person's faults.[^j04-reversed-lines]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4576:023 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4576:024 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Master Wang and Master Cheng both read “in Splitting Apart, no blame” as a connected expression: this is the person who is without blame in a time of Splitting Apart. Considered closely, the *Original Meaning* appears to take “parting from them” as stripping away one's attachment to one's own party.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4576:024 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4576:025 -->

### Six in the Fourth Place: Splitting the bed reaches the skin. Misfortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4576:025 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4576:026 -->

**Original Meaning.** The calamity of yin reaches the body itself. Thus the text no longer says “steadfast rightness is destroyed,” but directly says “misfortune.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4576:026 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4576:027 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** What began by splitting the bed's legs gradually reaches the skin. The skin is the outside of the body; the body itself is about to be destroyed. The misfortune is evident. Yin has grown powerful, and yang has been stripped away severely. The Way of steadfast rightness has disappeared. Thus the text no longer says “steadfast rightness is destroyed,” but directly says “misfortune.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4576:027 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4576:028 -->

### Six in the Fifth Place: A string of fish. Favor through the palace women. Nothing is unbeneficial.[^j04-palace-women]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4576:028 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4576:029 -->

**Original Meaning.** Fish are creatures of yin. Palace women represent the beauty of yin under the governance of yang. The fifth is leader of the many yin and should lead its kind to accept yang's governance. Hence this image. If the diviner acts thus, nothing is unbeneficial.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4576:029 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4576:030 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Splitting has reached the ruler's position: the utmost of Splitting Apart, whose misfortune is evident. Therefore the text no longer speaks of splitting, but establishes a different meaning to open a way for petty people to turn toward goodness. The fifth governs the many yin. Fish are yin creatures, hence their use as an image. If the fifth can bring the many yin into an orderly sequence, like fish on a string, and instead win the favor of the yang above, as palace women do, nothing is unbeneficial. Palace women are the women of the palace: wives, concubines, and attendants. They are invoked as yin and specifically to express receiving affection and favor. With one yang above, the many yin have a Way of following compliantly; hence this meaning.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4576:030 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4576:031 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4576:031 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4576:032 -->

**Master Zhang:** At the meeting of yin and yang, proximity necessarily brings association. Only if the fifth six can attach itself upward to yang and turn back to restrain the many yin, preventing their encroachment, does it find the good Way of dwelling in Splitting Apart. Below, it need not fear being stripped away; above, it receives the protection of yang's achievement. Thus “nothing is unbeneficial.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4576:032 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4576:033 -->

**Xiong Liangfu:** The hexagram as a whole shows yin stripping away yang, with misfortune for yang. The lines, however, show that stripping away yang brings misfortune upon those who do it. Thus the fifth, by complying with the one above, finds nothing unbeneficial; the third, by responding to the one above, is without blame; and the top has the images of the great fruit and the carriage received.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4576:033 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4576:034 -->

**Zhang Zhenyuan:** In Retreat, yin is growing but still weak and may be restrained; therefore yang is told how to restrain yin: “keep servants and concubines in check.” In Splitting Apart, yin's growth has reached its utmost and cannot be restrained; therefore yin is taught how to follow yang: “favor through the palace women.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4576:034 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4576:035 -->

### Top Nine: A great fruit remains uneaten. The noble person gains a carriage; the petty person strips away the roof.[^j04-fruit]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4576:035 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4576:036 -->

**Original Meaning.** A single yang is above. The stripping away is not complete, and it can generate life anew. If a noble person occupies the upper place, the many yin bear that person up. If a petty person occupies it, the stripping reaches its utmost above, and that person destroys his own shelter. There is then no longer an image of the great fruit or the carriage received. The images are clear, but the prognostications differ for the noble and petty person. The sages' intention becomes all the more apparent.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4576:036 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4576:037 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** All the yang have been cut and stripped away; only the top nine remains, like a great fruit left uneaten, in which renewed generation is about to appear. If the top nine also changes, there is pure yin. Yet yang cannot be utterly exhausted: when it changes above, it is born below, without an interval large enough to admit a breath. The sages disclose this principle to show that yang and the noble person's Way cannot perish. Someone asks: “When the stripping is complete, there is pure Kun. How can yang still exist?” The reply: “In matching hexagrams to months, Kun corresponds to the tenth month. In terms of the waxing and waning of vital energy, yang's stripping away makes Kun, and yang's return makes Fu. Yang has never been exhausted: when it is stripped away above, it is born again below. Thus the tenth month is called the yang month, lest anyone suppose it to be without yang. The same is true of yin; the sages simply do not speak of it.” When yin's Way reaches its greatest strength, the resulting disorder is evident. When disorder is extreme, people naturally long for order, and the hearts of the multitude wish to bear up the noble person: “the noble person gains a carriage.” This is why “Not the Wind” and “The Downward Spring” stand at the end of the altered Airs in the *Odes*.[^j04-yang-renewal] As the principle is such, so in the hexagram the many yin look up to yang, an image of bearing it up together. “The petty person strips away the roof”: if a petty person is at the extreme of Splitting Apart, he strips away his own roof and has nowhere to shelter his body. Here the line's yin or yang character is no longer the issue; the text simply says that when a petty person reaches splitting's utmost, it extends to his roof. The roof is an image taken from the position above. Someone asks: “Does not the waning of yin or yang have to reach exhaustion before it is born again below? Why is renewed generation already signified here at the top? And why does the top six of Breakthrough say ‘in the end there is misfortune’?” The reply: “At Splitting Apart's utmost only one yang remains. Since yang cannot be exhausted, the text makes clear the principle of its renewed generation, showing that the noble person's Way cannot perish. In Breakthrough, yang diminishes yin; yin is the Way of petty people. Thus the text speaks only of its disappearance. Why need it further say that renewed generation will follow?”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4576:037 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4576:038 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4576:038 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4576:039 -->

**Master Cheng:** *Xi*, growth, is glossed as generation because in growing, life is generated, without interruption between. The great fruit remains uneaten: this is already Return.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4576:039 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4576:040 -->

**Yang Wenhuan:** The string of fish is the image of the many yin below; the great fruit is the image of the single yang above.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4576:040 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4576:041 -->

**Hu Bingwen:** Qian represents the fruit of trees. All the yang have changed and only the top remains, giving the image of the great fruit left uneaten. Within the fruit is a kernel, where Heaven and Earth's heart of ceaseless generation is preserved. The great fruit speaks solely in terms of the image; gaining a carriage and stripping away a roof also speak in terms of prognostication. A bed is what those above rely upon below for rest; a roof is what those below rely upon above for shelter. At first, by splitting the bed, they sought to deprive the one above of rest. Now, by stripping away the roof, they deprive themselves of shelter. Since antiquity, when petty people have sought to harm noble people, has this truly benefited the petty people themselves?

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4576:041 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4576:042 -->

**Cai Qing:** The *Changes* certainly counsels the noble person. Yet in counseling the noble person it also counsels petty people, as we can see in the words “the petty person strips away the roof.” This is simply how the principle stands. Could the world between Heaven and Earth be without good people for a single day? Otherwise, humankind would perish. Clearly the sages are not merely suppressing one side in order to advance the other.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4576:042 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4576:043 -->

**Qiao Zhonghe:** The great fruit that remains uneaten is the seed, the kernel, the root of ceaseless generation. Since antiquity there has been no trunk that never decays, but there have been fruits that transmit life onward. This is why Splitting Apart gives way to Return.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4576:043 -->

## 24. Fu 復 — Return

<!-- Source: https://www.eee-learning.com/book/4577 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4577:001 -->

![Fu: Zhen below, Kun above](../assets/eee-learning/e3f5dbc85985-yi24b.png) **Fu 復 — Return** — Zhen below; Kun above.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4577:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4577:002 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Concerning Fu, the *Sequence of the Hexagrams* says: “Things cannot remain utterly stripped away. Having reached the limit above, they return below; therefore Splitting Apart is followed by Return.” Things cannot be stripped away completely; thus, when splitting reaches its utmost, they return, and when yin reaches its utmost, yang is born. Yang is stripped away to the limit above and born again below: reaching the limit above and returning below. This is why Return follows Splitting Apart. In the hexagram, one yang is born beneath five yin: yin reaches its utmost and yang returns. In the tenth month of the year, yin's strength has already reached its limit; at the winter solstice a single yang is born again within the earth. Hence Return. Yang is the noble person's Way. Yang wanes to the utmost and returns; the noble person's Way wanes to the utmost and grows again. Thus the meaning is returning to goodness.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4577:002 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4577:003 -->

### Return: success. Going out and coming in without harm. Companions come: no blame. Returning along its course, in seven days it comes again. It is beneficial to have somewhere to go.[^j04-seven-days]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4577:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4577:004 -->

**Original Meaning.** Return means yang being born again below. When the stripping away is complete, there is pure Kun, the hexagram of the tenth month; yet yang energy has already been born below. It accumulates for over a month before the form of a single yang is first completed and returns. Thus the eleventh month's hexagram is Return. Since yang has departed and now comes back, there is a Way of free passage. Moreover, Zhen within and Kun without give the image of yang moving below and advancing upward compliantly. Thus the prognostication also says that one's own going out and coming in are without harm, and the coming of one's companions likewise brings no blame. Again, from the fifth month's Coming to Meet, when one yin first arises, to this point there are seven lines, and a single yang returns: the natural course of Heaven's movement. Thus the prognostication also says that, returning along its course, by the seventh day it should come again. Moreover, since the virtue of firmness is now growing, the prognostication further says that it is beneficial to have somewhere to go. “Returning along its course” means going and coming again, coming and going again. “Seven days” is the period predicted for the return.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4577:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4577:005 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** “Return: success”—once there is return, there is free passage. Yang energy is born again below and gradually develops and flourishes, giving birth to and nourishing the myriad things. Once the noble person's Way returns, it gradually finds free passage and brings benefit to the world. Thus Return contains the principle of flourishing fulfillment. “Going out and coming in without harm”: going out and coming in mean generation and growth. Being born again within is coming in; growing and advancing outward is going out. Going out is mentioned first merely because this is the natural order of the expression. Yang's birth does not come from outside; it arrives within and is therefore called coming in. When things first arise, their energy is exceedingly slight, and they encounter many difficulties. When yang first arises, its energy is exceedingly slight, and it suffers many setbacks. The emerging yang of spring is checked by yin cold, as one can see by observing plants morning and evening. “Going out and coming in without harm” means that the faint yang is born and grows with nothing injuring it. With nothing injuring it, its kind gradually advance and come, and it will flourish; hence no blame. What is called blame means irregularity in vital energy, or, for the noble person, suppression and obstruction that prevent the principle from finding full expression. When yang is due to return, even something that harms it cannot stop its return, but only obstruct it. Yet the hexagram's capacities contain the meaning of freedom from harm: the goodness of the Way of Return. One yang at its birth is exceedingly slight and cannot yet overcome the many yin to bring forth the myriad things. It must wait for the other yang to arrive; only then can the work of generating things be accomplished without irregularity. Thus the coming of companions brings no blame. The three yang are the energies of zi, chou, and yin; the birth and completion of the myriad things are the work of the many yang together.[^j04-three-yang] If the noble person's Way has waned and then returns, how could it immediately overcome petty people? It must await the gradual increase of its companions, who can then prevail through their united efforts. “Returning along its course” means that the Way of waning and waxing returns in alternating succession. Yang wanes and comes again after seven days. Coming to Meet is the beginning of yang's waning; seven changes produce Return. Thus “seven days” means seven successive changes. Approach says “in the eighth month there is misfortune”: this means that eight months pass from yang's growth to yin's growth. As yang advances, yin retreats; as the noble person's Way grows, the petty person's Way diminishes. Hence it is beneficial to have somewhere to go.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4577:005 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4577:006 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4577:006 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4577:007 -->

**Fang Qiao:** “Going out and coming in” with nothing to injure or harm them expresses delight in yang energy's return. “Companions come” with none to condemn or blame them expresses the wish that the many yang should gradually advance.[^j04-fu-readings]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4577:007 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4577:008 -->

**Master Shao:** Return follows Splitting Apart: does this not show order arising from disorder? After Breakthrough comes Coming to Meet: does this not show disorder arising from order? The time! The time! There has never been Splitting Apart without Return, or Breakthrough without Coming to Meet.[^j04-sequence-slip]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4577:008 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4577:009 -->

**Zheng Gangzhong:** Seven is a yang number, and the day belongs to yang; hence the growth of yang is expressed as seven days. Eight is a yin number, and the month belongs to yin. In Approach, the firm grows, but yin supplies the warning; hence the eighth month.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4577:009 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4577:010 -->

**Classified Conversations of Master Zhu:** “Seven days” simply takes the meaning of seven, just as “in the eighth month there is misfortune” simply takes the meaning of eight.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4577:010 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4577:011 -->

**Hu Bingwen:** “Returning along its course” speaks comprehensively of the coming and going of yin and yang: such is its principle. “In seven days it comes again” speaks specifically of a single yang's coming and going: such is its number.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4577:011 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4577:012 -->

**Lin Xiyuan:** The affairs of the world cannot be accomplished by one person alone. To act effectively in the world, the noble person must join heart and effort with those of the same kind. Thus Return emphasizes the coming of companions, and Peace emphasizes advancing with one's kind.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4577:012 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4577:013 -->

**Zhang Zhenyuan:** “Returning along its course” is like saying that one reckons the journey's course back and forth. These two clauses show precisely that Heaven's movement has its own appointed periods, which the noble person must receive and further well.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4577:013 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4577:014 -->

### First Nine: Return before going far. No reaching the point of regret. Supreme good fortune.[^j04-early-return]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4577:014 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4577:015 -->

**Original Meaning.** A single yang is born again below and governs Return. *Zhi* means to reach. Moreover, the line is at an undertaking's beginning. Having gone astray only a little way, it can return to goodness without reaching the point of regret: the Way of great goodness and good fortune. Hence this image and prognostication.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4577:015 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4577:016 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Return means yang turning back and coming again. Yang is the noble person's Way; thus Return signifies returning to goodness. The first is firm yang returning at the hexagram's beginning, the earliest to return: it returns before going far. Return follows a lapse; without a lapse, what return would there be? Only by returning before the lapse has carried one far does one avoid reaching the point of regret: great goodness and good fortune. *Zhi* should be pronounced like *di*, and means to reach. The *Jade Chapters* glosses it as “to arrive,” which has the same meaning. “No reaching regret” means not arriving at regret. Kan says, “Once it reaches the level, no blame,” likewise meaning reaching the level.[^j04-zhi] Master Yan had no outwardly manifest faults. Confucius said that he was close to perfection: this is “no reaching regret.” If a fault is corrected before it takes form, what regret can there be? Since he had not yet attained effortless accord with the mean, or the state in which his desires never overstepped the measure, he still had lapses. Yet he was clear-sighted and firm: whenever something was not good, he never failed to recognize it, and once he recognized it, he never failed to change it immediately. Thus he did not reach regret. This is returning before going far. Lu Deming pronounces 祗 as *zhi*. The *Jade Chapters*, *Written Forms of the Five Classics*, and *Distinctions of Sounds in the Classics* all place the character under the clothing radical.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4577:016 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4577:017 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4577:017 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4577:018 -->

**Yang Shi:** In the first nine, yang has just arisen but has not yet taken form: movement at its subtlest. Good and ill fortune, regret and humiliation arise from movement. To return before it takes form is to return before going far. Thus there is no reaching regret, and supreme good fortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4577:018 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4577:019 -->

**Yu Yan:** The first is at the beginning of Zhen's movement. As soon as movement begins, it returns: returning before going far, the earliest return of all. Thus it does not reach regret, and there is supreme good fortune.

## 25. Wuwang 无妄 — Without Falsity

<!-- Source: https://www.eee-learning.com/book/4578 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4578:001 -->

![Wuwang: Zhen below, Qian above](../assets/eee-learning/651f97adce4d-yi25b.png) **Wuwang 无妄 — Without Falsity** — Zhen below; Qian above.[^j04-wuwang]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4578:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4578:002 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Concerning Wuwang, the *Sequence of the Hexagrams* says: “With return, there is no falsity; therefore Return is followed by Without Falsity.” Return means returning to the Way. Once one returns to the Way, one accords with correct principle and is without falsity. Thus Return is followed by Without Falsity. The hexagram has Qian above and Zhen below. Zhen is movement. Movement according to Heaven is without falsity; movement according to human desire is false. How great is the meaning of Without Falsity!

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4578:002 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4578:003 -->

### Without Falsity: great success. It is beneficial to be steadfast in what is right. What is not right brings fault. It is not beneficial to have somewhere to go.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4578:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4578:004 -->

**Original Meaning.** Without Falsity means the natural spontaneity of genuine principle. The *Records of the Historian* writes “without expectation,” meaning to gain something without expecting it; this meaning, too, is intelligible. The hexagram changes from Contention: a nine comes from the second place to occupy the first, where it also governs Zhen, moving without falsity. Hence Without Falsity. Moreover, of its two constituent figures, Zhen moves and Qian is vigorous; the fifth nine is firm and centered and responds to the second six. Thus the prognostication is great success and benefit in correctness. If one is not correct, there is fault, and going forward is not beneficial.[^j04-wuwang-transformation]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4578:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4578:005 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Without Falsity is utmost sincerity; utmost sincerity is Heaven's Way. Heaven transforms and nurtures the myriad things, generating life without end and bringing each to its proper nature and mandate: this is without falsity. If people can accord with this Way, they “unite their virtue with Heaven and Earth.” Without Falsity contains the principle of great fulfillment. By practicing its Way, the noble person can bring about great fulfillment. Without Falsity is Heaven's Way; the hexagram speaks of people following that Way. “It is beneficial to be steadfast in what is right”: in taking the Way of Without Falsity as one's model, benefit lies in steadfast correctness. To lose steadfast correctness is falsity. Even without an evil intention, if one fails to accord with correct principle, this is falsity—and thus an errant heart. Hence what is not right becomes fault and error. Once one is already without falsity, one should not go further; going further would become falsity.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4578:005 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4578:006 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4578:006 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4578:007 -->

**Classified Conversations of Master Zhu:** Though the hexagram Without Falsity says that calamity and blessing come unpredictably, what one holds to must nevertheless find its benefit in correctness. Their uncertainty must not make our own commitment uncertain. Thus “Without Falsity: great success; it is beneficial to be steadfast in what is right. What is not right brings fault.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4578:007 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4578:008 -->

**A question:** “Even without an evil intention, if one fails to accord with correct principle, this is falsity. But with no evil, how can one fail to accord with what is right?” The reply: “Someone may have a heart entirely free of evil and yet fail to accord with correct principle. For instance, worthy people may go too far. Was there ever evil in their hearts? Yet they fail to accord with correct principle. Nor need the Buddhists have evil intentions.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4578:008 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4578:009 -->

**Qiu Fuguo:** Precisely because there is no falsity, there is no expectation. If one's heart is not yet free of falsity, one may lack any proper way to bring blessing and yet vainly seek it: this is not what is meant by an unexpected blessing. One may commit faults that invite disaster and yet vainly wish to escape: this is not what is meant by an unexpected disaster. All this still inserts deliberate calculation between calamity and blessing; it is not Without Falsity. A person genuinely without falsity is wholly aligned with correct principle, leaving calamity and blessing entirely to Heaven, without a mind bent on unwarranted gain or a fortunate escape.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4578:009 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4578:010 -->

**Hu Bingwen:** Master Zhu explains “sincerity” in the *Doctrine of the Mean* as being genuine and without falsity. Here he explains Without Falsity as the natural spontaneity of genuine principle. The words “natural spontaneity” already include the meaning of having no expectation.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4578:010 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4578:011 -->

**Hu Juren:** Without Falsity is sincerity; sincerity is the reality of Heaven's principle. The sage simply follows the natural course of genuine principle, without the slightest fabrication of private intention. To depart from this genuine principle, this absence of falsity, is fault and error. To follow it in action brings good fortune, with nothing unbeneficial. Should calamity or illness unfortunately arrive, one still holds to this genuine principle without falsity, and it need not cause anxiety. The hexagram and line statements all express this meaning.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4578:011 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4578:012 -->

### First Nine: Without falsity. Going forward brings good fortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4578:012 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4578:013 -->

**Original Meaning.** Firmness within is the governing source of sincerity. Going forward in this way, good fortune is evident. Hence this image and prognostication.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4578:013 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4578:014 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The nine, with yang firmness, governs within: the image of being without falsity. The firm and substantial replaces the yielding and dwells within; its inward sincerity is without falsity. Going forward without falsity, where would one not find good fortune? The hexagram statement says that it is not beneficial to have somewhere to go, meaning that once one is without falsity, one must not go further: excess becomes falsity. The line says that going forward brings good fortune, meaning that action according to the Way of Without Falsity is fortunate.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4578:014 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4578:015 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4578:015 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4578:016 -->

**Lan Tingrui:** At the beginning one should act; at the end one should stop. When action and stopping are appropriate, there is no falsity; without falsity, there is good fortune. The beginning of Without Falsity calls for action, so going forward is fortunate. Its end calls for stopping, so action brings fault.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4578:016 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4578:017 -->

**Hu Bingwen:** The *Commentary on the Judgments* says, “The firm comes from outside and governs within.” Here the *Original Meaning* says, “Firmness within is the governing source of sincerity.” “Governing” is the most forceful word. Falsity is the opposite of sincerity. With sincerity governing in this way, falsity naturally disappears. Going forward thus, good fortune is surely fitting.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4578:017 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4578:018 -->

**He Kai:** This line suffices to encompass the whole hexagram Without Falsity. Zhen's yang first stirs; sincerity remains undivided and single. This is called Without Falsity. Going forward in this way, movement accords with Heaven. How could there fail to be good fortune?[^j04-wuwang-readings]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4578:018 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4578:019 -->

### Six in the Second Place: Do not plow with the harvest in view, or break new ground with the seasoned field in view. Then it is beneficial to have somewhere to go.[^j04-plowing]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4578:019 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4578:020 -->

**Original Meaning.** Yielding, compliant, centered, and correct, it accords with the time and follows principle, without a heart of private intention and expectation. Hence the image of neither plowing and harvesting nor breaking and seasoning the ground. This says that it fabricates nothing beforehand and anticipates nothing afterward. If the diviner is like this, going forward is beneficial.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4578:020 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4578:021 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Whatever is so according to principle is not false. What people desire to contrive is false. Hence the illustration through plowing and harvesting, newly broken and seasoned fields. The second six is centered and correctly placed and responds to the fifth's centered correctness. It dwells in the moving trigram yet is yielding and compliant: its movement can accord with centered correctness and is therefore without falsity. Thus the line expresses the meaning of Without Falsity to the utmost. Plowing is agriculture's beginning; harvesting is its final completion. A field in its first year is called *zi*, newly broken; in its third year, *yu*, seasoned. “Harvesting without plowing, a seasoned field without breaking new ground” means not initiating the undertaking oneself, but following what the principle of the affair requires. Initiating the undertaking is a fabrication of the human heart and therefore falsity. Following what the affair requires is responding to things according to principle, not falsity: this is harvesting and having a seasoned field. Once there is plowing, there must be harvesting; once ground is broken, it must become a seasoned field. This belongs inherently to the affair's principle; it is not something manufactured by one's mind. To act thus is to be without falsity, and without falsity one's going forward is beneficial and harmless. Someone asks: “The sages established institutions to benefit the world; these were all new beginnings. Were they not false?” The reply: “The sages created in accordance with the time and with the requirements of prevailing conditions. They never opened the way before its time. If the time need not be awaited, a single sage could have done everything: why would successive sages have been needed? The time is the beginning of affairs; the sages act in accordance with it.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4578:021 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4578:022 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4578:022 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4578:023 -->

**A question in the Classified Conversations of Master Zhu:** “Cheng's explanation of the line statement may not be clear. I venture to suggest that there can be no harvesting without plowing, nor a seasoned field without breaking the ground. It simply means not calculating the harvest's profit while plowing. Cheng's explanation of the *Commentary on the Images* could be transferred here to explain the line.” The reply: “Though the wording of Cheng's explanations of the line and the Image appears opposed, the meanings are actually close. It is only the expression that is not fully adequate. Under the line he says one should follow principle; under the Image, that one should not calculate profit.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4578:023 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4578:024 -->

**Chen Zhi:** Yichuan's general intention is merely that one should not plow for the sake of harvesting, or break new ground for the sake of having a seasoned field. Whenever one acts for an ulterior purpose, the private calculation of gain is already falsity. Yet the Classic does not phrase it this way, and Cheng's commentary therefore expends considerable explanation. At first, saying “harvesting without plowing, a seasoned field without breaking new ground” means not initiating the undertaking, he seems to treat plowing and breaking ground as private intention. In the middle, saying that plowing necessarily leads to harvesting and breaking ground to a seasoned field, not through mental fabrication, he treats all four as free of private intention. At the end, saying that once one has plowed one must harvest, and once one has broken ground the field must become seasoned, without necessarily acting for the wealth of harvest and seasoned fields, he again seems to treat harvest and seasoned fields as private intention. These three explanations cannot avoid conflicting with one another. Thus the *Original Meaning* simply follows the Classic directly, saying that there is no private calculation concerning plowing, harvesting, breaking ground, and seasoned fields.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4578:024 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4578:025 -->

**Hu Bingwen:** Plowing and harvesting mean sowing and gathering in; breaking ground and seasoning it mean opening land and bringing it into cultivation. A year's agriculture begins in plowing and ends in harvesting; a field's three years begin with new ground and end with seasoned land. The various commentators take “not plowing and harvesting, not breaking and seasoning” as “harvesting without plowing, having seasoned fields without breaking ground.” Only the *Original Meaning* takes it as an image of no deliberate contrivance from beginning to end. It insists upon “according with the time and following principle” because principle is inherently natural and uncontrived. From beginning to end there is absolutely no calculation of achievement or pursuit of profit. Thus the prognostication says, “It is beneficial to have somewhere to go.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4578:025 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4578:026 -->

**Lin Xiyuan:** A field must be plowed before it is harvested, and broken before it is seasoned. One plows precisely in expectation of harvesting, and breaks ground precisely in expectation of a seasoned field. How could there be no plowing and harvesting, no breaking and seasoning? These words are used solely to make clear the complete absence, from beginning to end, of a scheming and calculating heart.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4578:026 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4578:027 -->

**He Kai:** Human falsity lies in expectation. “Not plowing and harvesting” means not expecting to possess the harvest as soon as one begins to plow. “Not breaking and seasoning” means not expecting the field to be seasoned as soon as one breaks the ground. This is how students should remove false-mindedness while never neglecting the work before them. Thus “then it is beneficial to have somewhere to go”: only when one acts in this way is there benefit.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4578:027 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4578:028 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Master He's explanation differs considerably from Cheng's commentary and the *Original Meaning*. Tested against Confucius's teachings to put the work before the gain, the difficulty before the reward, it is especially sound in principle. Moreover, the *Commentary on the Images* explains the line with “not yet wealth,” precisely referring to the absence of an expectant heart fixed upon the harvest. It need not mean that plowing may be abandoned.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4578:028 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4578:029 -->

### Six in the Third Place: An undeserved calamity. Someone has tethered an ox: the passerby's gain is the townspeople's calamity.[^j04-undeserved]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4578:029 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4578:030 -->

**Original Meaning.** All six lines of the hexagram are without falsity. The third six, however, is not correctly placed. Thus whoever encounters its prognostication suffers calamity without cause—as when a passerby leads away an ox and the residents instead suffer the disturbance of interrogation and arrest.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4578:030 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4578:031 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The third is yielding yin, neither centered nor correct: it is false. Moreover, its intention is to respond above. This is desire, and desire is also falsity. Within the Way of Without Falsity, it becomes calamity and harm. People's false actions arise from desire. When false action gains something, it must also lose something. Even if one gains the desired advantage, the action's falsity is already a great loss—how much more when misfortune and regret follow! The wise, seeing a gain obtained through falsity, know that its loss must be commensurate. Thus, from the third six's image of falsity, the sages disclose the principle: “The calamity of Without Falsity: someone has tethered an ox; the passerby's gain is the townspeople's calamity.” This means that falsity such as the third's is a calamity to Without Falsity. Suppose something is gained: a loss immediately follows, as with a tethered ox. “Someone” introduces a supposition. Suppose an ox is tethered and acquired: the passerby obtains it and regards it as a gain; the townspeople lose the ox, and this is a calamity. Conversely, suppose the townspeople tether and acquire a horse: the passerby loses the horse, and this is a calamity. This says that gain brings loss, so the gain is insufficient to count as a gain. “Passerby” and “townspeople” merely express gain accompanied by loss; they do not stand specifically for another person and oneself. Blessings falsely obtained bring calamity in their wake; gains falsely obtained are matched by losses. They are certainly insufficient to count as gains. If people understand this, they will not act falsely.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4578:031 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4578:032 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

### Nine in the Fourth Place: One may remain steadfast. No blame.[^j04-may-be-steadfast]

**Original Meaning.** Yang firmness in Qian's body, without a correspondent below: one may hold firmly and be without blame. This is a prognostication that one should not undertake action.

### Nine in the Fifth Place: An illness without wrongdoing. Do not medicate it; there will be joy.[^j04-no-medicine]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4578:042 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4578:043 -->

**Original Meaning.** Qian's firmness, centered and correct, occupies the honored position, while its correspondent below is also centered and correct: the utmost of being without falsity. If such a person becomes ill, without medication the illness will heal of itself. Hence this image and prognostication.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4578:043 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4578:044 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The nine, centered and correct, occupies the honored position, and below another centered and correct line responds compliantly. This may be called the utmost of being without falsity; nothing can be added to its Way. Illness means something that afflicts it. If the fifth nine, without falsity, has an illness, there will be joy if it is not treated with medicine. When people are ill, medicines and medicinal stones attack and remove what is deviant in order to nourish what is right. But if the vital energy and body are balanced and harmonious, originally free of illness, attacking them with treatment instead harms what is right. Thus not medicating brings joy: joy means that the illness disappears of itself. The “illness” spoken of in Without Falsity refers to such things as governing people who will not be governed, leading those who will not follow, or transforming those who will not reform: falsity afflicts what is without falsity. Shun had the Miao, the Duke of Zhou had Guan and Cai, and Confucius had Shusun Wushu.[^j04-opposition] Once one is without falsity, if something nevertheless afflicts one, one should remain as one is. An illness without wrongdoing need not cause distress. If one then attacks and treats oneself, one alters the absence of falsity and moves into falsity. The fifth has already reached the utmost of being without falsity; the warning therefore concerns action alone. To act would be false.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4578:044 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4578:045 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** “Do not” is a prohibition. The line says that when one is without falsity yet happens to fall ill, one should follow the natural course and the vital energy will recover of itself. Do not add medication and produce other symptoms. Likewise, if someone encounters an undeserved calamity, let matters follow their natural course and settle of themselves. Do not add clever contrivance and produce further blame. Every “do not” in the *Changes* has the same prohibitive force. The illness of this line is like the calamity of the third six. Yet here the text says “there will be joy,” because firmness, centeredness, correctness, and the honored position make its virtue and position genuinely different.

**Cheng's Commentary.** The top nine is at the hexagram's end, the utmost of being without falsity. To act further at the utmost exceeds principle, and exceeding principle is falsity. Thus, if the top nine acts, there is fault and error, and nothing beneficial.

The fourth's permission to remain steadfast without blame is preserving this principle without moving. The third's calamity and the fifth's illness are unfortunate encounters with unforeseen events that one has not caused; the noble person simply accepts them, preserving the principle without being moved from it. Whether acting or remaining still, following principle alone is what makes one without falsity. The top nine is at the utmost of Without Falsity and must not act. If it moves without following principle, it instead becomes false. Fault and lack of benefit are then fitting indeed.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4578:050 -->

**General Discussion — Hu Bingwen:** All six lines are without falsity. The top nine is incorrectly placed at Qian's extreme: its time has passed. Thus, although its action is without falsity, there is fault and nothing beneficial. To learn the *Changes* well, therefore, lies in recognizing the time. The first says good fortune and the second benefit: this is the time. The third says calamity, the fifth illness, and the top fault—not because falsity brought them about, but likewise because of the time. The first and second may both advance: they move when the time calls for movement. The fourth may remain steadfast, the fifth must not medicate, and the top's action brings fault: they remain still when the time calls for stillness.

## 26. Daxu 大畜 — Great Restraint

<!-- Source: https://www.eee-learning.com/book/4580 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4580:001 -->

![Daxu: Qian below, Gen above](../assets/eee-learning/de2ffd027953-yi26b.png) **Daxu 大畜 — Great Restraint** — Qian below; Gen above.[^j04-daxu]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4580:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4580:002 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Concerning Daxu, the *Sequence of the Hexagrams* says: “Only with the absence of falsity can there be storing up; therefore Without Falsity is followed by Great Restraint.” Without falsity there is substance, which can therefore be stored and accumulated. This is why Great Restraint follows Without Falsity. The hexagram has Gen above and Qian below: Heaven within the mountain, an image of something supremely great being stored. *Xu* means both holding back and gathering in. When something is held still, it gathers. Taking the image of Heaven within the mountain, the meaning is storing within; taking Gen's stopping of Qian, it is holding back. Only after stopping is there accumulation; hence stopping is one meaning of xu.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4580:002 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4580:003 -->

### Great Restraint: it is beneficial to be steadfast in what is right. Not eating at home brings good fortune. It is beneficial to cross the great river.[^j04-eating-at-home]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4580:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4580:004 -->

**Original Meaning.** “Great” means yang. Gen restrains Qian, and the restraint is also great. Moreover, Qian within is firm and vigorous, while Gen without is deeply substantial and radiant. Thus one can daily renew one's virtue, making the accumulation great. In terms of hexagram transformation, this figure comes from Waiting: a nine rises from the fifth place. In terms of its structure, the fifth six honors and esteems it; in terms of its virtues, it can also stop vigor. None of these is possible without great correctness. Hence the prognostication is benefit in steadfast rightness and good fortune in not eating at home. Moreover, the fifth six responds below to Qian, which is responding to Heaven; hence the further prognostication that crossing the great river is beneficial. Not eating at home means living on a stipend at court rather than eating at one's own home.[^j04-daxu-transformation]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4580:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4580:005 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Nothing is greater than Heaven, yet it is within a mountain. Gen is above, stopping Qian below. Both are images of storing what is supremely great. In a person, this is learning, cultivation of the Way, and virtue filling and accumulating within: a great store indeed. Everything stored and accumulated falls under this meaning, though the text speaks especially of what is great. A person's inward accumulation should accord with the correct Way; hence “it is beneficial to be steadfast in what is right.” Heterodox or one-sided learning can certainly accumulate a great deal without being correct. Once the Way and virtue fill the interior, one should occupy high office, enjoy Heaven's stipend, and act for the world. Then the good fortune is not merely one's own, but the world's. To remain in obscurity and feed oneself at home is obstruction of the Way; hence good fortune in not eating at home. Once the store is great, it should be applied to the time, relieving the world's difficulties and dangers: this is Great Restraint's use. Hence it is beneficial to cross the great river. This explanation proceeds solely from the meaning of Great Restraint. The *Commentary on the Judgments* additionally speaks from the hexagram's capacities and virtues; the individual lines speak only of restraining and stopping. The *Changes* embodies the Way as circumstances require, selecting meanings that are clear and close at hand.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4580:005 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4580:006 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4580:006 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4580:007 -->

**Classified Conversations of Master Zhu:** In writing the *Original Meaning*, I wanted to explain King Wen's hexagram statements briefly and broadly, following his original intention, and then disclose the reasons behind them under Confucius's *Commentary on the Judgments*. For example, “Great Restraint: it is beneficial to be steadfast in what is right; not eating at home brings good fortune; it is beneficial to cross the great river” merely means that one who obtains Great Restraint finds benefit in steadfast rightness, good fortune in not eating at home, and benefit in crossing the great river. As for “the firm rises,” “esteeming the worthy,” and the like, these are Confucius's elucidations, each with its own focus. The lines and their Images are the same. In this way King Wen's original intention would not be lost, and Confucius's intention would also be visible. But I have not yet had time to put the work in order.[^j04-zhu-revision]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4580:007 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4580:008 -->

**Hu Bingwen:** Not eating at home means that the worthy are sustained not at home but at court. Crossing the great river also seems to suggest restraint reaching its limit and opening into free passage. In any case, the two expressions of benefit and the one of good fortune divide the prognostication into three parts; there is no need to force them into one.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4580:008 -->

### First Nine: There is danger. It is beneficial to stop.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4580:009 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4580:010 -->

**Original Meaning.** Qian's three yang are stopped by Gen, so the inner and outer trigrams each receive their own interpretation. The first nine is stopped by the fourth six. Hence the prognostication is danger in advancing and benefit in stopping.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4580:010 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4580:011 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** In Great Restraint, Gen stops and restrains Qian. Thus all three lines of Qian signify being stopped, while all three of Gen signify stopping the others. The first is yang firmness, also part of the vigorous trigram and situated below: it is bound to advance upward. The fourth six is above, holding it back. How could it oppose the power of one above who occupies the proper position? To challenge that power and advance brings danger. Therefore benefit lies in stopping and not advancing. In other hexagrams the fourth and first are proper correspondents who help one another. In Great Restraint, correspondence instead becomes restraint. The top and the third are both yang, so their intentions unite. Yang things all advance upward, giving the image of a shared intention rather than mutual restraint.[^j04-restraint-correspondence]

One should practice daily with one's carriage and defenses: then going forward is beneficial. The third belongs to Qian and is correctly placed, able to be steadfast. Because it advances keenly, it is warned to recognize difficulty and not lose steadfastness. With the intention so keen upon advance, even firmness and clarity can sometimes go astray. An admonition is necessary.

### Six in the Fourth Place: A young ox's horn-bar. Supreme good fortune.[^j04-horn-bar]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4580:022 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4580:023 -->

**Original Meaning.** “Young” means not yet horned. The horn-bar is a crosspiece of wood fitted to an ox's horns to prevent its butting—the *biheng* mentioned in the *Odes*. Restraining it before the horns develop requires little effort: good fortune of the highest goodness. Hence this image and prognostication. The *Record of Learning* says, “Preventing something before it arises is called anticipation.” This is precisely the meaning.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4580:023 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4580:024 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** In terms of position, the fourth responds below to the first and restrains it. The first is lowest of all, where yang is faint. Restraining it while faint makes control easy: like fitting a horn-bar to a young ox, it is supremely good and fortunate. Speaking generally of the Way of restraint, the fourth belongs to Gen, occupies the upper trigram, and is correctly placed. This is correct virtue in a great minister's position, bearing responsibility for restraint. A great minister's task is to restrain the ruler's errant heart above and the world's wrongdoers below. Human evil is easy to stop at its beginning; once powerful, prohibition meets resistance and is difficult to carry through. Thus, when evil above is already grave, even a sage's remedy cannot avoid opposing the ruler; when evil below is already grave, even a sage's governance cannot avoid punishment and execution. Nothing equals stopping it at the beginning, like fitting a horn-bar to a young ox: supreme good fortune. An ox naturally butts with its horns; hence the bar to restrain it. If it is fitted when the young calf's horns are just beginning, so that the tendency to butt never develops, the task is easy and causes no injury. This illustrates the fourth six's ability to restrain evil above and below before it appears: good fortune of the highest goodness.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4580:024 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4580:025 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4580:025 -->

**Classified Conversations of Master Zhu:** Great Restraint's lower trigram signifies being able to restrain oneself and not advance. Its upper trigram signifies being able to restrain the other and prevent advance. Yet the fourth can stop it at the beginning, so the effort is easy. In the fifth, yang has already advanced before being stopped, so the task is difficult. Yieldingness occupies the honored position, finds the pivotal opportunity, and can bring it under control. Thus there is still good fortune, but not the supreme good fortune of the fourth.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4580:026 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4580:027 -->

### Six in the Fifth Place: The tusk of a castrated boar. Good fortune.[^j04-boar]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4580:027 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4580:028 -->

**Original Meaning.** To stop yang after it has advanced is less easy than at the beginning. Yet yieldingness occupies the center and the honored position, so it can find the pivotal opportunity and bring it under control. Hence this image. The prognostication is fortunate, but does not say supreme.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4580:028 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4580:029 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The fifth six occupies the ruler's position, stopping and restraining the world's evil. When the innumerable people give rise to errant desires, a ruler who wishes to subdue them by force cannot prevail, however exhaustive the laws and severe the punishments. Yet things have governing principles, and affairs have pivotal openings. When the sage grasps what is essential, the hearts of the countless multitude are like a single heart: guide them and they proceed; restrain them and they desist. Thus order is attained without toil. The method is like the tusk of a castrated boar. A boar is a fierce and restless creature, its tusks powerful and sharp. Forcibly controlling its tusks exhausts one's strength without stopping its restless fierceness. Even binding and tethering it cannot change it. But castrate it and remove its sexual potency: though its tusks remain, its fierce restlessness ceases of itself. Such is the method, and why it is fortunate. Taking the castrated boar as a model, the noble person knows that the world's evil cannot be controlled by force. One examines the pivotal cause, grasps the essential point, and blocks the source: evil then ceases of itself without recourse to harsh laws and punishments. Consider stopping theft. People have desires, and the sight of profit moves them. If they have received no instruction and are pressed by hunger and cold, could daily punishments and executions overcome the desires for gain in the hearts of countless people? The sage knows the Way to stop this. Rather than exalting punitive force, he improves governance and instruction, giving people occupations in farming and sericulture and knowledge of integrity and shame. They would then refuse to steal even for a reward. Thus the Way of stopping evil lies simply in knowing its root and grasping what is essential. Instead of intensifying punishment against them, improve governance here: just as, concerned by a boar's sharp tusks, one does not control the tusks themselves but removes its potency.

### Top Nine: Heaven's thoroughfare—how open! Success.[^j04-heaven-road]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4580:030 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4580:031 -->

**Original Meaning.** “Heaven's thoroughfare—how open!” exclaims at the greatness of its free passage. Restraint reaches its utmost and opens out, spacious and unobstructed. Hence this image and prognostication.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4580:031 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4580:032 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** I heard Master Hu say that the words should be “Heaven's thoroughfare: success,” and that *he*, “how,” was mistakenly added. Reversal at the utmost is the constant principle of affairs; thus restraint reaches its utmost and opens into success. Small Restraint is restraint on a small scale, and therefore reaches completion at its utmost. Great Restraint is restraint on a great scale, and therefore disperses at its utmost. At the limit change is due; moreover, yang naturally moves upward, so it disperses. Heaven's thoroughfare is Heaven's road: the empty expanse through which clouds and birds come and go. Its success means vast, open passage without concealment or obstruction. The Way of restraint has changed. This is success through change, not the success of restraint itself.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4580:032 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4580:033 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4580:033 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4580:034 -->

**Zhang Jun:** Firmness above is “bearing.” Bearing means being equal to one's responsibility.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4580:034 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4580:035 -->

**Wang Zongchuan:** The *Commentary on the Judgments* says, “The firm rises and the worthy are esteemed”: this is the top nine. With yang virtue above the fifth, esteemed by the fifth, it gives the image of bearing Heaven's thoroughfare. Heaven's thoroughfare is an open and eminent position. The three yang below advance upward through this line. Thus the third nine says “fine horses race onward,” and its Image says “the intention unites with the one above.” This is how the Way of the worthy finds free passage. *He* is the same word as in “bearing the wooden collar.” The *Explanation of Texts* records Emperor Wu of Liang's reading with the pronunciation *he* as in 賀. This means bearing the world's responsibility in one's own person. At a time when the worthy are being nurtured, one is esteemed by the fifth and upholds the path through which the worthy advance. Never is the fulfillment of the worthy greater than this.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4580:035 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4580:036 -->

**Wu Cheng:** Wang Yanshou of the Later Han says in his *Rhapsody on the Hall of Numinous Light in Lu*, “Receiving Heaven's thoroughfare, thereby attaining great success.” He writes 荷 in place of 何. “Receiving Heaven's thoroughfare” resembles the *Odes'* “receiving Heaven's blessing” and “receiving Heaven's favor.” In Great Restraint, one yang holds still on the outside while three yang are stored within. At restraint's utmost comes dispersal; at stopping's utmost comes movement. Thus, though the top nine belongs to Gen, at restraint's end it no longer stops but moves.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4580:036 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4580:037 -->

**Hu Bingwen:** Releasing something as soon as it has been stored is insufficient to be Great Restraint. Only when storage reaches its utmost does free passage become vast and unobstructed, like Heaven's thoroughfare. This is not merely a prognostication for someone entering office. The *Great Learning's Commentary on the Chapters and Sentences* speaks of exerting effort for a long time until one day all opens out in a comprehensive understanding: the meaning is the same. Those who accumulate virtue through wide knowledge of earlier sayings and past conduct may apply it thus.[^j04-storing-virtue]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4580:037 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4580:038 -->

**Cai Qing:** Considering the meaning of restraint reaching its utmost and opening into free passage, we know that the noble person is concerned only that withdrawal has not gone far enough, not that eventual expansion will fail.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4580:038 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4580:039 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Cheng's commentary regards *he* as mistakenly added; the *Original Meaning* treats it as an exclamation; the other commentators explain it as 荷, receiving or bearing. This interpretation may also be followed. Only the top nine answers to “the firm rises and the worthy are esteemed.” Moreover, it governs Gen and so governs the hexagram. Taking the meaning of esteeming the worthy, this is the path of the worthy opening wide: what the hexagram calls “not eating at home.” Taking its governance of Gen, it can respond to Heaven and stop vigor: what the hexagram calls “crossing the great river.” Thus Heaven's thoroughfare depicts its free passage; receiving Heaven's thoroughfare means encountering a time of free passage. The *Miscellaneous Hexagrams* says, “Great Restraint is the time.” This is precisely the meaning. Master Wu's quotations from the Hymns of Shang are particularly close in sense.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4580:039 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4580:040 -->

**General Discussion — Hu Bingwen:** Other hexagrams take yin and yang as responding to one another; this one takes them as restraining one another. The inner trigram undergoes restraint and signifies stopping oneself; the outer can restrain and signifies stopping the other. Only the third and top, at the limits of the inner and outer trigrams, show restraint reaching its utmost and opening into passage; stopping is not their meaning.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4580:040 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4580:041 -->

**Ye Liangpei:** The hexagram's Judgment takes both restraint and accumulation; the Great Image takes accumulation alone, and the six lines take restraint alone. If the first nine advances, there is danger; only stopping is beneficial. It recognizes difficulty and stops. The second nine occupies the middle Way and can remove the axle-binding and refrain from traveling: it stops when the time calls for stopping. The third nine unites its intention with the top, advancing like fine horses racing onward: restraint reaching its limit and opening into passage. Yet it is still warned to remain steadfast through difficulty and practice its preparations, lest permission to advance become eagerness to advance. The fourth six bears the task of Great Restraint and can stop evil at the beginning, like placing a horn-bar on a young calf whose horns are just emerging: good fortune of the highest goodness. The fifth six has a Way to control evil and grasps its pivotal opportunity. Hence the image of a castrated boar's tusk. Its prognostication is fortunate, but falls short of the fourth's. Someone asks: “The fourth six has supreme good fortune, yet its Image says ‘there is joy’; the fifth six has good fortune, yet its Image says ‘there is blessing.’ Why?” The reply: “In terms of the effort's difficulty, the fourth's is easy, hence supreme good fortune. In terms of the accomplishment's scope, the fifth's is broad, hence blessing.” The top nine's success is restraint reaching its utmost and opening into great passage; hence the image of Heaven's thoroughfare. “Clouds move and rain is bestowed; all under Heaven is at peace.” Is this not how its Way is greatly carried out?

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4580:041 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4580:042 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Danger and the removed axle-binding are like still eating at home and being blocked by the great river. The ox's horn-bar and the castrated boar are like governing the unworthy and broadly relieving difficulty. With the fine horses racing onward, passage gradually opens. Yet one still guards against the roughness of the worthy person's path through daily practice with carriage and defense. Only on reaching Heaven's thoroughfare does the great Way become level and danger and obstruction disappear. Here lies the relation between the meanings of hexagram and lines.

## 27. Yi 頤 — Nourishment

<!-- Source: https://www.eee-learning.com/book/4597 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4597:001 -->

![Yi: Zhen below, Gen above](../assets/eee-learning/cdb75919e5e1-yi27b.png) **Yi 頤 — Nourishment** — Zhen below; Gen above.[^j04-nourishment]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4597:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4597:002 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Concerning Yi, the *Sequence of the Hexagrams* says: “Once things are gathered, they can be nourished; therefore Great Restraint is followed by Nourishment.” Once things are gathered and stored, there must be a means of nourishing them; without nourishment they cannot endure or grow. This is why Nourishment follows Great Restraint. The hexagram has Gen above and Zhen below: two yang lines, above and below, enclose four yin. Above there is stillness, below movement; outside there is solidity, within emptiness: the image of a person's jaws. Yi means nourishing. The mouth takes in food and drink to nourish the body; hence the name. In establishing the hexagram, the sages extend the meaning of nourishment as far as Heaven and Earth's nurture of the myriad things and the sage's nourishment of the worthy, extending through them to all the people. People's nourishment of life, bodily form, virtue, and other people likewise belongs to this Way. Regulating activity and rest, restraint and release, nourishes life; food, drink, and clothing nourish the body; dignified conduct and the practice of rightness nourish virtue; extending oneself to others nourishes other people.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4597:002 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4597:003 -->

### Nourishment: steadfast rightness brings good fortune. Observe nourishment; seek for yourself what fills the mouth.[^j04-observe-nourishment]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4597:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4597:004 -->

**Original Meaning.** Yi is the region beside the mouth. The mouth takes in food to nourish oneself; hence the meaning of nourishment. The hexagram's two yang, above and below, enclose four yin: solid outside and empty within, still above and moving below, it gives the image of jaws and the meaning of nourishment. “Steadfast rightness brings good fortune” means that if the diviner attains correctness, there is good fortune. “Observe nourishment” means examining the Way in which one nourishes; “seek for yourself what fills the mouth” means examining the means by which one nourishes the body. If both are correct, there is good fortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4597:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4597:005 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** In the Way of nourishment, correctness brings good fortune. Nourishing one's body, nourishing one's virtue, nourishing others, and receiving nourishment from others are all fortunate when they follow the correct Way. Heaven and Earth's transformations nurture the myriad things so that each receives what is appropriate: this, too, is simply correctness. “Observe nourishment; seek for yourself what fills the mouth”: examine what people nourish and their way of seeking sustenance for themselves, and goodness and evil, good and ill fortune can be seen.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4597:005 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4597:006 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4597:006 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4597:007 -->

**Classified Conversations of Master Zhu:** Nourishment must be correct to bring good fortune. “Observe nourishment” means examining whether one's nourishment of virtue is correct. “Seek for yourself what fills the mouth” means examining whether one's nourishment of the body is correct. The passage has not yet reached the nourishment of others.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4597:007 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4597:008 -->

**Lin Xiyuan:** People nourish two things: their nature and their body. Neither may be nourished incorrectly. Examine the Way of nourishment: the Way of the sages and worthies in the *Great Learning* is correct, whereas heterodox and lesser ways are not. One must also seek one's own sustenance. Giving weight to the Way and rightness and less to appetite and bodily comfort is correct; being urgent about appetite and bodily comfort while slighting the Way and rightness is not. If both are correct, there is good fortune; if not, misfortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4597:008 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4597:009 -->

**Chen Chen:** Nourishing one's vital energy by accumulating righteous acts, nourishing one's heart by diminishing desire, preserving the sages' Way without sinking into emptiness and nothingness, and honoring their learning without drifting into techniques and calculations: these are correct means of nourishing virtue. In poverty, refusing food offered with a shout or a kick; in office, refusing to eat without earning one's keep; not allowing poverty, low rank, hunger, or thirst to harm one's heart, nor sounds, colors, smells, and tastes to muddle one's nature: these are correct means of nourishing the body.[^j04-nourishment-allusions]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4597:009 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4597:010 -->

**Lu Quan:** “Observe nourishment” means examining whether it is good or not. “Seek for yourself what fills the mouth” means simply taking it from within oneself.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4597:010 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4597:011 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Master Lu's explanation differs from Cheng's commentary and the *Original Meaning*. It says that one examines what one nourishes in order to seek nourishment for oneself. Is it virtue that one nourishes? Then seek for oneself the Way to nourish virtue. Is it the body? Then seek for oneself the means to nourish the body. This agrees particularly well with the language and intention of Confucius's *Commentary on the Judgments*.

### First Nine: You abandon your numinous tortoise and look at me, your jaws hanging in desire. Misfortune.[^j04-tortoise]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4597:012 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4597:013 -->

**Original Meaning.** The numinous tortoise is a creature that does not eat. *Duo* means to droop; drooping jaws show the desire to eat. The first nine, yang firmness below, is sufficient to go without eating. Yet it responds upward to the yin of the fourth six and is moved by desire: a way to misfortune. Hence this image and prognostication.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4597:013 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4597:014 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The first six of Unformed Understanding is the unformed one, yet its line is chiefly spoken from the standpoint of opening that person's understanding. Nourishment's first nine likewise borrows an outside voice. “You” means the first: you abandon your numinous tortoise and instead look at me with drooping jaws. “Me” is established in contrast to “you.” The fourth is what makes the first's jaws droop, but the fourth is not itself the speaker: this is a hypothetical way of speaking. The nine is yang, firm and clear in constitution, with ability and wisdom sufficient to nourish correctness. The tortoise can swallow its breath without eating. The numinous tortoise illustrates clarity and wisdom capable of dispensing with external nourishment. Yet, although its ability is such, it is yang in the moving trigram at a time of Nourishment. Seeking nourishment is what people desire. Responding upward to the fourth, it cannot preserve itself; intent on advancing, it delights in the desired object and its jaws droop. Once the heart has been moved, losing oneself is inevitable. Bewildered by desire and losing oneself, yang follows yin: where will it stop? Hence misfortune. Drooping jaws means moving and lowering the jaws. When people see food and desire it, their jaws move and their mouths water; hence this image.

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4597:015 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4597:015 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4597:016 -->

### Six in the Second Place: Inverted nourishment. Departing from the constant rule, seeking nourishment on the hill. Advancing brings misfortune.[^j04-inverted-nourishment]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4597:022 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4597:023 -->

**Original Meaning.** Seeking nourishment from the first is an inversion that violates the constant principle. Seeking nourishment from the top brings misfortune in advancing. A hill is raised earth, an image of the top.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4597:023 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4597:024 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** A woman cannot establish herself alone but must follow a man; yin cannot stand alone but must follow yang. The second, yielding yin, cannot nourish itself and awaits nourishment from another.[^j04-yi-hierarchy] The Son of Heaven nourishes the world; the feudal lords nourish their states; ministers live on their rulers' stipends; the people depend on the nurture of those who govern them. All are instances of those above nourishing those below: the correct principle. Since the second cannot nourish itself, it must seek nourishment from firm yang. If it instead seeks downward from the first, the order is inverted; hence inverted nourishment. Inversion violates the constant rule and cannot be practiced. If it seeks nourishment on the hill, going forward must bring misfortune. The hill is outside and high, referring to the top nine. The hexagram has only two yang. Since it cannot invert nourishment by seeking it from the first, seeking it from the top nine also brings misfortune in advancing. In a time of Nourishment, correspondents nourish one another. The top is not its correspondent: going to seek nourishment there is reckless action outside the Way, hence misfortune. Inverting nourishment violates the constant rule, so one merely fails to obtain nourishment. Recklessly seeking it above brings misfortune in advancing. Suppose someone lacks the ability to support himself, sees a person above with the power to nourish others, and, though not of that person's circle, rashly goes to seek support: humiliation and misfortune are certain. The second six is centered and correct, usually fortunate in other hexagrams. Why is it unfortunate here? Because of the time. Yielding yin cannot nourish itself, while neither the first nor the top belongs to it as a proper partner. Going to seek support therefore violates principle and brings misfortune.

### Six in the Fourth Place: Inverted nourishment. Good fortune. The tiger gazes intently; its desire follows on and on. No blame.[^j04-tiger]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4597:036 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4597:037 -->

**Original Meaning.** Yieldingness in the upper trigram is correctly placed, and its correspondent is also correct. It relies on that line's nourishment to bestow nourishment below. Thus, though inverted, it is fortunate. The tiger's intent gaze is downward and concentrated; its continuing desire is a seeking that does not cease. If one can also act in this way, there is no blame.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4597:037 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4597:038 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The fourth is above others, a great minister's position. A six occupies it with yin: yielding yin is insufficient to nourish itself, much less the world. The first nine is firm yang below, a worthy person in a low place. It corresponds with the fourth, which is yielding, compliant, and correct. The fourth can therefore follow the first and depend on its nourishment. Nourishment flowing from above to below is the normal order; seeking nourishment from below reverses it. Hence inverted nourishment. Yet, being unequal to its own responsibility, it seeks a worthy person below and follows that person to accomplish its work. The world thereby receives nourishment, while the minister avoids the blame of neglect and failure. Hence good fortune. Those in high positions must have ability, virtue, dignity, and authority, commanding the people's respect and awe, so that affairs proceed and hearts comply. If those below slight their superiors, policies are issued but disobeyed, punishments imposed but resented, and people readily encroach and offend: the source of disorder. Although the fourth six can follow firm yang without neglecting its office, it is inherently yielding and depends on another to succeed, inviting people's contempt. Thus it must nourish its dignity and authority, gazing intently like a tiger. Then its bearing has weight and those below dare not slight it. Moreover, following another must be constant. If the connection is interrupted, its governance fails. “Its desire” means what it needs to employ: this must come continuously, without lack, if its affairs are to succeed. Taking from others without continuity ends in exhaustion. With dignity and authority, and with an inexhaustible ability to bestow, it can be without blame. Why does the second's inverted nourishment violate the constant rule, while the fourth's is fortunate? Because the second is above and reverses the order by seeking nourishment below from someone who is not its correspondent or kind. The fourth occupies a high position but, honored, humbles itself before the lowly. It lets the worthy below practice their Way through it; the intentions above and below respond and benefit the people. What good fortune could equal this? From the third downward, nourishment concerns appetite and body; from the fourth upward, virtue and rightness. A ruler drawing nourishment from a minister, or a superior relying on nurture from below, concerns the nourishment of virtue.

### Six in the Fifth Place: Departing from the constant rule. Remaining steadfast brings good fortune. One must not cross the great river.[^j04-dependent-ruler]

**Cheng's Commentary.** The fifth six occupies the ruler's position in a time of Nourishment and is responsible for nourishing the world. Yet its yielding yin constitution lacks the ability to do so. There have nevertheless been those compelled by necessity to overcome such difficulties. This meaning is set forth as a profound warning to rulers. ### Top Nine: The source of nourishment. Danger; good fortune. It is beneficial to cross the great river.[^j04-source-nourishment]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4597:051 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4597:052 -->

**Original Meaning.** The fifth six relies upon the top nine's nourishment to nourish others: thus all receive nourishment through the top nine. <!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4597:052 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4597:053 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The top nine, with the virtue of yang firmness, bears the responsibility of teacher and counselor. The ruler at the fifth six follows compliantly and depends on its nourishment: this is bearing the world's responsibility, with the world nourished through it. A minister bearing such responsibility must always keep danger in mind; then there is good fortune. Yi Yin and the Duke of Zhou were never without anxious diligence, caution, and apprehension; thus they attained good fortune in the end. Since the ruler's capacity is inadequate and he relies upon oneself, one personally bears the world's great responsibility and should exhaust one's ability and strength to relieve its difficulties and dangers and establish peace and order. Hence it is beneficial to cross the great river. With the ruler's trust so complete and one's charge so weighty, if one did not relieve the world's difficulties and dangers, how could one be worthy of that trust or be called worthy? One should give one's utmost sincerity and strength without shrinking from the task. Yet vigilance in danger must never be forgotten.

## 28. Daguo 大過 — Great Excess

<!-- Source: https://www.eee-learning.com/book/4598 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4598:001 -->

![Daguo: Xun below, Dui above](../assets/eee-learning/7910b8a4c210-yi28b.png) **Daguo 大過 — Great Excess** — Xun below; Dui above.[^j04-great-excess]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4598:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4598:002 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Concerning Daguo, the *Sequence of the Hexagrams* says: “Nourishment means nurturing. Without nurturing there can be no movement; therefore Nourishment is followed by Great Excess.” Things must be nourished before they can mature; once mature, they can move; movement then brings going beyond. This is why Great Excess follows Nourishment. The hexagram has Dui above and Xun below: the marsh is above the trees, submerging them. A marsh moistens and nourishes trees, but here it goes so far as to drown them: the meaning of Great Excess. Great Excess means yang exceeding: the great exceeds, the exceeding is great, and great affairs exceed the ordinary. The Way, virtue, and achievements of sages and worthies greatly surpass those of others; all matters greatly exceeding the ordinary belong here. Yet the sage fulfills the human Way, not exceeding principle. In ordering affairs according to the world's correct principle, corrective measures appropriate to the time may go a little beyond the mean—as in conduct exceeding in reverence, mourning exceeding in grief, and expenditure exceeding in frugality. A slight excess in correction is needed before the mean can be reached: a use directed toward attaining the mean. What is called Great Excess is simply the greater form of ordinary affairs, not an excess beyond principle. Because it is great, it is seldom seen. Compared with what is ordinarily seen it is greater, and is therefore called Great Excess. Yao and Shun yielding the throne, and Tang and Wu banishing and attacking, all followed the Way. The Way is never without the mean or without constancy. Because people do not ordinarily see such acts, they are called a great exceeding of the ordinary.[^j04-corrective-excess]

### Great Excess: the ridgepole sags. It is beneficial to have somewhere to go. Success.[^j04-ridgepole]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4598:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4598:005 -->

**Original Meaning.** “Great” means yang. Four yang occupy the middle in excessive strength, hence Great Excess. The two yin above and below cannot bear their weight, giving the image of the sagging ridgepole. Yet, though the four yang are excessive, the second and fifth attain the center; within is deference and without gladness. There is a Way that can be carried out. Thus going forward is beneficial and brings success.

Both the third and fourth nines take the ridgepole as their image, signifying heavy responsibility. Sagging derives from weakness at the base and tip: strong in the middle but weak at both ends, it bends. Yin is weak and yang strong; noble people flourish and petty people decline. Thus going forward is beneficial and brings success. The ridgepole is what people today call a roof-beam.

### First Six: Lay white cogongrass beneath it. No blame.[^j04-white-grass]

### Top Six: Wading too far, submerged above the head. Misfortune; no blame.[^j04-overhead]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4598:050 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4598:051 -->

**Original Meaning.** At excess's utmost, one's capacity is weak and insufficient to complete the crossing. Yet in terms of rightness there is no blame: this is giving up one's life to fulfill humaneness. Hence this image and prognostication.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4598:051 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4598:052 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The top six is yielding yin at excess's utmost: a petty person exceeding the ordinary to the extreme. What is called Great Excess in a petty person is not the ability to accomplish things greatly surpassing other people's. It is merely exceeding ordinary bounds and transgressing principle, disregarding danger and destruction, treading into peril and calamity—like wading too far into water until even the top of the head is submerged. The misfortune is evident. A petty person brings calamity upon himself through reckless agitation: it is fitting. Whom else can he blame? Hence “no blame”: he has done it himself and has no one else to resent or blame. The image of the marsh gives rise to the meaning of wading.

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4598:053 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4598:053 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4598:054 -->

**Qian Zhili:** The marsh submerges the trees; this is why the top is submerged above the head. Though the water goes above one's head, there are circumstances in which one cannot refrain from crossing and therefore must exceed ordinary limits. This is why Confucius, contemplating the hexagram's image, conceived the thought of standing alone without fear.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4598:054 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4598:055 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Cheng's commentary takes this line as a petty person treading into danger and calamity; the *Original Meaning* takes it as a noble person giving up his life to fulfill humaneness. The *Original Meaning* is certainly preferable to Cheng's explanation. Yet another interpretation is possible: at Great Excess's utmost, nothing can be done. The yielding top six governs gladness and can therefore accommodate itself calmly, without rigid agitation that would add to the situation's weight. Thus, though facing the misfortune of wading too far and being submerged above the head, it is without blame. At the end of the Eastern Han, men such as Fan and Li merely drove the waves higher, rather than finding a way to rescue their time. Moreover, the top six occupies no official position. The noble person does not condemn flexible, harmonious conduct such as that of Shentu Pan and Guo Tai. This interpretation may also be preserved.[^j04-late-han]

**General Discussion.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4598:056 -->

**Feng Yi:** In the *Changes*, figures whose upper and lower strokes balance one another generally take their images by dividing at the center and viewing the halves in reverse, rather than through the usual correspondence of other hexagrams. Nourishment, Inner Sincerity, and Small Excess all do this, but it is particularly clear here. The third and fourth oppose each other, both as ridgepoles: the upper rises and the lower sags. The second and fifth oppose each other, both as withered poplars: flowers above and root-shoots below. The first and top oppose each other: the first has the caution of cushioning with white grass, the top the misfortune of wading too far and being submerged above the head.

**Gong Huan:** Great Excess fundamentally means yang exceeding. If yang additionally occupies a yang position, the excess increases. Thus yang lines in yin places are all fortunate, while yang lines in yang places are all unfortunate. The principle is roughly the same as in the lines of Great Strength.

## 29. Kan 坎 — Repeated Danger

<!-- Source: https://www.eee-learning.com/book/4599 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4599:001 -->

![Kan: Kan below, Kan above](../assets/eee-learning/a883ee04ffd8-yi29b.png) **Kan 坎 — Repeated Danger** — Kan below; Kan above.[^j04-repeated-danger]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4599:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4599:002 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Concerning Repeated Danger, the *Sequence of the Hexagrams* says: “Things cannot remain forever in excess; therefore Great Excess is followed by Kan. Kan means falling into a pit.” There is no principle of excess continuing without end. At excess's utmost, one must fall into a pit; thus Kan follows Great Excess. *Xi* means repetition. Though the other trigrams are also doubled, their names receive no addition. Only Kan adds xi to show danger redoubled, danger again within danger: a meaning of great importance. The trigram has one yang in the middle and two yin above and below. Yang is solid, yin empty; with no support above or below, one yang is caught between two yin. Hence Kan's meaning of entrapment. Yang within yin is entrapment; yin within yang is attachment. A yang above gives the image of stopping; in the middle, entrapment; below, movement. A yin above gives the image of gladness; in the middle, attachment; below, deference. Entrapment makes danger. Xi means doubling: study through practice and review both imply repetition. Kan means entrapment; the hexagram speaks of the Way of dwelling amid dangers and difficulties. Kan is water. The one begins in the middle and is the first of things generated; hence water. Falling into hollows is water's form.[^j04-trigram-structure]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4599:002 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4599:003 -->

### Repeated Danger: there is sincerity; the heart finds free passage. Action is worthy of esteem.[^j04-heart-passage]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4599:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4599:004 -->

**Original Meaning.** Xi means repetition. Kan means a dangerous pit. Its image is water: yang is trapped within yin, empty outside and solid at the center. In this hexagram both upper and lower are Kan: repeated danger. The solid center is the image of sincerity and the heart's free passage. Acting through this, one must accomplish something. Hence this prognostication.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4599:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4599:005 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Yang solidity at the center means sincerity and trust within. “The heart finds free passage”: precisely because the heart is sincere and undivided, it can find free passage. Utmost sincerity can penetrate metal and stone and tread through water and fire. What danger or difficulty could not be passed through? “Action is worthy of esteem” means that acting with sincere singleness enables one to emerge from danger and accomplish something worthy of praise and esteem. Without action, one would remain in danger forever.[^j04-kan-readings]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4599:005 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4599:006 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4599:006 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4599:007 -->

**Kong Yingda:** Kan names dangerous entrapment; xi means becoming practiced and familiar. Dangerous and difficult affairs cannot be undertaken without acquired familiarity. Thus one must become practiced in Kan before affairs can be put into effect: hence Repeated Danger. Note that the names of the other hexagrams receive no additional word. Kan alone adds xi because Kan is danger and difficulty; hence the special addition of “practice” to its name.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4599:007 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4599:008 -->

**Hu Yuan:** This hexagram belongs to the eight pure figures. The other seven each have a one-character name; why does this one alone add xi? Qian chiefly signifies vigor, Kun compliance, and so forth: one character generally suffices to express the meaning. But here both above and below are dangerous, giving a situation of difficulties piled upon difficulties. The noble person must accumulate familiarity and practice beforehand, and only then can overcome such obstacles. This is why the sages specially added xi.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4599:008 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4599:009 -->

**Su Shi:** Kan is dangerous terrain: that through which water runs, not water itself. Only water can repeatedly make its way through danger. Naming it not simply Kan but Repeated Kan takes its meaning from water.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4599:009 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4599:010 -->

**Lü Dalin:** Repeated Danger means being tested again in the greatest difficulty. Of the eight trigrams, Qian is vigorous, Kun compliant, Zhen moving, Gen still, Li bright, Kan dangerous, Xun penetrating, and Dui glad. Danger alone is not an auspicious virtue and is not something the noble person adopts. Thus Kan alone is named Repeated Danger. Repeated trial in doubled danger is indeed something with which the noble person must concern himself.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4599:010 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4599:011 -->

**Xue Wenqi:** Kan is not itself a thing to put to use; practice makes it usable. Thus its name differs from other hexagrams. It speaks of the person who makes use of Kan.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4599:011 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4599:012 -->

**Zhang Jun:** Xi describes proceeding at ease without cessation. Through practice in Kan, danger can be escaped. Yang is trapped in yin; unless it emerges from danger, accomplishment has no way to arise. “Practicing Kan” is seeking a way out of danger.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4599:012 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4599:013 -->

**Zheng Ruxie:** Both becoming familiar through practice and reviewing what one has learned imply repetition. Though water is exceedingly dangerous, those practiced in water can enter and leave it without drowning. Thus those practiced in danger and difficulty can be at ease wherever they enter.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4599:013 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4599:014 -->

**Li Shunchen:** Kan's solid center is sincerity; Li's empty center is clarity. The solid center is Kan's function; the empty center, Li's. Those who made the *Changes* took the centers of Kan and Li to embody the functions of sincerity and clarity: the ancient sages' learning of the heart.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4599:014 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4599:015 -->

**Hu Bingwen:** In other hexagrams, the *Original Meaning* regularly treats “success” as a prognostication. Here alone it says that the solid center is an image of sincerity and the heart's free passage. Elsewhere it is success in affairs; here it is free passage of the heart. Yang's solidity is the image of sincerity; yang's brightness, the image of the heart's free passage.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4599:015 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4599:016 -->

**Zhang Huang:** Among the sixty-four hexagrams, Kan alone explicitly points to the heart to instruct people. Clearly the heart within the body is truly like one yang enclosed between two yin. This is what is meant by “the heart of the Way is subtle.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4599:016 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4599:017 -->

**Wu Yueshen:** Yang trapped within yin makes Kan. Solidity at the center and sincerity provide the means of dwelling in danger. With sincerity, genuineness is established; with the heart's free passage, clarity opens through. The heart's underlying constitution is still and constantly luminous, like one yang hidden between two yin. Its functioning moves without ceasing, like the flowing of one yang between two yin. The one yang is the underlying reality of the flow; the two yin are the boundaries of its location. Flowing without exceeding its bounds is movement that is still; bounded yet flowing at ease is stillness that moves. How fully the meanings of sincerity and the heart's free passage are disclosed in Repeated Danger!

### Six in the Fourth Place: A jar of wine, a gui vessel; supplement with earthenware. Establish the bond through the window. In the end, no blame.[^j04-wine-vessels]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4599:038 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4599:039 -->

**Original Meaning.** Master Chao says that earlier scholars read “a jar of wine, a gui vessel” as one clause, and “supplement with earthenware” as another. We follow them here. *Er* means to add or replenish. Examples are the *Rites of Zhou's* “three replenishments at a great sacrifice,” and the *Duties of Disciples*: “Holding an empty dou vessel in the left hand and the serving implement in the right, turn about and replenish.” The fifth nine occupies the honored position, and the fourth six is close to it. In a time of danger, firmness and yieldingness meet. Thus there is the image of using only modest ceremonial gifts, supplementing them with a sincere heart, and advancing to form a bond through the window. The window is not the proper entrance, but it is where the room receives light. Though the beginning is difficult and obstructed, in the end there is no blame. Hence this prognostication.[^j04-window]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4599:039 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4599:040 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The fourth six is yielding yin with no help below, unable to relieve the world's danger. Since it occupies high office, the text speaks of a minister's Way of dwelling in danger. At a time of difficulty, a great minister can be assured of freedom from blame only if utmost sincerity wins the ruler's trust, making their association firm and invulnerable to interference, and if he can also open and illuminate the ruler's heart. To win deep trust from above, one need only exhaust one's unadorned sincerity. Nothing has more ceremony or prized ornament than a banquet, so the banquet provides the illustration. This says not to value superficial display, but substance alone: one jar of wine, two gui vessels of food, and earthenware utensils—the utmost of simplicity. With such simplicity, one must also “establish the bond through the window.” Establishing the bond means advancing into a connection with the ruler; the window signifies opening a passage. Because the room is dark, a window is made to let light through. “Through the window” means through the place open to light, illustrating the illuminated part of the ruler's heart. The *Odes* says, “Heaven's opening of the people's understanding is like the ocarina and flute sounding together.” Master Mao explains “opening” as guiding, likewise meaning opening a passage. A minister who binds himself to the ruler's heart through loyalty, trust, and the good Way must enter through what the ruler already understands. The human heart has places that are obscured and places that are open: the obscured are dark, the open bright. Address the person through the bright place, and trust is easier to obtain. Hence “establish the bond through the window.” Acting thus, even in a time of difficulty and danger, one is finally without blame. Suppose a ruler's heart is obscured by dissolute pleasures: precisely because it is obscured, it acts so. However forcefully one condemns those pleasures, what can be done if the ruler will not recognize the wrong? One must begin with something unobscured and extend from it to the matter at hand; then his heart can awaken. Since antiquity, those able to remonstrate successfully with rulers have always proceeded from what the ruler understood. Thus harsh denunciation and forceful insistence generally provoke opposition, while warm-heartedness and lucid reasoning more often prevail. Han's founder, for example, loved Lady Qi and intended to replace the heir apparent: this was his obscurity. Many ministers disputed with him. The distinction between the principal wife's son and a concubine's, and the order of elder and younger, were not unclear—but what could they do when his obscurity prevented him from discerning them? The Four Elders were men whose worth Gaozu had long recognized and respected: this was the unobscured clarity of his heart. Proceeding from that clarity to the matter at hand awakened him as readily as turning a hand. Yet how could those four men's power compare with Zhang Liang, the great ministers, and the scholars of the world? How could their words match the urgency of Zhou Chang's and Shusun Tong's? That he followed these rather than those arose simply from the difference between attacking his obscurity and approaching his clarity. Likewise, the queen dowager of Zhao loved her younger son, the Lord of Chang'an, and refused to send him as hostage to Qi: private affection obscured her. Though the great ministers remonstrated forcefully, how could she listen while obscured? Her love for her son and desire for his lasting wealth and honor were what her heart understood. Thus Chu Long, the Left Counselor, began from that understanding and guided her toward a lasting plan. She responded like an echo.[^j04-remonstrance] This applies not only to addressing a ruler but also to teaching. Teaching must begin with people's strengths: their strengths are where their hearts are illuminated. Enter through what their hearts understand, then extend to the rest. This is what Mencius calls completing virtue and developing ability.

**Collected Explanations.**

The first and third entering the pit within the pit, and the top failing to attain for three years, instead show yin's entrapment as more severe. Differences between the meanings taken by hexagrams and their lines often work this way.

![Li: Li below, Li above](../assets/eee-learning/16dc5a89f6c2-yi30b.png) **Li 離 — Clinging** — Li below; Li above.[^j04-clinging]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4600:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4600:002 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Concerning Li, the *Sequence of the Hexagrams* says: “Kan means entrapment. What is trapped must attach to something; therefore Kan is followed by Li. Li means attaching.” Amid danger and difficulty, one necessarily has something to cling to: this is natural principle. Thus Li follows Kan. Li means attachment and brightness. Taking its yin as attached to the yang above and below gives the meaning of clinging; taking its empty center gives the meaning of brightness. Li is fire: empty in constitution, it clings to things and shines. It also represents the sun, likewise through the image of empty brightness.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4600:002 -->

### Clinging: it is beneficial to be steadfast in what is right. Success. Nurturing a cow brings good fortune.[^j04-cow]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4600:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4600:004 -->

**Original Meaning.** Li means attachment. Yin clings to yang. Its image is fire, yin in underlying constitution and yang in activity. What things cling to should above all be correct. A cow is a yielding, compliant creature. Thus, if the diviner can be correct, there is success; nurturing a cow brings good fortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4600:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4600:005 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Li means attachment. Nothing among the myriad things is without something to which it attaches. With form comes attachment. For people, the persons they draw close to, the Way they follow, and the affairs they undertake are all what they attach to. Benefit in one's attachments lies in steadfast correctness. When attachment is correct, there can be free passage. Thus “Clinging: it is beneficial to be steadfast in what is right; success.” “Nurturing a cow brings good fortune”: cattle are compliant in nature, and a female all the more so—the utmost of compliance. Having attached to what is correct, one must be able to follow the correct Way compliantly, like a cow; then there is good fortune. Nurturing a cow means nourishing the virtue of compliance. This virtue is completed through cultivation. Having attached to what is correct, one should nourish and practice it to complete one's compliant virtue.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4600:005 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4600:006 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4600:006 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4600:007 -->

**Wang Bi:** As a hexagram, Li takes yieldingness as correct. Thus steadfast correctness must precede success. Yieldingness within, treading the correct center, is the goodness of the female. Strength outside and compliance within are the goodness of cattle. Li's constitution is governed by yielding compliance. Thus nurturing something fierce and forceful will not do; nurturing a cow is fortunate.

**Wu Cheng:** Both cattle and the female belong to Kun's imagery. Li's one yin in the middle is Kun's middle line; hence the image of a cow.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4600:010 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4600:011 -->

**Hu Bingwen:** Kan's brightness is within, carried outward through firmness and vigor. Li's brightness is without, and should be nourished inwardly through yielding compliance.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4600:011 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4600:012 -->

**Wu Yueshen:** Kan's nature is to descend; descent without stopping enters the pit within the pit. Li's nature is to flame upward; excessive flaming brings sudden eruption and burning. Kan's sinking resembles desire; Li's flaming resembles anger. In Kan, “the heart finds free passage”: firm centeredness prevents sinking. In Li, “nurturing a cow”: centered compliance prevents eruption.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4600:012 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Masters Hu and Wu are especially apt on nurturing the cow. Li is clarity. When lofty brightness is governed through yieldingness, one can employ clarity without causing harm.[^j04-water-fire]

### First Nine: Footsteps cross and recross. Attend with reverence: no blame.[^j04-footsteps]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4600:014 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4600:015 -->

**Original Meaning.** Firmness below, within the bright trigram, intends to advance upward. Hence the image of crossing footsteps. Attending with reverence brings freedom from blame. The diviner is admonished to act thus.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4600:015 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4600:016 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Yang naturally delights in movement; moreover, it occupies the lower place in Li's body. Yang below wishes to advance, while Li's nature flames upward. Its intention to attach above approaches restless movement. “Footsteps cross and recross” means intersecting movements. Though it has not yet advanced, its tracks already show movement. Moving would transgress the proper role of one below and bring blame. Yet with its firm and clear capacity, if it understands what is fitting and attends reverently and cautiously, it need not reach blame. The first is below, without official position. Understanding one's own advance and withdrawal is the Way of correct attachment. Once the intention is stirred, without reverent caution movement becomes reckless. This fails to understand what one attaches to and brings blame.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4600:016 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4600:017 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4600:017 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4600:018 -->

**Kong Yingda:** Situated at Li's beginning, one's steps are always cautious and reverent amid their crossings, never daring to settle into ease. Hence “footsteps cross and recross; attend with reverence: no blame.” With such reverence, one can avoid calamity and be without blame.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4600:018 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4600:019 -->

**Wang Zhaosu:** It stands at the beginning of the myriad things becoming visible to one another, a time of stepping into their manifold crossings.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4600:019 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4600:020 -->

**Hu Yuan:** “Crossing and recrossing” describes reverent caution. At Li's beginning, like the sun first rising, and at the start of affairs, one should always be alert and apprehensive, advancing virtue and cultivating one's work. This is how blame is avoided.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4600:020 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4600:021 -->

**Feng Dangke:** The sun is just rising: the morning when people rise early. Crossing footsteps are the beginning of movement. Bring reverence to the beginning, and in the end there must be good fortune. The subtle beginnings of calamity and blessing often sprout with the first movement; hence the warning at the start.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4600:021 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4600:022 -->

**Zhao Yansu:** With reverence, movement and intercourse with things are all Heaven's principle. Without reverence, one is enslaved by things and blame arises. People set to work at sunrise; hence this image.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4600:022 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4600:023 -->

**Hu Yigui:** “Crossing and recrossing” means the complex intersection of affairs and things. With reverence, the heart has a governing center, and its responses remain undisturbed: blame can be avoided. Without reverence, the reverse is true.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4600:023 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4600:024 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Masters Wang, Feng, and Hu are right about crossing footsteps. The manifold crossings are the beginning of responding to things; reverence is the root of nourishing luminous virtue. The heart's virtue is clear with reverence and obscure without it. Those who know reverence at the beginning of responding to things seldom approach blame.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4600:024 -->

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4600:025 -->

Cultured clarity with centered correctness is beauty at its height; hence yellow brightness. With the virtues of cultured clarity and centered correctness, it unites above with a ruler of cultured clarity and centered compliance. Its clarity is such, and its attachment such: good fortune of the highest goodness.

### Nine in the Third Place: The light of the declining sun. If one does not beat the earthenware pot and sing, there comes the lament of great old age. Misfortune.[^j04-declining-sun]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4600:034 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4600:035 -->

**Original Meaning.** Between the two Li trigrams, the earlier light is about to end: the image of the declining sun. Unless one rests content with the ordinary course and finds joy in it, one cannot handle one's situation and there is misfortune. The diviner is admonished to act accordingly.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4600:035 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4600:036 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** All eight pure hexagrams have meanings arising from their two constituent bodies. Qian is vigorous within and without; Kun compliant above and below; Zhen's awe-inspiring thunder follows thunder; Xun above and below follows compliantly; Kan repeats doubled danger; Li's two lights shine in succession; Gen is still within and without; Dui is mutual gladness between self and other. Yet Li's significance in human affairs is greatest. The third nine is at the lower trigram's end, when the earlier light is about to end and the later should succeed it: the beginning and ending of human life, the changes of the time. Hence the light of the declining sun, the brightness of the sun as it inclines downward. Having declined, it is about to disappear. In principle, flourishing must have decline and beginning must have ending: the constant Way. Those who understand take joy in following principle. The earthenware pot is an everyday utensil. Beating the pot and singing means taking joy in the ordinary course. Unable to do so, one laments and worries over great old age, bringing misfortune. Great old age means sinking and disappearance, the ending and exhaustion of human life. Those who understand recognize the constant principle and simply rejoice in Heaven. Whatever ordinary course they encounter, they rejoice, like beating the pot and singing. Those who do not understand are fearful and grieved at the coming end: the lament of great old age, and their misfortune. This is the Way of meeting life and death. The character 耋 is equivalent to 昳, declining.[^j04-li-readings]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4600:036 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4600:037 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4600:037 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4600:038 -->

**Xun Shuang:** The first is sunrise, the second noon, the third the sun's decline.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4600:038 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4600:039 -->

**Liang Yin:** The third stands at the end of lower Li, the time of the sun's decline. Only a centered and correct noble person can maintain fullness and steady what is falling. The third is at the sun's evening, yet excessively firm and uncentered; its intention runs wild. Thus “if one does not beat the pot and sing, there comes the lament of great old age.” Its singing is joy that has lost its proper measure; its lamenting is grief that has lost its proper measure. With joy and grief both out of measure, how could there be no misfortune? At such a time, the noble person's concern for danger and sincere joy in Heaven proceed together without conflict. He certainly has no leisure for singing; yet why should he descend into lamentation?

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4600:039 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4600:040 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Master Liang alone grasps the line's meaning. The declining sun illustrates the heart's obscuration, not a change in outward circumstances.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4600:040 -->

### Nine in the Fourth Place: Abruptly it comes. Burning; dying; cast aside.[^j04-sudden-fire]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4600:041 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4600:042 -->

**Original Meaning.** At the time when the later light is about to succeed, the fourth nine presses upon it with firmness. Hence this image.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4600:042 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4600:043 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The fourth nine leaves the lower body and rises into the upper, the beginning of succeeding light. Thus the text speaks of succession. Above and near the ruler, it stands in the place of succession. Yang occupies Li's body at the fourth, firm and restless, neither centered nor correct. Moreover, firmness is redoubled without correctness. Its powerful momentum arrives abruptly: it is no good successor. A good successor must have sincere deference and yielding, the Way of receiving compliantly, like Shun or Qi. The fourth's abrupt arrival loses the Way of good succession. Moreover, it supports the yielding yin ruler of the fifth six, while its overpowering firmness scorches upward, its aggressive blaze like burning. Hence “burning.” With conduct so bad, the fourth must suffer calamity and harm; hence “dying.” It loses both the fitting Way of succession and the Way of supporting the one above. Both are contrary virtues, rejected by the multitude; hence “cast aside.” Death and rejection are calamity's utmost, making an explicit mention of misfortune unnecessary.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4600:043 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4600:044 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4600:044 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4600:045 -->

**Zhang Huang:** Clarity in a person is like fire in wood. Fire lodges in wood yet can burn it; clarity is rooted in a person yet can harm that person. Everything depends upon how it is used. The fourth nine is neither centered nor correct; its firm energy is rash and violent, with harm such as this.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4600:045 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4600:046 -->

**He Kai:** The third is at the end of the lower trigram, like the sun past noon. The fourth is at the beginning of the upper trigram, like fire suddenly blazing fiercely.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4600:046 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4600:047 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Li is luminous virtue. Succeeding light is what is meant by “continuously maintaining shining brightness”: its illumination never ceases. This has nothing to do with hereditary succession. Earlier scholars interpreted the two lights as generations succeeding one another, so Cheng's commentary used that explanation for the fourth nine. This seems remote from the Classic's intention. Masters Zhang and He are right to speak of rash violence and sudden intensity: the fault is failing to nourish clarity through the virtue of compliance.[^j04-successive-light]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4600:047 -->

### Six in the Fifth Place: Tears pour down; sorrow and sighing. Good fortune.[^j04-tears]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4600:048 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4600:049 -->

**Original Meaning.** Yin occupies the honored position; yieldingness clings to the center. Yet it is not correctly placed and is pressed by yang above and below. Only after such anxiety and apprehension does it attain good fortune. The diviner is admonished to act thus.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4600:049 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4600:050 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The fifth six occupies the honored position and keeps the center, possessing the virtue of cultured clarity: it may be called good. Yet yieldingness above has no help below and clings alone between firm forces: a situation of danger and apprehension. Precisely because of its clarity, its fear can be so deep that tears flow, and its anxious consideration so deep that it sighs in sorrow. This is how it preserves good fortune. Tears and sorrowful sighs merely express the depth of its anxiety and apprehension: the time calls for them. In the honored position, with cultured clarity and such awareness of danger, it attains good fortune. If it relied upon its cultured clarity and its attachment to centered correctness, resting at ease without fear, how could it preserve good fortune?

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4600:050 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4600:051 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4600:051 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4600:052 -->

**Cai Yuan:** The functioning of Kan and Li lies in the center. The second and fifth are each at a trigram's center. In Kan, the fifth is correctly placed and the second is not, so the fifth is superior. In Li, the second is correctly placed and the fifth is not, so the second is superior.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4600:052 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4600:053 -->

**Liu Dingzhi:** Kan is a hexagram of yin danger; only firmness is sufficient to overcome it—governing what is deep and submerged through firmness. Li is a hexagram of yang restlessness; only yieldingness is sufficient to harmonize it—governing lofty brightness through yieldingness. The second and fifth both arrive at good fortune because of their yieldingness.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4600:053 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4600:054 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Only the second six attains the correctness of luminous virtue. The third's singing and sighing and the fourth's abrupt arrival show luminous virtue obscured and one's nature and feelings unrestrained. Anger and desire follow, and calamities arrive. What could turn this back except a heart of sorrow and remorse? The fifth has the virtue of centeredness and also meets the moment when obscurity reaches its limit and clarity is about to return. Hence this image. The third's sigh is grief after excessive pleasure; the fifth's sigh is self-reproach and self-correction.

### Top Nine: The king uses this to march out in chastisement. A praiseworthy deed: sever the leader's head; do not seize his followers. No blame.[^j04-leader-followers]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4600:055 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4600:056 -->

**Original Meaning.** Firm clarity reaches far. Its authority inspires awe, but punishment is not indiscriminate: the Way to be without blame. Hence this image and prognostication.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4600:056 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4600:057 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** A nine occupies the top with yang, at Li's end: the utmost of firm clarity. Clarity can illuminate; firmness can decide. Illumination suffices to discern wrongdoing; decision suffices to exercise authority and punishment. Thus a king should employ such firm clarity to distinguish the world's wrongdoing and undertake chastising expeditions, achieving praiseworthy merit. A military expedition is punishment on its greatest scale. Yet clarity at its utmost leaves no detail unexamined, and decisiveness at its utmost leaves nothing pardoned. Unless bounded by the mean, these become harmful through severity and scrutiny. In removing the world's evil, if one pursues every gradual contamination and unwitting entanglement, how could punishment ever end? The injury and devastation would be grave. Thus one should sever and take only the ringleader; those seized should not be the whole class of his followers. Then there is no blame for cruelty and violence. The *Documents* says: “Destroy their chief ringleaders; do not prosecute those coerced into following.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4600:057 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4600:058 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** The top nine follows the fourth and fifth and has the image of renewed clarity. In the human heart, this means overcoming oneself and rooting out the whole source; in the state, removing disorder and eliminating its chief evil. The *Odes* says: “Like a raging fire, none dares stop me. Though the root-stock has three new shoots, let them neither grow nor spread.” This is the line's meaning.[^j04-root-shoots]

## Translator's Endnotes

**Textual basis.** **B** is the repository's [archived 易學網 text][base04], which remains unchanged. **S** is the [parallel 四庫全書 transcription, fixed Wikisource revision 759588][siku04], consulted on 25 September 2026. References below name the hexagram, line, and commentator so that a reading can be located in both witnesses. These notes distinguish attested alternative readings, contextual interpretations, and conjectural corrections. Comparison was selective and between transcriptions, not a systematic collation of historical printed leaves. The English has not yet received independent bilingual review. “Editorial Judgment” in the translation always belongs to the imperial compilers; the notes in this section alone are the translator's.

[^j04-bo]: **Splitting, stripping, and falling away.** 剝 encompasses peeling or stripping away, with the resulting collapse and loss of support. “Splitting Apart” serves as the title, while the verbs vary with the object: a bed is split, a roof stripped away, and yang diminished. The commentators' moral identifications of yang with noble people and yin with petty people belong to their interpretive argument; they are not a claim here that every yin line, every yielding action, or women as a class are evil. The fifth line itself offers a constructive role for the many yin. See B and S, Bo, Judgment and Six in the Fifth Place.

[^j04-months]: **The months are not Gregorian months.** The ninth month here belongs to the traditional calendrical correspondence between seasonal change and hexagrams. Bo has five yin and one yang; Kun follows as the tenth-month figure, and Fu as the eleventh-month figure. “Ninth month” must therefore not be silently converted to September. Cheng and Zhu also distinguish the visible completion of a line from the continuity or initial stirring of vital energy: a diagram with no yang strokes does not, for them, imply the annihilation of yang. See Bo's Top Nine and Fu's opening commentaries.

[^j04-bed-punctuation]: **Two readings of 蔑貞凶.** Zhu and Cheng take 蔑貞 as destroying steadfast rightness, followed by “misfortune.” Yu Yan attaches 蔑, destruction, to the preceding description of the bed, then reads 貞凶 as a warning that persistence brings misfortune. The compilers explicitly preserve Yu's alternative. Thus one reading diagnoses the destruction of the right order; the other warns against rigidly holding to one's course while circumstances deteriorate. The line heading follows the first, while Yu's commentary is translated according to his own punctuation. The same issue recurs in the second line; no character is changed. B and S agree on the underlying text.

[^j04-bed-frame]: **辨 and the bed.** Both Zhu and Cheng gloss 辨 as 牀幹, the bed's supporting frame; Cheng adds that it divides upper from lower. “Frame” preserves that structural function without claiming a precisely identified archaeological fitting. The progression is from legs, to frame, to the body's skin: damage to support becomes direct harm to the person. The translation does not turn the bed into a purely abstract “foundation,” because the later roof image depends upon its concrete relation to shelter and support.

[^j04-parting]: **An ambiguous pronoun and verb.** “Parting from them” renders 剝之 through the compilers' account of Zhu's meaning: stripping away attachment to one's own party and following the remaining yang. Wang Bi and Cheng instead connect the whole phrase as freedom from blame while situated in Splitting Apart. The English does not make their explanations endorse a grammatical reading they do not share. Nor is 無咎, “no blame,” upgraded to 吉, “good fortune”: Cheng expressly refuses that stronger promise. See B and S, Bo, Six in the Third Place, especially the Editorial Judgment.

[^j04-reversed-lines]: **Bo's third and Fu's fourth.** Turn Bo's six-line figure upside down and it becomes Fu; the third position thereby becomes the fourth. Hu Bingwen is comparing this reversal, not saying that changing Bo's third line produces Fu. The distinction matters throughout the commentary: reversing a figure, exchanging the places of lines in a theory of hexagram transformation, and changing a line obtained in a consultation are different operations.

[^j04-palace-women]: **Fish and palace favor.** 貫魚 is fish threaded in sequence, a concrete image of the many yin placed in order. 宮人 includes wives, concubines, and attendants of the palace in Cheng's explanation. The line's image is their ordered access to favor under the yang above, not a modern prescription about courtship. “Favor through the palace women” keeps the compressed oracular wording; the commentaries expand it into favor received as palace women receive it. Their political and gender hierarchy is retained rather than replaced with contemporary egalitarian language. B and S, Bo, Six in the Fifth Place.

[^j04-fruit]: **Kernel, carriage, and roof.** The uneaten fruit preserves the possibility of further life. Hu Bingwen and Qiao Zhonghe call its kernel 仁, the same graph used for humaneness; the English must use “kernel” in the botanical image without losing sight of that ethical resonance. The carriage figures others bearing up the noble person. The roof is the shelter the petty person destroys along with the order supporting him. These are different outcomes attached to different conduct, not a single undifferentiated prediction for anyone receiving the line.

[^j04-yang-renewal]: **Continuity beneath the diagram.** Cheng acknowledges that the top yang can change into pure Kun, yet denies that generative yang is thereby extinguished. He also explicitly says that yin has the same continuity, while explaining why the moral rhetoric emphasizes yang's survival. This is his cosmological interpretation, not a modern physical claim. The two titles 匪風 and 下泉 are separate poems, translated “Not the Wind” and “The Downward Spring.” Cheng invokes their placement at the end of the altered Airs as an expression of the longing for restored order. The punctuation in B is clarified accordingly; S has the two titles in continuous text.

[^j04-seven-days]: **Seven days and the return-cycle.** The oracle says 七日, seven days, and that wording is retained. Zhu relates the number to the progression from the first yin of Coming to Meet, assigned to the fifth month, to the returning yang of Fu in the eleventh; Cheng calls them seven successive changes. Neither explanation licenses silently replacing “days” with “months” in the oracle. Zhu also treats seven days as the period indicated in a consultation, while his recorded conversation stresses the numerical meaning of seven. These distinct explanations are preserved rather than converted into a universally reliable forecasting rule.

[^j04-three-yang]: **Zi, chou, and yin.** 子、丑、寅 are consecutive earthly branches used here for the months of increasing yang: the eleventh, twelfth, and first months in the scheme employed by the commentary. Cheng's point is that a single returning yang needs companions before it can accomplish the work of generation. This is a calendrical and structural analogy, not a reference to personal zodiac characteristics. The sequence is also the basis of the contrast between Fu's coming companions and Tai's advancing group.

[^j04-fu-readings]: **Corrections in Fu.** Three apparent transcription slips in B are translated with the readings attested in S: 大善而占之道 becomes 大善而吉之道, “the Way of great goodness and good fortune,” in Zhu on First Nine; 復之休美，言之道也 becomes 復之休美吉之道也, “the beauty of return, the Way to good fortune,” in Zhu on Six in the Second Place; and 君於之道 becomes 君子之道, “the noble person's Way,” in Li Jian on Six in the Fifth Place. Fang Qiao's opening gloss also requires a punctuation clarification: 無疾害之者 means there is nothing to injure or harm, not a new sentence beginning “those who harm.” The continuous wording in S supports this parsing, without supplying a changed character. [Compare S.][siku04]

[^j04-sequence-slip]: **A sequence reversed in both witnesses.** B and S both read 夬次姤 in Master Shao's gloss. Taken in the same syntax as 復次剝, this would make Breakthrough follow Coming to Meet, contrary both to the received sequence and to his own concluding 未有夬而不姤, “there has never been Breakthrough without Coming to Meet.” The English therefore says “After Breakthrough comes Coming to Meet.” This is a contextual correction of the relation, not a reading recovered from the parallel witness. The transmitted wording remains unchanged in the Chinese source.

[^j04-early-return]: **Return before the fault becomes manifest.** “Before going far” retains the spatial image but is interpreted as correcting a lapse at its inception. Yan Hui is the figure called Master Yan and Hui in the surrounding comments. Cheng carefully distinguishes this immediate recognition and correction from a sage's effortless freedom from transgression. The passage therefore concerns the timing and quality of correction, not a claim that the exemplary person never has an incipient error. The favorable “supreme” in 元吉 retains the commentators' association of greatness with goodness.

[^j04-zhi]: **祗, 祇, and 抵.** Cheng explains the disputed graph through 抵, “reach,” and compares the same expression in Kan's fifth line. His final philological sentence also discusses a form under the clothing radical and Lu Deming's different pronunciation. The transcriptions themselves do not consistently distinguish the visually close graphs. The translation preserves the reported disagreement rather than silently presenting a standardized graph as universally agreed. “No reaching the point of regret” follows the shared contextual explanation. The romanizations are explanatory approximations of the comparison, not a reconstruction of medieval pronunciation.

[^j04-beautiful-return]: **休 is not inactivity.** The commentators explain 休復 through 休美, a good or beautiful return, and through the second line's willingness to humble itself before the humane first yang. “Beautiful return” therefore does not mean taking a rest from the work of returning. Cheng's language also recalls the ethical relation between returning to ritual propriety and humaneness. Zhu's explanation stresses the practical assistance afforded by proximity to someone humane. B and S, Fu, Six in the Second Place.

[^j04-alone]: **Correct action without a promise of success.** The fourth is the middle of the five yin, with two above and two below, and alone corresponds to the first yang. Zhu says that rightness, not calculation of fortune, is decisive. Cheng likewise praises the intention but argues that weak support prevents accomplishment; he even explains why “no blame” is absent. Their positions should not be flattened into “doing the right thing guarantees success.” The saying attributed to Master Dong is translated as it appears in this compilation; its ethical force depends upon distinguishing 義 and 道 from 利 and 功.

[^j04-inward-reading]: **An explicitly inward interpretation.** Miao Changqi reads 中 as inward centeredness and 獨 through the *Doctrine of the Mean's* vigilance in what one alone knows. This is an alternative to Kong Yingda's positional explanation of “the midst,” not a correction of it. Miao's “first thought” is an analogy for the first yang to which all five yin return. The passage is not expanded into a meditation technique or an account of altered consciousness, neither of which is supplied by the source.

[^j04-dun]: **敦 as depth, substance, and constancy.** “Wholehearted return” renders 敦復 in its ethical setting. The same 敦 appears in 敦臨 and 敦艮, which the commentary compares as forms of accumulated depth or firmness at a figure's end. Here the fifth rather than the sixth is the culmination, because the top fails to return. Thus “wholehearted” should be heard as established, substantial constancy, not simply strong feeling. The English varies the modifier with the governing action rather than forcing one unnatural equivalent into every compound.

[^j04-lost-return]: **Calamity, fault, and the ruler within.** Cheng distinguishes 災, calamity arriving from outside, from 眚, fault arising from one's own action. Zhu reads 以 as 及, extending the military defeat to the state's ruler; Cheng also applies the image to governing a state. Yang Qixin and He Kai explicitly internalize the ruler as the heart's governing faculty, 天君. Both the public and inward readings are preserved. “Ten years” is retained in the oracle, while Cheng interprets ten as a completed cycle and thus lasting incapacity; it is not converted into a guaranteed calendar of events.

[^j04-wuwang]: **Why “Without Falsity.”** 妄 includes the false, arbitrary, reckless, and self-contrived, not merely a conscious lie. The commentators connect 无妄 with sincere action according to genuine principle and also with events not produced by one's wrongdoing. “Innocence” alone would obscure the argument about contrivance; “unexpected” alone would obscure the ethical dimension. Zhu explicitly records 无望, “without expectation,” as another written form in the *Records of the Historian*. The English follows his discussion without treating 妄 and 望 as identical in every occurrence.

[^j04-wuwang-transformation]: **Contention becoming Without Falsity.** Zhu's account exchanges the yang of Contention's second place with the yin of its first. The resulting lower trigram is Zhen, governed by the first yang, while Qian remains above. This is a theoretical account of the figure's derivation, not an instruction that a consultation changing one line of Contention necessarily produces Without Falsity. The same distinction applies to the transformation account in Great Restraint.

[^j04-wuwang-readings]: **Corrections in Without Falsity.** In He Kai's First Nine gloss, B writes the surname-and-name attribution as 何氏揩; S has 何氏楷, followed here. In Cheng on Six in the Second Place, B has 國事之當然, which would introduce “state affairs”; S reads 因事之當然, following what the affair requires, consistent with the surrounding explanation. In Hu Bingwen's gloss on that line, B's 利有牧往 is corrected to S's 利有攸往, “it is beneficial to have somewhere to go.” Qiu Fuguo's Judgment gloss is also repunctuated to keep 妄欲徼福 together, “vainly seek blessing.” This last change is punctuation, not an attested substitution of characters. [Compare S.][siku04]

[^j04-plowing]: **A genuine interpretive dispute.** The compressed 不耕穫，不菑畬 permits very different expansions. The line heading follows He Kai and the compilers: do the work without prematurely claiming its gain. Cheng's explanation initially gives harvesting without initiating plowing; Zhu stresses the absence of contriving and expectation throughout. Chen Zhi explicitly finds tensions within Cheng's several explanations, and that criticism is retained. 菑 is newly broken ground and 畬 the field in its third year in Cheng's gloss; “seasoned field” distinguishes the latter from freshly opened land. The compilers expressly deny that the passage necessarily authorizes abandoning cultivation.

[^j04-undeserved]: **Calamity without wrongdoing.** Zhu says all six lines are without falsity and explains the ox as taken by a passerby, leaving residents to suffer investigation. Cheng instead treats the third as false and the story as illustrating the loss accompanying wrongful gain. These are substantive differences, not synonymous paraphrases. The line heading follows the undeserved-calamity reading, while Cheng's explanation is translated on its own terms. No conclusion that a sufferer's misfortune proves wrongdoing should be imposed on Zhu's reading; the contrast with 匪正有眚 is made explicit by Hu Bingwen.

[^j04-may-be-steadfast]: **可貞 is not the same as 利貞.** Cheng distinguishes permission to maintain one's present course from a general statement that steadfast correctness is beneficial. Hu Bingwen further separates the senses “correct” and “firm”: some phrases emphasize correctness, while prohibitions of 貞 can warn against rigid persistence. The fourth's apparently incorrect line-place pairing does not settle the matter, because excessive firmness would itself become falsity. Ethical rightness and formal positional correctness are related but not interchangeable.

[^j04-no-medicine]: **A historical oracle, not treatment guidance.** 勿藥 is literally a prohibition against medicating, and the translation does not soften it out of the text. Zhu describes an illness that resolves of itself; Cheng chiefly develops an analogy in which external opposition does not justify altering an already correct Way; the compilers compare needless intervention in illness and in an undeserved calamity. These are premodern interpretations and assumptions. This passage is not a recommendation to refuse, stop, or delay medical treatment, nor can a consultation establish that an actual illness needs no treatment.

[^j04-opposition]: **The examples of opposition.** Cheng names Shun's encounter with the Miao, the Duke of Zhou's difficulties with Guan and Cai, and Confucius's with Shusun Wushu as examples of something afflicting a person without requiring that person's own rightness to be “treated.” They are historical or traditional exempla as deployed by the commentator, not clinical analogies established by evidence. The translation retains the names and the argumentative direction without supplying invented dialogue or dates.

[^j04-daxu]: **Restraint, storage, and cultivation.** 畜 is deliberately multivalent: to stop or restrain, to accumulate or store, and to sustain or cultivate. Cheng begins by explaining how stopping permits accumulation, and the later discussion turns stored virtue toward public service. “Great Restraint” is the title, but “great store,” “accumulation,” and “nurture” are needed where the argument changes register. The same word's range is already important in Small Restraint; the present chapter does not reduce the two hexagrams to a merely quantitative difference in obstruction.

[^j04-eating-at-home]: **Not eating at home.** Zhu explicitly glosses 不家食 as receiving a stipend at court rather than living privately at home. Cheng extends its good fortune beyond personal employment to the benefit that cultivated virtue brings the world. The image is therefore not about dining arrangements or dietary practice. The translated oracle keeps the concrete phrase so that the commentators' movement from food and maintenance to office and responsibility remains visible.

[^j04-daxu-transformation]: **Waiting becoming Great Restraint.** In Zhu's derivation, Waiting's yang in the fifth position exchanges places with the yin above it, producing Gen above Qian. The fifth six can then esteem the worthy yang above itself. This positional account underlies the later interpretations of a ruler honoring an adviser, but it must not make every top line a ruler or every fifth line a teacher. Roles are assigned according to the particular argument.

[^j04-zhu-revision]: **Zhu's uncompleted editorial intention.** The recorded conversation states how Zhu wanted to organize his explanation: King Wen's oracle first, Confucius's reasons afterward. It ends by saying that he had not yet had time to put the work in order. This is a report of an intention, not evidence that the text before us already incorporates a completed revision. It also reinforces the distinction between an oracle statement, the explanation attached to it, and a later comment on the adequacy of that explanation. B and S both preserve this statement.

[^j04-restraint-correspondence]: **Correspondence can restrain rather than assist.** The first and fourth, and the second and fifth, occupy corresponding positions. Cheng expressly says that the relation here signifies restraint, whereas elsewhere it may signify assistance. The third and top, although both yang and therefore not an ordinary yin–yang pair, unite in an intention to advance. The translation preserves “respond” where used in this broader sense and does not silently impose one mechanical rule on every occurrence of 應.

[^j04-axle]: **The removed fitting is not a broken wheel.** 輹 denotes a fastening or supporting fitting beneath the carriage body associated with the axle. “Axle-binding” distinguishes it from 輻, the wheel's spokes, debated in Small Restraint's third line. Here the point is that the carriage can deliberately be made unable to travel: the second line stops itself. The source does not require a catastrophic breakage. The exact construction is not settled by the general gloss alone, so the English does not add an elaborate reconstruction of ancient vehicle mechanics.

[^j04-horses-practice]: **A correction already made by the commentator.** B's oracle writes 曰閑輿衛. Zhu explicitly says 曰 should be 日, “day,” and Cheng explains daily practice with carriage and defense. The English follows that authorial emendation; it is not presented as a newly discovered typo. S likewise preserves both the transmitted 曰 and Zhu's correction. 閑 here is training or practiced familiarity, not leisure. The favorable permission to advance is qualified by careful preparation and steadfastness through difficulty, not by an assurance that speed alone succeeds.

[^j04-animal-referents]: **Why Xiang names animals under different lines.** Xiang Anshi calls the first nine the young ox and the second nine the castrated boar, although the corresponding animal statements occur under the fourth and fifth. He is identifying what those upper lines restrain: first is restrained by fourth, second by fifth. This is not corrected as mistaken numbering. The third, pure vigor no longer restrained at the limit, is the fine horse of its own statement.

[^j04-horn-bar]: **牿 and prevention before force is needed.** Zhu describes a wooden crossbar attached at the horns to prevent butting and emphasizes intervention before the horns develop; Cheng also speaks of the calf whose horns are just emerging. The difference in phrasing is retained. “Horn-bar” keeps the device visible without replacing it with a modern behavioral metaphor. The analogy is preventive restraint of an incipient tendency, in contrast to the more difficult fifth-line situation where force has already developed.

[^j04-boar]: **Castrated, not toothless.** 豶 refers to castration; the boar's tusks remain. Cheng's whole explanation depends on removing the source of aggressive force rather than attacking the tusks directly. His political application is to address the conditions and education underlying wrongdoing, not to prescribe the literal animal procedure for people. The premodern claim about the animal's disposition is transmitted as the commentator's premise, not offered as independently verified veterinary guidance.

[^j04-heaven-road]: **Three treatments of 何.** Zhu takes 何天之衢 as an exclamation at openness; Cheng reports that Master Hu regarded 何 as an erroneous addition; Zhang Jun, Wang Zongchuan, and Wu Cheng connect it with 荷, bearing or receiving. The line heading follows Zhu, while each commentary receives its own corresponding English. Wang emphasizes bearing responsibility and opening the path of the worthy; the compilers especially favor the sense of receiving an opportune opening. These alternatives change the grammar and cannot all be hidden under a single smooth rendering. The quoted 龍 in Wu's comparison is favor or beneficence in context, not a literal dragon.

[^j04-storing-virtue]: **The opening is also intellectual and ethical.** Hu Bingwen explicitly extends the oracle beyond holding office to the cumulative work of learning, culminating in comprehensive understanding. His allusion to the *Great Learning's Commentary on the Chapters and Sentences* is left within his own explanation. It is not a promise of instant illumination without prior effort. Likewise, Cai Qing's “withdrawal” and “expansion” translate the paired 屈 and 伸 as the commentary's image of preparation and eventual expression, not as a literal bodily exercise.

[^j04-nourishment]: **Jaws and nurture.** 頤 names the jaw region and, by its relation to eating, nourishment. The figure has solid upper and lower strokes enclosing four divided strokes, with the upper trigram still and the lower moving. Cheng deliberately extends the bodily image to sustaining life, cultivating virtue, supporting other people, and the world's nurture. “Nourishment” is therefore broader than food, but the concrete mouth and jaw imagery must remain available to the reader.

[^j04-observe-nourishment]: **What is observed, and who seeks?** Zhu distinguishes the Way of nourishing from the means of feeding the body; his conversation focuses on virtue and body, not yet on nourishing others. Cheng includes what one nourishes and how one seeks sustenance. Lu Quan, favored by the compilers, emphasizes seeking for oneself the means appropriate to whatever is being nourished, whether virtue or body. The oracle's compact “observe nourishment; seek for yourself what fills the mouth” is kept open enough for these explanations, rather than converted into a single instruction about self-sufficiency.

[^j04-nourishment-allusions]: **Food accepted or refused.** Chen Chen's 嘑蹴 evokes food offered with a contemptuous shout or kick; 素餐 evokes consuming a stipend without fulfilling its work. Both are part of the ethical argument about how sustenance is obtained, not praise of deprivation itself. His contrast between the sages' learning and emptiness, nothingness, techniques, and calculations is retained as his judgment; 術數 is not silently expanded into a condemnation of every divinatory practice. The compilation itself contains differing uses and evaluations of such methods.

[^j04-tortoise]: **The numinous tortoise.** 靈龜 is the tortoise credited here with sustaining itself without ordinary eating; Cheng adds swallowing or drawing in the breath. This is a traditional image of inward sufficiency and numinous intelligence, not an established zoological description or an instruction to undertake fasting. “Your” marks something the first line already possesses and abandons. The contrast with the desirous jaws is therefore a loss of self-possession, not simply a comparison between two animals.

[^j04-jaws-speaker]: **Who is “me”?** Cheng regards the speech as a hypothetical voice directed at the first line: the fourth occasions its desire but need not itself speak. Xiang Anshi's supplementary gloss instead identifies “me” and the upper jaws with the top nine, the source of nourishment. The compilers preserve that reading as an alternative. “Supplement” labels material already present in the Chinese compilation, not an addition invented by the translator. The different assignments must remain visible because they alter the network of relations among the lines.

[^j04-inverted-nourishment]: **顛, 拂, and the hill.** 顛 reverses the ordinary direction of nourishment; 拂 departs from its constant rule. Zhu and Cheng separate the downward attempt to seek nourishment from the first and the upward attempt to seek it from the top, attaching the explicit misfortune especially to the latter. Huang Gan reads “inverted nourishment” as one clause and “departing from the rule, seeking nourishment on the hill” as another, with the prognostication governing the whole. The compilers favor Huang and Xiang and note that Zhu's Image commentary also broadens the warning. The hill remains the concrete image of the elevated top line, not an unexplained geographical destination.

[^j04-yi-hierarchy]: **The source's dependent roles.** Cheng's statements about women following men and yin following yang are rendered directly as part of his account of nourishment from above. The translation neither endorses them as universal social truths nor erases them in favor of modern role-neutral abstractions. Importantly, the same discussion later allows a ruler to depend on a worthy minister: the symbolic hierarchy is not used in only one direction. The reader should distinguish the source's historically situated assumptions from its analysis of a particular line's relationship.

[^j04-departing-nourishment]: **Persistence or correctness despite the situation?** The line heading takes 貞凶 as persistence bringing misfortune. Zhu explicitly says that, once nourishment has been violated, even correctness does not avert misfortune; his explanation is therefore not made to repeat the heading's grammar. Cheng stresses deviation from the correct Way and the prohibition of use. Ten years remains literal in the oracle, while Cheng takes ten as completion and therefore as a comprehensive prohibition. Neither interpretation is turned into a practical ten-year timetable.

[^j04-tiger]: **Unwavering and continuous seeking.** The fourth's tiger is not condemned as simple greed. Zhu reads its gaze as downward and concentrated, its desire as continuously renewed seeking; the object is the assistance needed to nourish others. Cheng also emphasizes authority, while Wu Cheng contrasts the tiger's outward seeking with the tortoise's inward sufficiency. The image's force is retained without making the same kind of seeking automatically good in the first line. Its evaluation depends upon the purpose and position of the seeker.

[^j04-su-nourishing]: **Su Shi's compressed expression.** B and S both have 四之得養初九. Read in the surrounding discussion, this is taken here as the fourth obtaining nourishment from the first nine, contrasted with the first becoming dependent upon the fourth. The English supplies the relational “of/from” needed for that reading, without pretending that a missing Chinese preposition has been recovered from another witness. Because the unpunctuated construction is compressed, it remains a point for fuller bilingual collation.

[^j04-dependent-ruler]: **Dependence can be right without conferring every capacity.** The fifth's reliance on a worthy person is a departure from the usual direction of nourishment but can still be the correct course for its situation. The prohibition on crossing the great river marks a further distinction: maintaining good dependence in ordinary circumstances is not automatically the ability to manage a crisis. The top line can receive the opposite instruction because it represents the responsible adviser rather than the dependent ruler.

[^j04-cheng-trust]: **King Cheng and the Duke of Zhou.** The example illustrates Cheng Yi's concern that a yielding ruler may fail to maintain trust during a crisis. The quotation about not yet daring to rebuke the Duke is preserved as Cheng presents it, together with his reference to “the two dukes.” Their identities are not supplied where the compilation leaves them implicit. This is a historical exemplum within the interpretation, not a separately reconstructed narrative. The wording does not make King Cheng the author of the commentary: “Cheng's Commentary” throughout means Cheng Yi.

[^j04-source-nourishment]: **A source without the ruler's position.** 由頤 means that nourishment proceeds through this line. The fifth depends on the top to nourish others; the top can therefore carry a public responsibility without occupying the fifth's ruler-position. “Danger; good fortune” retains the warning that heavy responsibility requires apprehension. It does not promise that office or influence is intrinsically safe. The comparison with 由豫, the source of enthusiasm, concerns this governing function.

[^j04-yi-readings]: **Two consequential corrections in Nourishment's Top Nine.** B's 以養入 in Zhu's explanation is translated with S's 以養人, “to nourish others.” More importantly, B's 苟之濟天下艱危 in Cheng's rhetorical question is translated with S's 苟不濟天下艱危: “if one did not relieve the world's difficulties and dangers.” The missing negative reverses the condition on which the minister would fail to merit the ruler's trust. Both corrections are documented here rather than made silently in the Chinese source. [Compare S.][siku04]

[^j04-great-excess]: **Exceeding the ordinary, or exceeding principle?** 大過 can mean the great or yang exceeding, a great excess, and extraordinary action surpassing what is ordinarily seen. Cheng insists that the sage never exceeds correct principle, though corrective action can exceed an ordinary measure. Zhu instead insists that a time of excess can properly require an act that is itself excessive relative to ordinary circumstances. “Great Excess” preserves the difficult term rather than replacing it with either “great virtue” or “great error,” both of which would prejudge this dispute.

[^j04-corrective-excess]: **Punctuation and the source's own variant note.** In Cheng's opening discussion, B punctuates 矯時之用小，過於中者. The English connects 小 with 過: corrective action may go a little beyond the mean. S's continuous text permits this reading. B itself adds the note that 時, “time,” is alternatively written 失, “error”; that note is translated as its own source block, explicitly labeled “Base-edition note.” The main translation retains 時 rather than adopting the alternative without notice.

[^j04-ridgepole]: **The central roof-beam.** 棟 is the principal beam along the roof's ridge, not an upright pillar. The figure's four central yang supply weight or strength while the yin ends are weak; its potential failure is therefore not explained simply as a weak center. The third and fourth later receive contrasting beam-images according to their positions and use of firmness. Keeping the building image concrete preserves the compilers' comparison between a heavy beam above and the slight grass that cushions below.

[^j04-opposite-figures]: **Pairs by exchanged line-values.** Nourishment and Great Excess are complementary when every yin and yang value is exchanged; Inner Sincerity and Small Excess form another such pair. This is not the upside-down reversal used earlier for Bo and Fu. Hu Yigui's question concerns why these names do not simply follow the number of yin or yang strokes. His answer emphasizes where the four strokes occur—inside or outside—and the interpretation's preference for yang within. No casting procedure is being prescribed.

[^j04-white-grass]: **Care in placing an object.** 藉用白茅 means cushioning or setting something upon clean white grass. The object is not specified in the oracle, and the English does not invent one. The repeated explanation stresses an excess of care: setting it on the ground would already suffice. “Cogongrass” is a conventional rendering of 茅 here, not a claim that the passage establishes a uniquely identifiable modern botanical specimen. The ritual cleanliness and the contrast between slight material and weighty use carry the argument.

[^j04-poplar-shoots]: **Renewal below.** 楊 is conventionally rendered “poplar” here; the text's concern is a moisture-loving tree, not a botanical diagnosis. 稊 is explained as root or renewed growth at the root, and 荑 is cited as an equivalent written form. “Shoots at the root” lets the reader understand why renewed life below differs from flowers above in the fifth line. The old man and young wife are the commentary's generative analogy; the translation preserves its historical assumptions without turning them into contemporary advice about age-disparate marriage.

[^j04-daguo-readings]: **Corrections and retained wording in Great Excess.** In Hu Bingwen on Nine in the Second Place, B's 枯陽, “withered yang,” is translated with S's 枯楊, “withered poplar.” In Cheng on Nine in the Fourth Place, B's 加棟之隆起 is translated with S's 如棟之隆起, “like a ridgepole rising.” In Cheng on Nine in the Third Place, both witnesses read 常常之功; the English takes the phrase contextually as “an ordinary achievement” rather than claiming an attested replacement by 尋常. B's 遠於中和 and S's 違於中和 differ as being distant from or contrary to centered harmony; “departs from centered harmony” preserves the argument without treating this intelligible variation as an obvious typo. [Compare S.][siku04]

[^j04-other-attachment]: **有它 and a balance already achieved.** “Another attachment” is the further intention toward the first yin, according to Zhu and Cheng. The fourth has already moderated firmness by occupying a yielding position; adding a binding relation to yin can therefore overcorrect it. This differs from the second's helpful proximity to the first. The commentary's distinctions are relational and situational, not a blanket rule that attachment, correspondence, or yieldingness is always harmful.

[^j04-poplar-flowers]: **Growth is not always renewal.** The fifth's flowers look like life but, in the commentators' account, expend an already weakened tree rather than restoring its root. The old woman and husband in his prime form the contrasting human image. “No blame, no praise” remains a deliberately limited assessment and is not converted into either a moral denunciation of such a couple or an auspicious promise. Cheng's further evaluation of the image is preserved as his own, including the hierarchy of value he assigns to generative capacity.

[^j04-overhead]: **Misfortune and three meanings of no blame.** Zhu reads the final line as failure of physical capacity without failure of rightness, associating it with giving up life to fulfill humaneness. Cheng reads reckless self-caused disaster: there is no one else to blame. The compilers prefer Zhu to Cheng, but preserve a third interpretation—accommodation without further inflaming an impossible situation. The translation does not harmonize these incompatible judgments. “No blame” is retained in the oracle so that the reader can see how differently the same words are explained. These historical ethical readings do not constitute advice to endanger oneself.

[^j04-late-han]: **東京 and the historical examples.** 東京 here refers to the Eastern Han setting, not modern Tokyo. The compilers contrast the conduct of “Fan and Li” with Shentu Pan and Guo Tai to argue for restrained accommodation in an impossible time. The English preserves that attribution and the abbreviated names as given, rather than presenting the editors' judgment as an independent historical verdict. The alternative is offered alongside, not as an erasure of, Zhu's account of blameless sacrifice. B and S, Great Excess, Top Six, Editorial Judgment.

[^j04-repeated-danger]: **習 is repetition and practice.** The title “Repeated Danger” follows Zhu and Cheng's doubled-pit reading, but the collected commentators also hear familiarity acquired through repeated experience. Su Shi even distinguishes the dangerous channel, Kan, from the water that moves through it. These are not interchangeable glosses, so “practice,” “repetition,” and “dangerous terrain” appear where each is argued. The introductory discussion also distinguishes being trained to meet danger from valuing danger itself as a virtue.

[^j04-trigram-structure]: **One distinctive line within three.** Cheng's description of yang above, in the middle, or below refers respectively to the single yang in Gen, Kan, and Zhen; the corresponding single yin gives Dui, Li, and Xun. It does not identify every yang in the top position of any six-line figure with Gen. His statement that “the one begins in the middle” belongs to the correlative account of water and first generation. It is retained as such, without expanding it into a modern physical explanation or an esoteric practice not stated here.

[^j04-heart-passage]: **心亨 is not merely a favorable event.** Hu Bingwen explicitly distinguishes success in affairs from the heart's free passage. 維 gives emphasis or connection in the phrase, while Cheng explains sincerity and singleness as what allows this passage. The English does not make “heart” merely emotion, nor does it replace the term with a modern technical psychology. 尚 is esteem or worthiness of esteem; Cheng explains it through accomplishment in emerging from danger. The statement therefore joins inward coherence and action rather than recommending indefinite inward retreat.

[^j04-kan-readings]: **Corrections and counter-checks in Kan.** B's 維心享 in Cheng's Judgment explanation is translated with S's 維心亨. B attributes an introductory gloss to 鄧氏汝諧; S has 鄭氏汝諧, Zheng Ruxie, followed here. In Wang Shenzi on Six in the Third Place, B's 息而來安 is translated with S's 息而未安, resting without yet being secure. In Guo Yong on Six in the Fourth Place, B's 筐莒 is read with S's 筐筥, square and round baskets. S is not followed indiscriminately: its 王氏中子 at the third line is not adopted over B's 王氏申子, and its 水流而下盈 in He Kai's fifth-line gloss is not adopted over B's coherent 水流而不盈. [Compare S.][siku04]

[^j04-deeper-pit]: **The pit within the pit.** 窞 is a deeper hollow inside a pit, not simply a second independent danger. Zhang Jun reads 習 in the first line negatively, as habituation to wrongdoing, and the editors use that interpretation to give the repeated word a specific function. This is distinct from the constructive practice discussed in the Judgment. The reference to scheming deepening disaster is the editors' application, not a reason to assume that everyone facing danger caused it through wrongdoing.

[^j04-small-attainment]: **A source before a river.** Yang Shi and Chen Renxi take 求 as self-directed work rather than a request to another person. Their trickling spring is already flowing water before it becomes a river. The compilers accordingly explain the small attainment as a beginning in learning and action, not merely a minor concession from an opponent. At the close of their judgment B reads 為行險之本, “the foundation for making one's way through danger”; S has 乃行險之術, “the method for making one's way through danger.” The English retains B's intelligible reading rather than substituting the parallel phrase.

[^j04-uneasy-rest]: **Coming, going, and resting.** 來之 is read as two movements, coming and going, not as one verb with a pronoun. 枕 literally evokes resting one's head or leaning on a support. Zhu emphasizes danger before and behind; Cheng adds that staying is also insecure; Wang Shenzi takes temporary rest as a warning against going deeper, even though safety has not yet been reached. The line heading retains the uneasy-rest image without eliminating this dispute. The correction 未安 in Wang's gloss is attested in S and recorded in the Kan textual note.

[^j04-wine-vessels]: **A disputed division of the offering.** Zhu, following Chao, divides 樽酒簋 / 貳用缶 and explains 貳 as adding or replenishing. Cheng and Wang Bi read 簋貳 as two gui vessels. He Kai takes 貳 as an accompaniment: the gui goes with the wine, and the compilers prefer his explanation. The heading follows the punctuation preserved in the selected base and Zhu's gloss; the commentaries retain their own constructions. A gui is a food-offering vessel; 缶 is earthenware, not necessarily the identical object named 樽. The restrained transliteration avoids pretending that the disputed vessels and quantities have one universally agreed arrangement.

[^j04-window]: **An indirect entrance for a bond or an offering.** 納約 is read by Zhu and Cheng as advancing into a binding connection, while Wang Bi also hears bringing in a most modest offering. 牖 remains a window, contrasted with the proper entrance but important as the source of light in a room. Cheng develops the analogy of entering through what another person already understands, and extends it from remonstrance to teaching. The English retains both the physical image and the differing explanations instead of replacing the oracle with an abstract maxim about communication.

[^j04-remonstrance]: **The historical examples and a name variant.** Cheng first discusses the Han founder's proposed change of heir, contrasting ministerial opposition with his regard for the Four Elders. He then turns to the queen dowager of Zhao and the Lord of Chang'an. B's comma in 趙王，太后 is not retained: 趙王太后 names the queen dowager of Zhao, not two separate actors. The adviser is 觸龍 in B, retained as Chu Long, but 觸讋 in S; the variant is recorded without silently changing the base's name. These are the commentator's examples, not invented dialogue. The four plant names in Guo Yong's following gloss are kept as pin, fan, yun, and zao rather than assigned uncertain modern species.

[^j04-level-water]: **Not full, or not overflowing?** Cheng takes 坎不盈 as the hollow not yet being filled, so escape and freedom from blame remain incomplete. Yu Yan and He Kai instead understand a flow that reaches the proper level without overflowing; the compilers prefer this as the virtue of water. The line heading follows that preferred interpretation. Zhu's separate gloss remains less explicit, while his recorded conversation discusses 祗 as reaching. “Level” preserves the water image as well as the ethical language of measured adequacy; it does not erase the competing account of an unfilled pit.

[^j04-cords-thorns]: **Confinement and its duration.** 徽纆 are binding cords, and 叢棘 clustered thorns, read here as the setting of a prison. The English does not add a precise cord construction absent from the selected commentary. “For three years, no release” follows Cheng's literal confinement reading; Wang and the compilers give “attainment” a further ethical force. Wu Cheng's explanation of prison discipline and release belongs to his reading of the ancient *Offices of Zhou*, not to any present-day legal system.

[^j04-attaining-way]: **The compilers' rehabilitative reading.** They explicitly gloss 不得 as failing to attain the Way. In this reading, regret for wrongdoing and reflection upon faults make eventual release possible. This is not the same as a prediction that confinement simply ends after three years. B's 罪苦, guilt and suffering, is retained in the English; S instead has a net-radical graph in the second position, suggesting the imagery of penal entanglement. No settled critical reading of that graph is claimed. The important point shared by the context is that the instruction does not abandon someone already deeply caught in the consequences of wrongdoing.

[^j04-clinging]: **Attachment and brightness together.** 離 is explained through 麗, attaching or clinging, and through 明, brightness or clarity. “Clinging” is the established title, not a judgment that attachment is inherently unhealthy. Fire depends on something to which it adheres; human attachment includes one's associates, chosen Way, and responsibilities. Where the oracle moves explicitly into light and color, “brightness” is necessary. The translation therefore does not force the English title mechanically into every use of 離.

[^j04-cow]: **The female animal is explicit.** 牝牛 is a female bovine; “cow” conveys the distinction without the redundant “female cow.” The contrast with Qian's horse, Kun's mare, and Kun's ox is retained in Guo Zhongxiao's gloss. Cheng interprets nurturing the cow as cultivating compliant virtue, while Zhu preserves the divinatory image more directly. Neither is replaced by a modern exercise or by a claim that an animal's sex determines a universal human temperament.

[^j04-water-fire]: **Two directions of excess.** Wu Yueshen compares sinking water to desire and upward-flaming fire to anger. The remedy differs accordingly: Kan needs firm centeredness; Li needs centered compliance. Hu Bingwen similarly contrasts inward and outward brightness. These are the commentators' ethical uses of the images, not an instruction to manipulate bodily substances or a physiological diagnosis. The compilers' phrase about governing lofty brightness through yieldingness explains why clarity itself still requires cultivation.

[^j04-footsteps]: **錯然 is not necessarily a mistake.** It can describe crossing, mingling, or a complex array. Zhu and Cheng hear incipient, potentially restless movement; Wang Zhaosu, Feng Dangke, and Hu Yigui emphasize the many affairs encountered at the start of the day. Hu Yuan also connects the expression with watchful reverence. The compilers favor the beginning of responding to a complex world. “Footsteps cross and recross” preserves the image without translating 錯 as “wrong” in advance of the discussion. 敬 is reverent attentiveness, not fear alone.

[^j04-yellow]: **The color of the center.** Yellow is explicitly interpreted as the central color. The second's yieldingness, correct place, and central position in the lower trigram make the image favorable. “Yellow brightness” draws out Li's luminous aspect without losing its attachment to the center; a literal “yellow clinging” would conceal the argument rather than preserve it. Yu Yan's comparison with noon is his inference from the third's declining sun, not an additional sentence in the oracle. The related Yellow Lower Garment in Kun is a cross-reference within the compilation.

[^j04-declining-sun]: **Singing is not evaluated uniformly.** Zhu and Cheng understand singing while beating an everyday pot as contentment with the ordinary course, opposed to lamenting decline. Liang Yin instead regards both the singing and the lament as emotions out of measure. The compilers expressly prefer Liang and interpret the sunset as obscuration of the heart rather than external aging or changing circumstances. The line heading preserves the conditional structure, but the annotations must not make these readings agree. 大耋, great old age, also receives Cheng's comparison with 昳, the sun's decline; the phonetic or graphic equivalence is his claim.

[^j04-li-readings]: **A corrected expression and sound base readings.** In Cheng on Nine in the Third Place, B reads 恐恒; S has 恐怛, fear and distress, which underlies “fearful and grieved” here. The next phrase supplies grief at the approaching end. Conversely, S's 苟氏爽 is not adopted over B's 荀氏爽, Xun Shuang, and S's 拆取 in Cheng's Top Nine gloss is not adopted over B's 折取, consistent with the oracle's 折首. These examples again show why agreement with one parallel transcription is not a substitute for judgment. [Compare S.][siku04]

[^j04-sudden-fire]: **The repeated 如.** 突如、焚如、死如、棄如 create a forceful sequence of arrival, burning, death, and rejection. The English retains that abrupt progression rather than inventing a narrated historical event. Cheng interprets the fourth through an unfit successor and a forceful subordinate pressing upon a yielding ruler. Zhu is more compressed, while Zhang Huang and He Kai return to the danger of unchecked, sudden brightness. No separate 凶 is supplied in the heading; Cheng explicitly explains why the disastrous images make it unnecessary.

[^j04-successive-light]: **Not necessarily hereditary succession.** The two Li trigrams invite the image of light following light. Cheng applies it to succession in rule; the compilers explicitly reject that application here and interpret 繼明 as maintaining luminous virtue without interruption. Their quoted 緝熙 emphasizes continuing brightness. “Successive light” and “renewed clarity” are therefore kept distinct from “hereditary succession.” This is a methodological disagreement about what the figure can support, not merely a different English synonym.

[^j04-tears]: **Apprehension and remorse, not sentiment alone.** Zhu and Cheng explain the tears through the fifth's exposed position between yang forces and its capacity to recognize danger. The compilers additionally read them as the remorse that turns back the third's and fourth's excesses. They distinguish the third's grief after excessive pleasure from the fifth's self-reproach. The favorable judgment therefore does not mean that tears of any kind are inherently auspicious; it belongs to the particular moral and structural account given here.

[^j04-leader-followers]: **Limited punishment within a violent image.** 折首 retains the concrete severity of severing the leader's head. 醜 is explained as 類, the class or company of followers, not physical ugliness. The line heading makes explicit the commentators' distinction between the chief offender and those drawn along with him; its “do not seize” renders that limitation rather than introducing a new moral outside the source. Cheng's quotation exempts coerced followers and rejects indiscriminate pursuit of every contamination or error. “The king uses this” does not require the top line itself to be the king; it can be the firm clarity the king employs.

[^j04-root-shoots]: **The last image completes the moral argument.** The compilers distinguish rooting out the source of one's own wrongdoing from removing the chief cause of disorder in the state. Their final quotation combines a raging fire with a root-stock's renewed shoots. This is not the same evaluative use of renewed growth as Bo's preserved fruit or Great Excess's root-shoots: what grows and what is being removed differ. The severe image is preserved as the editors' closing application, not softened into a general sentiment about renewal.
