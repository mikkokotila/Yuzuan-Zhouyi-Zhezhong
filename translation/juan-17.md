**Discussion of the Trigrams · 說卦傳**[^j17-title]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:002 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:003 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:004 -->

**Kong Yingda:** Confucius takes Fuxi to have drawn the eight trigrams and afterward doubled them to make the sixty-four hexagrams. In the Appended Statements he briefly explains the eight trigrams’ lesser completion and their extension and enlargement. He also says, “The eight trigrams are arrayed; images are within them. By doubling them, lines are within them.” And again: “Observing images in Heaven, patterns on Earth, the markings of birds and beasts, and what is fitting to Earth; taking from the body close at hand and from things farther away, he first made the eight trigrams, to communicate with the virtue of the numinous powers and classify the dispositions of the myriad things.” Yet the meaning of extending and enlarging them, doubling three lines to make six, was still not clear. Nor were the images found by looking upward and examining downward, in the body nearby and in things farther away, yet displayed. Here, therefore, he explains more fully why the trigrams were doubled and which images the eight trigrams represent. Hence its name, Discussion of the Trigrams.

## Chapter 1 · Numbers, Figures, and the Mandate

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:005 -->

### In ancient times, when the sages made the Changes, they aided the numinous powers in their hidden workings and brought forth the yarrow.[^j17-yarrow]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:005 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:006 -->

**Original Meaning.** Aiding the numinous powers in their hidden workings is like saying “assisting transformation and nourishment.” The Account of Turtle and Stalk Divination says: “When the world is peaceful and harmonious and the kingly Way is attained, yarrow stalks grow a zhang tall, a full hundred stems growing together in a clump.”[^j17-guice]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:006 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:007 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:007 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:008 -->

**Kong Yingda:** Through their sagely wisdom they deeply understood the Way of the numinous powers and brought into being the method of using yarrow to obtain hexagrams. Hence “they aided the numinous powers in their hidden workings and brought forth the yarrow.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:008 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:009 -->

**Master Cheng:** “They aided the numinous powers in their hidden workings and brought forth the yarrow” means using yarrow to obtain hexagrams. It does not mean that only after there was yarrow were the figures drawn.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:009 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:010 -->

**Su Shi:** Serving as an intermediary to convey a command is called assisting. Heaven, Earth, ghosts, and spirits cannot enter into direct intercourse with human beings; thus yarrow and the turtle serve as their intermediaries.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:010 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:011 -->

**Xiang Anshi:** “Bringing forth the yarrow” means establishing the method of using it. Spirits cannot speak: the yarrow speaks for them, assisting the spirits in issuing their commands. Hence “aiding the numinous powers in their hidden workings.” This is precisely what the discussion of the Great Expansion calls “assisting the numinous.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:011 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:012 -->

**Gong Huan:** Xiang’s explanation of “bringing forth the yarrow” differs from the Original Meaning. Yet considered alongside the following references to establishing numbers, setting up hexagrams, and bringing forth lines, Xiang’s explanation seems the correct one.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:012 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:013 -->

**Su Jun:** “Bringing forth the yarrow” should be understood by analogy with “bringing forth the lines.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:013 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:014 -->

### Assigning three to Heaven and two to Earth, they established the numbers upon them.[^j17-three-two]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:014 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:015 -->

**Original Meaning.** Heaven is round and Earth square. A circle with a diameter of one has a circumference of three; each of the three is one odd unit. Thus Heaven is tripled to make three. A square with a side of one has a perimeter of four; the four combine into two even pairs. Thus Earth is doubled to make two. All numbers arise upon this basis. Accordingly, at the end of the three changes in counting the stalks, three odd remainders give three threes, or nine; three even remainders give three twos, or six. Two twos and one three make seven; two threes and one two make eight.[^j17-eight-correction]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:015 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:016 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:016 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:017 -->

**Kong Yingda:** Seven and nine are odd, Heaven’s numbers; six and eight are even, Earth’s numbers. Taking the odd from Heaven and the even from Earth, one establishes the numbers seven, eight, nine, and six. Why use “three” and “two” to designate odd and even? In antiquity, odd and even were also spoken of as three and two. Moreover, two begins the even numbers and three begins the odd numbers. Why not designate the odd by one? Master Zhang says that three contains two within it, expressing one’s inclusion of two: this shows Heaven’s virtue of encompassing Earth and yang’s Way of encompassing yin.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:017 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:018 -->

**Lu Zhenqi:** “Established upon” means relying upon. Establishing the numbers follows bringing forth the yarrow and precedes setting up the hexagram. One uses yarrow to obtain numbers, then lays them out as a hexagram. Thus the numbers in question are seven, eight, nine, and six.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:018 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:019 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** To determine Heaven’s three and Earth’s two through the dimensions and perimeters of circle and square gives only a general outline. In fact, a diameter of one yields more than a circumference of three: it is not an exact ratio. In terms of principle, Master Zhang’s explanation that one encompasses two is right. Heaven can include Earth, so one joins two to make three. In terms of calculation, Kong’s explanation that two begins the even numbers and three the odd is right. Multiplying one by one or dividing one by one produces no change; thus the numbers employed in multiplication and division begin with three and two. In terms of images, to make a circle, place three points, find their center, and draw with a compass. To make a square, place two points, turn a right angle, and draw with a carpenter’s square. Taken together, all these concern number. Thus Heaven’s three and Earth’s two are the source of numbers. Their use in divination yields seven, eight, nine, and six. Broadly speaking in terms of principle, the numbers three and two are both taken three times: three threes make nine; three twos make six; one three and two twos make seven; one two and two threes make eight. In terms of calculation, odd numbers begin with one and three and reach completion in nine and seven; even numbers begin with two and four and reach completion in eight and six. Thus their completed numbers mark yin and yang. Yang advancing is old; retreating, young. Yin retreating is old; advancing, young. In terms of images, a circular arrangement surrounds one with six: empty the center and there are six; fill it and there are seven. A square arrangement surrounds one with eight: fill the center and there are nine; empty it and there are eight. Yang is round, yin square; yang is solid, yin empty. Thus seven alone, round and solid, is flourishing yang; eight alone, square and empty, is vigorous yin. Nine, though solid, is arranged as a square: yang is about to change into yin, hence old yang. Six, though empty, is arranged as a circle: yin is about to change into yang, hence old yin. All these numbers come from three and two; thus the text says “established the numbers upon them.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:019 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:020 -->

### Observing change in yin and yang, they established the hexagrams; unfolding the firm and yielding, they brought forth the lines. They harmonized and accorded with the Way and virtue, and ordered things through rightness. They fathomed principle, fully realized nature, and thereby reached the mandate.[^j17-principle-nature]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:020 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:021 -->

**Original Meaning.** Harmonizing and according means moving with ease, without conflict or resistance: this speaks of the whole. Ordering means finding the proper pattern in each affair: this speaks of its distinctions. Fathoming the principles of the world, fully realizing the natures of human beings and other things, and according with Heaven’s Way—this is the sages’ ultimate achievement in making the Changes.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:021 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:022 -->

**Original Meaning — Chapter Summary.** This is Chapter One.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:022 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:023 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:023 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:024 -->

**Han Kangbo:** The hexagrams are images; the yarrow is number. In the hexagrams, “thunder and wind press upon one another, mountains and lakes communicate their qi”: these represent the constitution of yin and yang’s changes and transformations. The yarrow interweaves Heaven and Earth’s numbers of three and two. The yarrow exhausts numbers to determine images; the hexagrams encompass images to exhaust numbers. Thus of the yarrow it says, “assigning three to Heaven and two to Earth, they established the numbers upon them”; of the hexagrams, “observing change in yin and yang.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:024 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:025 -->

**Kong Yingda:** The Appended Statements speaks of the beginning of Fuxi’s making of the Changes, so it simply describes looking upward and examining downward. Here the discussion concerns what follows the doubling of the trigrams: arranging the stalks and laying out the lines. Thus it mentions bringing forth the yarrow before establishing the hexagrams. It does not mean that the sages’ hidden assistance preceded their observation of change.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:025 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:026 -->

**Master Shao:** Heaven’s causing me to possess this is called the mandate. The mandate present in me is called nature. Nature present in things is called principle.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:026 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:027 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu:** Asked, “Does ‘observing change in yin and yang and establishing the hexagrams’ mean observing through the stalk numbers?” He replied, “I think it means simply observing yin and yang. There is no need yet to speak of stalk numbers.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:027 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:028 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu:** Asked, “Once there are hexagrams, there are lines. Why mention hexagrams first and lines afterward?” He replied, “When the hexagram is first established, one sees only the hexagram. When its distinctions are examined closely, there are six lines.” Asked further, “Yin and yang are the same as firm and yielding. Why speak of them separately?” He replied, “‘Observing change in yin and yang’ speaks more closely of creative transformation; ‘unfolding the firm and yielding’ speaks more closely of human affairs. Take Peace: considered as a hexagram, one sees only the meaning of ‘the small goes, the great comes,’ the waning and waxing of yin and yang. Within its lines there are such things as ‘embracing the uncultivated.’”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:028 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:029 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu:** “Harmonizing and according with the Way and virtue is silent accord with the source. Ordering through rightness is responding fittingly to change. Every thing has its principle, and each must be investigated to the utmost. Nature is where principle reaches its fullness; hence ‘fully realize.’ The mandate is the source from which nature comes; hence ‘reach.’”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:029 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:030 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu:** Asked about “fathoming principle, fully realizing nature, and reaching the mandate,” he replied, “This originally speaks of the Changes. All these principles are fully present in it: it truly fathoms the principles of things, fully realizes human nature, and reaches Heaven’s mandate. Thus the Tongshu says that the Changes is the source of nature and the mandate.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:030 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:031 -->

**Xiang Anshi:** The Way is the mandate; virtue is nature; rightness is principle. “Harmonizing and according with the Way and virtue, and ordering things through rightness,” and “fathoming principle, fully realizing nature, and thereby reaching the mandate” express the same relationship in both directions. The Changes’ odd and even lines, in Heaven’s mandate, are the Way of yin and yang; in human nature, the virtues of humaneness and rightness; in what is fitting to Earth, the principles of firm and yielding. Harmonizing and according with the Way and virtue and ordering through rightness proceeds from the hidden to the manifest: this is what is called “making the Way manifest.” Fathoming principle, realizing nature, and reaching the mandate proceeds from the manifest to the hidden: this is what is called “making virtue and conduct numinous.”[^j17-first-chapter-repairs]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:031 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:032 -->

**Chen Chun:** When principle and nature are contrasted, principle means principle in things, and nature means principle in oneself. In things, it is the principle shared by Heaven, Earth, human beings, and other things. In oneself, it is this same principle already fully present as one’s own.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:032 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:033 -->

**Xu Ji:** For example, Qian is Heaven’s Way; the Judgment’s “origination, success, benefit, and steadfastness” are its virtues; the lines’ hiding, appearing, leaping, and flying are its particular rightnesses. Speaking of the whole hexagram is what is meant by harmonizing and according; speaking of the six lines is what is meant by ordering. One who studies the Changes well extends the meanings of the lines to fathom the principles of the world and understands the hexagrams’ virtues to realize fully one’s own nature. Once principle is fathomed and nature realized, the great Way of advance and retreat, preservation and loss, gain and relinquishment can be known, and Heaven’s mandate is within oneself.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:033 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:034 -->

**Gong Huan:** The first clause proceeds from the source into its streams; the second, from the branches back to the root. Only by harmonizing and according with the Way and virtue can one order things through rightness. Only by fathoming principle and fully realizing nature can one reach the mandate.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:034 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:035 -->

**Master Lu:** Establishing hexagrams and bringing forth lines should be understood in terms of the sages’ making of the Changes. To explain them as changes in stalk numbers would instead concern using the Changes. Zhu is right to say that there is no need yet to speak of stalk numbers. The sages observed Heaven and Earth’s Way of change and transformation and established Qian, Kun, and the other hexagrams; hence “observing change in yin and yang, they established the hexagrams.” Having observed images and established hexagrams, they then minutely unfolded the two kinds of stroke, firm and yielding, whether above or below within each hexagram, bringing forth the changing lines. Hence “unfolding the firm and yielding, they brought forth the lines.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:035 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:036 -->

**He Kai:** Once numbers take shape, the hexagrams are established. Once hexagrams are established, lines arise. Harmonizing and according with the Way and virtue and ordering through rightness proceeds from union to distinction. Fathoming principle and realizing nature to reach the mandate proceeds from distinction to union. Principle and rightness are not two: as Master Cheng says, “In things it is principle; in dealing with things it is rightness.” Nature and mandate are not different from Way and virtue: as Zisi says, “What Heaven mandates is called nature; following nature is called the Way.” Fathoming, fully realizing, and reaching all mean attaining the utmost. Nature is principle’s source: fathom principle and one meets its source. Thus fathoming principle fully realizes nature. The mandate is nature’s source: realize nature and one meets its source. Thus realizing nature reaches the mandate. It is only one undertaking.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:036 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:037 -->

**General Discussion — Kong Yingda:** The passage from “in ancient times, the sages” through “thereby reached the mandate” is about to explain why the sages extended the trigrams and doubled them. It therefore first recounts their original institution of yarrow, numbers, hexagrams, and lines, fully setting forth the subtle and ultimate principles of Heaven’s Way and human affairs.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:037 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:038 -->

**He Kai:** This chapter comprehensively discusses yarrow, hexagrams, and line statements. “The sages” means Fuxi, King Wen, and the Duke of Zhou. The Qian zaodu says, “He who handed down the august stalks was Xi.” Thus stalks were already used in Fuxi’s time. The hexagram and line statements were not appended until King Wen and the Duke of Zhou. This shows that the chapter speaks of the whole process.[^j17-qiandao]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:038 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:039 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** This chapter’s sequence is entirely clear. Here one can see that the Changes is a book of divination and also the source of the Five Classics. Bringing forth the yarrow means establishing the method of yarrow divination; establishing numbers means giving rise to its numbers. Establishing hexagrams and bringing forth lines refers to drawing the figures and appending statements. These two form the substance of yarrow divination, yet they are mentioned afterward to show that the Changes was made for divination. “Harmonizing and according with the Way and virtue, and ordering things through rightness” means that once the figures are established, they accord with Heaven’s Way and nature’s virtue, extending downward to encompass what is fitting in affairs and things. “Fathoming principle, fully realizing nature, and thereby reaching the mandate” means that once line statements are provided, they fathom the principles of affairs and human nature, reaching upward to the source of Heaven’s mandate. The Changes teaches through divination, yet the depths of Way, virtue, nature, and mandate reside within it. Those who discuss it only in terms of the peripheral signs of fortune and misfortune have lost their way at the Way’s source. Those who discuss it only in terms of the outward traces of affairs and things have missed the intention of its teaching.

## Chapter 2 · The Three Powers and Six Positions

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:040 -->

### In ancient times, when the sages made the Changes, they intended to accord with the principles of nature and the mandate. Thus they established Heaven’s Way as yin and yang, Earth’s Way as yieldingness and firmness, and humanity’s Way as humaneness and rightness. Embracing the Three Powers and doubling them, the Changes therefore forms a hexagram with six strokes. Distinguishing yin and yang and employing yieldingness and firmness by turns, the Changes therefore forms a pattern through its six positions.[^j17-three-powers]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:040 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:041 -->

**Original Meaning.** Embracing the Three Powers and doubling them speaks of the six strokes as a whole. Distinguish them more closely, and the yin and yang positions interweave to form an articulated pattern.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:041 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:042 -->

**Original Meaning — Chapter Summary.** This is Chapter Two.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:042 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:043 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:043 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:044 -->

**Cui Jing:** This explains how the six lines of one hexagram contain the meaning of the Three Powers, each in two aspects. Thus Heaven’s Way is established through yin and yang, Earth’s through firm and yielding, and humanity’s through humaneness and rightness. Why? Though Heaven is firm, it also possesses yielding virtue; though Earth is yielding, it also possesses firm virtue. Thus the Documents says, “In deep reserve, firmness prevails; in lofty brightness, yieldingness prevails.” Human beings receive their endowment from Heaven and Earth: how could they not embrace both humaneness and rightness? Thus the Way of the Changes includes them together.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:044 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:045 -->

**Zhu Zhen:** The Changes has the Supreme Ultimate. Yin and yang are the Great Void gathering as qi; yieldingness and firmness are qi gathering into form. Humaneness and rightness are rooted in the Great Void, appear in qi and form, and act through awareness and understanding. Viewed from the myriad things’ single source, this is called nature; viewed from their endowment, the mandate; viewed from Heaven, Earth, and humanity, principle. The three are one. Intending to accord with the principles of nature and the mandate, the sages speak of yin and yang, yieldingness and firmness, humaneness and rightness, to establish the Ways of Heaven, Earth, and humanity. Each expression also reveals the others. The Changes embraces the Three Powers and doubles them, forming a hexagram with six strokes: the Three Powers thereby unite as one. Yet the Way changes and moves; hence “distinguishing yin and yang and employing yieldingness and firmness by turns.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:045 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:046 -->

**Guo Yong:** “Distinguishing yin and yang” does not refer only to the yin and yang that establish Heaven’s Way. It says that the two aspects of each of the Three Powers are one yin and one yang, shown in the six positions. “Employing yieldingness and firmness by turns” does not refer only to the yielding and firm that establish Earth’s Way. It says that the yin and yang of the Three Powers are distributed as six strokes, occupied in turn by the yielding and firm of six and nine. Thus, when the two aspects of the Three Powers are discussed without reference to nine and six, they are called six strokes. Only when the yielding and firm of nine and six are included are they called six positions.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:046 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:047 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu:** “Yin and yang, firm and yielding, humaneness and rightness: it seems the last should be ‘rightness and humaneness.’ Humaneness should correspond to yang. Were humaneness not yang and firm, how could it accomplish so much creative transformation? Rightness, though firm, presides over gathering inward; humaneness presides over unfolding outward. This too expresses yin within yang and yang within yin, each concealing the other’s root. Consider the use of rewards and punishments. In granting something to someone, one naturally has no doubt and proceeds. In punishment or execution, one hesitates and is unwilling to decide resolutely. Here one sees yang unfolding and yin drawing inward: humaneness belongs to yang, rightness to yin.”[^j17-virtue-order]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:047 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:048 -->

**Qiu Fuguo:** Earlier it said “fathoming principle, realizing nature, and reaching the mandate”; here it says “according with nature and the mandate.” Thus the principles spoken of in the Changes are all nature and mandate. Yet these principles are precisely yin and yang, yieldingness and firmness, humaneness and rightness. “Embracing the Three Powers and doubling them” describes doubling the trigrams. At their lesser completion, the three strokes already contain the Ways of the Three Powers. Doubled to six, Heaven, Earth, and humanity each have two: this is “forming a hexagram with six strokes.” Distinguishing yin and yang concerns positions. In every hexagram, the first, third, and fifth positions are yang; the second, fourth, and top positions are yin. From bottom to top, yin and yang each occupy half; hence “distinguishing.” Employing yieldingness and firmness by turns concerns lines. Yielding means six, firm means nine. A yang position can be occupied by either a firm or a yielding line; a yin position by either a yielding or a firm line. Now yielding, now firm, they serve alternately; hence “by turns.” The distinctions display its warp, the alternating use supplies its weft. Warp and weft interweave into a brilliant pattern: this is “forming a pattern through six positions.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:048 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:049 -->

**Wu Cheng:** The principle of nature means humanity’s Way; the principle of the mandate means Heaven and Earth’s Way. Heaven’s qi has yin and yang, Earth’s substance yieldingness and firmness, and human virtue humaneness and rightness. The Way is what governs this qi and substance and constitutes these virtues.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:049 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:050 -->

**Wu Cheng:** Above, yin and yang constitute Heaven’s Way; below, the same two words refer comprehensively to the six positions. Among them, the first, third, and fifth are distinguished as yang positions, the second, fourth, and top as yin positions. Above, yielding and firm constitute Earth’s Way; below, the same two words refer comprehensively to the six strokes. Among them, all odd strokes are called firm, all even strokes yielding. Positions have no substance, so they are named yin and yang. Strokes have substance, so they are named firm and yielding. The alternation of yin and yang positions is fixed in its distribution; the differing firm and yielding strokes occupy them by turns. The Appended Statements’ “things intermingling are called pattern” means precisely the formation of a pattern here.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:050 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:051 -->

**Hu Bingwen:** The previous chapter’s “harmonizing and according with the Way and virtue” speaks of the whole; “ordering things through rightness” speaks of distinctions. This chapter’s “forming a hexagram with six strokes” speaks of the whole. Distinguishing yin and yang, employing yieldingness and firmness by turns, and forming a pattern through six positions again speaks of distinctions.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:051 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:052 -->

**Cai Qing:** “Establishing Heaven’s Way” does not mean that someone establishes it. It means that Heaven’s Way is established through yin and yang. “Distinguishing yin and yang” means yin and yang distinguishing themselves. “Employing yieldingness and firmness by turns” means firm and yielding operating by turns of themselves. There is no separate agent who distinguishes or employs them.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:052 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:053 -->

**He Kai:** This chapter says that the figures accord with the principles of nature and the mandate, gathering into a single expression what the previous chapter called “harmonizing and according with the Way and virtue, ordering through rightness, fathoming principle, realizing nature, and reaching the mandate.” Nature is principle in humanity; mandate is principle in Heaven and Earth. Yin and yang, firm and yielding, humaneness and rightness are precisely these principles. Distinguishing yin and yang and employing firm and yielding to decide good fortune and misfortune and bring people’s endeavors to completion already includes the Way of humaneness and rightness.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:053 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:054 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** The previous chapter discussed the Way of the Changes as a whole; this chapter and those that follow explain the figures in particular. The previous chapter said, “observing change in yin and yang, they established the hexagrams,” and “harmonizing and according with the Way and virtue, and ordering through rightness.” This chapter unfolds those meanings. Nature is virtue; mandate is the Way. As nature and mandate circulate through affairs and things, they are named principle: this is Way and virtue dispersed as particular rightnesses. Hence the comprehensive expression “principles of nature and the mandate.” Six strokes forming a hexagram resemble the Way of the Three Ultimates. Do they not harmonize and accord with Heaven and Earth’s Way and the virtue of human nature? Six positions forming a pattern make the workings of yin and yang, firm and yielding, humaneness and rightness inexhaustible. Do they not fully articulate the principles of what is fitting in affairs and things?

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:054 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:055 -->

**Editorial Judgment, continued.** Earlier scholars read the three expressions “embracing the Three Powers and doubling them,” “distinguishing yin and yang,” and “employing yieldingness and firmness by turns” as speaking directly of the Changes. Examining the wording closely, they should first be understood as speaking of creative transformation. Embracing, distinguishing, and employing are not verbs of deliberate exertion. The passage says that the Ways of the Three Powers, taken together, each have two aspects: therefore the Changes forms a hexagram with six strokes. The Ways of the Three Powers are differentiated through opposition and operate through alternation: therefore the Changes forms a pattern through six positions. Only this reading accords with the force of “therefore the Changes.” Cai’s explanation fits especially well.

## Chapter 3 · Earlier Heaven: Opposition and Reckoning

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:056 -->

### Heaven and Earth establish their positions; mountains and lakes communicate their qi; thunder and wind press upon one another; water and fire do not assail one another. The eight trigrams interweave.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:056 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:057 -->

**Original Meaning.** Master Shao says: These are Fuxi’s positions of the eight trigrams. Qian is south, Kun north, Li east, Kan west; Dui occupies the southeast, Zhen the northeast, Xun the southwest, and Gen the northwest. The eight trigrams then combine with one another to form the sixty-four hexagrams. This is what is called the learning of Earlier Heaven.[^j17-earlier-heaven]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:057 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:058 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:058 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:059 -->

**Kong Yingda:** This passage explains the doubling of the trigrams through their images. Were Heaven and Earth not to interact, and water and fire to remain apart, the many kinds of things would have no means of generation and completion, nor would their changes and transformations have a principle. Thus Heaven and Earth establish their positions yet unite their virtues; mountains and lakes have different forms yet communicate their qi; thunder and wind each move yet press upon one another; water and fire do not enter one another yet assist one another. Such are the changing workings of the eight trigrams. Therefore the sages doubled them, making them interweave: Qian and Kun, Zhen and Xun, Kan and Li, Gen and Dui all interact, imaging the universal interweaving of Heaven, Earth, thunder, wind, water, fire, mountains, and lakes. Thus the Changes’ lines and hexagrams equal Heaven and Earth. The principles of nature and mandate, the numbers of good fortune and misfortune, past affairs, and the first stirrings of what is to come are all fully present within them.

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:060 -->

**Xiang Anshi:** Though the trigrams are eight, in truth they are simply the two terms yin and yang. Thus, although positions are established, qi communicates; although their forces press upon one another, their dispositions are not mutually averse. This shows that they are fundamentally one thing.

**Conversations of Master Zhu:** “Examine closely the intricacies of the Earlier Heaven diagram. If Qian One is arranged in a horizontal sequence through Kun Eight, this is entirely spontaneous. **Chen Zhi:** The Changes is fundamentally reverse reckoning. Once there is one, there are two; once two, four; once four, sixteen, and so on to sixty-four. From this one can know that, and from the present know the future. Thus Qian One through Kun Eight are all generated in sequence, just as in the horizontal diagram. Now, to make the circular diagram represent the rounded heavens, simply following that same sequence would put Qian and Kun side by side and fail to distinguish cold and heat. Therefore Fuxi establishes Qian and Kun above and below, arrays Kan and Li as the gates at left and right, and sets Gen and Dui, Zhen and Xun opposite one another, pairing yin and yang throughout. Starting with the birth of one yang at the winter solstice, one passes between Li and Zhen at the spring equinox and reaches pure yang in Qian. This advances to figures already born, like counting yesterday again from today; hence “counting what has gone is forward.” Starting with the birth of one yin at the summer solstice, one passes between Gen and Dui at the autumn equinox and reaches pure yin in Kun. This advances by tracing figures not yet born, like reckoning tomorrow in advance from today; hence “knowing what is coming is reverse.” Yet fundamentally the Changes is formed simply by proceeding from Qian One to Kun Eight, as in the horizontal sequence and on the right side of the circular diagram. Thus “the Changes reckons in reverse.”[^j17-chen-diagram]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:067 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:068 -->

**Hu Bingwen:** The various commentators all explain that the past, being readily seen, is forward, while foreknowing the future is reverse. Since the Changes chiefly precedes and guides the people’s use, it says, “the Changes reckons in reverse.” Only the Original Meaning, following Master Shao, takes the passage about counting the past forward to refer to the circular diagram and the circulation of trigram qi, and the phrase “the Changes reckons in reverse” to refer to the horizontal diagram and the generation of the lines. Had the Original Meaning not unfolded Master Shao’s depths, how would students know what is meant by the learning of Earlier Heaven?

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:068 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:069 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Such is Zhu’s understanding of forward and reverse in this passage. Yet it and Master Shao’s original meaning form distinct explanations. Shao’s original meaning takes tracing back from three yin and three yang to one yin and one yang as forward, and gradually tracing from one yin and one yang to three yin and three yang as reverse. Zhu takes counting the already-born figures among the four trigrams on the left as forward, and tracing the not-yet-born figures among the four on the right as reverse. Both explanations may stand. Shao’s, however, connects the wording of these two chapters especially well. The passage beginning “Heaven and Earth establish their positions” moves from Qian and Kun toward Zhen and Xun: this counts what has gone. The passage beginning “thunder sets them moving” moves from Zhen and Xun toward Qian and Kun: this knows what is coming. These three clauses continue the preceding passage and introduce the next, saying that in the diagram, counting what has gone is forward and knowing what is coming is reverse. The sequence just listed is forward reckoning, honoring Qian and Kun and placing the six children after them. But the intention in establishing the diagram is to display yin and yang’s circulation and the order of their activities. Its emphasis therefore lies in reverse reckoning, as developed in the next passage. The explanations are given fully in the Introduction to the Study of the Changes.

**Master Zhang:** Yin’s nature condenses and gathers; yang’s nature issues forth and disperses. What yin gathers, yang must disperse; their forces are evenly dispersed. **Editorial Judgment.** These two chapters explain Fuxi’s trigram positions. The principles of Heaven, Earth, and the myriad things are exhausted in reciprocal and successive change. Establishing positions, communicating qi, pressing upon one another without assailing one another, and ultimately interweaving: these are reciprocal change. Moving, dispersing, moistening, warming, resting, and delighting, gathered under ruling and storing: these are successive change. Establishing positions, communicating qi, pressing upon one another without assailing one another correspond to “rubbing against one another” in the first chapter of the Appended Statements. The eight trigrams interweaving correspond to its “stirring one another.” Zhen and Li on the left correspond to “stirring them with thunder and lightning”; Xun and Kan on the right to “moistening them with wind and rain.” Dui delighting things and Gen bringing them to rest correspond to “now cold, now heat.” Qian ruling and Kun storing and carrying correspond to Qian’s Way forming the male and governing the great beginning, and Kun’s Way forming the female and completing things. The intervening discussion of forward and reverse clarifies the trigram sequence. Beginning with Heaven and Earth and proceeding to the six children is the sequence of constitution. In the diagram’s positions, it counts what has gone; its principle is that of the Appended Statements’ passage beginning with Heaven exalted and Earth lowly and concluding with images and forms. Beginning with the six children and proceeding to Heaven and Earth is the sequence of activity. In the diagram’s positions, it knows what is coming; its principle is that of the passage beginning with thunder, lightning, wind, and rain and concluding with Qian and Kun. The diagram’s intention follows the sequence of activity. Master Shao is right to say that this passage directly explains the diagram. Yet without constitution there is no basis to establish. **Editorial Judgment, continued.** Gen and Dui do not name mountain and lake here because they refer to qi. Summer qi is warm, hot, and generative; hence “Dui delights them.” Winter qi is severe, congealing, and gathering; hence “Gen brings them to rest.” After thunder, lightning, wind, and rain, the upper Appended Statements likewise says “now cold, now heat” rather than naming mountains and lakes. Thunder moves the accumulated cold qi, and the sun warms it; wind disperses the accumulated summer qi, and rain moistens it. These fit the trigram images closely. Qian’s ruling and Kun’s storing also refer chiefly to high summer and deep winter. High summer, as the following chapter says, is when “all things become visible to one another” and one “faces the light to govern”: this is ruling them. Deep winter is “where all things return”: this is storing them within.

**Master Cheng:** The positions of the eight trigrams in the Changes have never really been explained. Abundant yin essence complies with yang, and nourishment reaches its fullest. Yang’s harmony is sufficient within, yin’s moisture sufficient without: things are delighted and reach completion. Yet Heaven’s Way relies on yin and employs it to accomplish its work, and overcomes yin and transforms it to carry out its mandate. From beginning to end, all is Heaven. At the juncture of ending and beginning, its unceasing vigor becomes apparent: this is what makes Heaven Heaven. Here those who serve come to rest; thus Kan, through the meaning of practiced familiarity, presides over labor. Here what moves comes to rest; thus Gen, through the inexhaustibility of movement and stillness, presides over completion. King Wen’s positions differ from Fuxi’s. Yet only by considering them together can one fully understand creative transformation: the subtle interweaving of constitution and activity, the workings through which movement and stillness are rooted in one another.[^j17-wen-fuxi]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:107 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:108 -->

**Editorial Judgment, continued.** This is the extent of Confucius’s explanation of King Wen’s intention. Some scholars explain it through the five phases. Their accounts are also detailed and precise, so we have included them fully for comparison. Yet even their accounts of Kun and Gen leave something unexplained. The Lü ordinances assign earth sole rule in the month of Wei and place it at the center: thus earth has only one position. Jing Fang distributes earth’s rule among Chen, Xu, Chou, and Wei, assigning it the ends of the four seasons: thus earth has four positions. King Wen’s trigrams, however, have only the two earths, Kun and Gen, placed at Chou and Wei. Compared with the Monthly Ordinances this adds one; compared with Jing Fang it removes two. Why? Wood’s generation of fire and metal’s generation of water do not depend on earth. But without earth, fire cannot complete metal, and water cannot generate wood. Earth’s achievement is therefore especially apparent here. Moreover, throughout the year, yin and yang qi alternately prevail over one another. Yang prevailing over yin produces wood’s warmth and fire’s heat: from Mao through Wei the hexagrams have more yang. Yin prevailing over yang produces metal’s coolness and water’s cold: from You through Chou the hexagrams have more yin. Only at Chou’s junction with Yin and Wei’s junction with Shen are there hexagrams of three yin and three yang. The two qi are then exactly balanced, meeting in centered harmony. Thus these alone are the seats of earth’s virtue. Such subtle meaning also lies beyond the reach of the various technical systems. There is further subtlety in the arrayed images and reciprocal transformations of Earlier and Later Heaven; see the supplementary discussion in the Introduction to the Study of the Changes.[^j17-earth-months]

## Chapter 6 · The Numinous Working of the Six Children

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:109 -->

### The numinous is spoken of in terms of the subtle working of the myriad things. Nothing moves the myriad things more swiftly than thunder; nothing bends them more swiftly than wind; nothing dries them more fiercely than fire; nothing delights them more than the lake; nothing moistens them more than water; nothing more abundantly ends and begins them than Gen. Thus water and fire reach one another, thunder and wind do not oppose one another, and mountains and lakes communicate their qi. Only then can change and transformation bring all things to completion.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:109 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:110 -->

**Original Meaning.** Qian and Kun are omitted here and only the six children discussed, to show what the numinous accomplishes. Yet their positions and sequence follow the preceding chapter’s explanation; the meaning is not fully clear.[^j17-six-summary]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:110 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:111 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:111 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:112 -->

**Han Kangbo:** Speaking of the numinous here makes clear that the eight trigrams move, change, transform, and shift without anything making them do so. The numinous is no thing: it is spoken of in terms of the subtle working of the myriad things. Thus thunder’s swiftness, wind’s movement, fire’s heat, and water’s moisture all spontaneously join in change and transformation, enabling the completion of all things.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:112 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:113 -->

**Cui Jing:** The text describes the workings of six trigrams without mentioning Qian and Kun because Heaven and Earth act without contrivance yet leave nothing undone. Thus they accomplish the numinous subtlety of thunder, wind, and the others’ active workings. Why does Gen alone use the trigram’s name rather than “mountain”? Moving, bending, drying, and moistening are the achievements of thunder, wind, fire, and water. But ending and beginning the myriad things does not fit the meaning of mountain in the same way. Thus Gen names the trigram while the others name things, each according to what best suits the explanation.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:113 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:114 -->

**Zhu Zhen:** Master Zhang says, “One is numinous; two bring transformation.” The subtle working of the myriad things is what is meant by one being numinous. And what equals the six children in moving, bending, drying, delighting, moistening, and ending and beginning the myriad things? Yet none can transform alone. They must reach one another, not oppose one another, and communicate their qi; only then can change and transformation complete all things. Joining, they transform; transforming, they are numinous.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:114 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:115 -->

**Xiang Anshi:** Moving, bending, drying, delighting, moistening, and abundant completion follow Later Heaven’s sequence of separate governance. Reaching one another, not opposing one another, communicating qi, and transforming return to Earlier Heaven’s positions of mutual union. This shows that when the five qi spread in sequence and take their allotted rule through the seasons, the reality of the Nonpolar and the essences of the two powers and five phases subtly unite and condense without ever contradicting the workings of Earlier Heaven.[^j17-ultimate-essences]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:115 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:116 -->

**Xiang Anshi:** The lake is assigned delight rather than moistening because moistening is qi’s inward wetness, while delight is color’s outward radiance. The lake’s qi floats upward and its brilliance overflows outward, making it delightful and lovely. But the work of moistening things, seeping deeply and enduringly through them, belongs fully to water alone.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:116 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:117 -->

**Wu Cheng:** Following the preceding chapter on King Wen’s trigram positions, this speaks of six trigrams’ workings. It omits Qian and Kun because Qian and Kun are the Sovereign governing the myriad things, active within the six children. This is what is meant by “the numinous is spoken of in terms of the subtle working of the myriad things.” The myriad things have visible traces; the numinous within them has none. Yet the numinous does not depart from things. What is unfathomably subtle within the myriad things is the numinous; hence their subtle working. Thunder’s moving, wind’s bending, fire’s drying, the lake’s delighting, water’s moistening, and Gen’s ending and beginning are all Qian and Kun’s numinous working. Moving brings forth shoots and rouses what hibernates: Zhen’s emergence. Bending blows upon things and nurtures their growth: Xun’s ordering. Drying blazes and warms them in the sun: Li’s mutual visibility. Delighting brings gladness and fullness: Dui’s delight. Moistening supplies fluids and returns things to their roots: Kan’s labor. Ending and beginning bring origination forth beneath steadfastness: Gen’s completion.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:117 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:118 -->

**Hu Bingwen:** Chapters Three and Four discuss Earlier Heaven; Chapter Five discusses Later Heaven. This sixth chapter works back from Later Heaven to Earlier Heaven. It omits Qian and Kun and speaks only of the six children to show what the numinous accomplishes. In speaking of the numinous, Qian and Kun are already included. What makes thunder move, wind bend, and ultimately Gen end and begin things—the changes and transformations of Later Heaven—actually comes from Earlier Heaven. Earlier Heaven’s water and fire reaching one another, and the other pairings in sequence, are yin and yang’s union. Later Heaven’s moving thunder, bending wind, and the other activities in sequence are the five phases’ transformations. Only because their union has such subtlety can their transformation have such subtlety.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:118 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:119 -->

**Yu Yan:** When things begin to sprout, thunder sets them moving. Sprouted but not yet unfolded, wind bends them. Unfolded but still tender, fire dries them. As they grow, the lake delights their outer aspect and water moistens their inner. Delighted and moistened, they are then brought to rest by Gen. Rest brings an end; the end brings a new beginning. Thus each of the six children has its own activity, and this is how they complete the myriad things. But the capacity to change, transform, and complete everything also depends on their acting in pairs. Only then can change and transformation complete all things.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:119 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:120 -->

**Liang Yin:** The numinous is the Sovereign. The Sovereign is the numinous in its constitution; the numinous is the Sovereign in its activity. Thus what governs the myriad things is the Sovereign; what subtly works through them is the Sovereign’s numinous activity.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:120 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:121 -->

**Cai Qing:** If thunder were confined to moving and wind to bending, each would be restricted to one aspect and could not be called subtle. Heaven and Earth employ the six children to transform the myriad things; their extensions, contractions, changes, and transformations are all Heaven and Earth’s doing. Thus the numinous corresponds to Qian and Kun. Here one can test the meaning of unity’s unfathomability, of being nowhere fixed yet nowhere absent. The numinous is like a ruler; the six children are like six offices with distinct responsibilities. All that the six offices carry out is governed by the sovereign; only then are their six duties jointly fulfilled and the work of government completed.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:121 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:122 -->

**Ye Erzhan:** The numinous is not Qian and Kun themselves, but their unfathomable operation through the six children. Moving, bending, drying, delighting, moistening, ending, and beginning are precisely the changes and transformations that complete all things. Yet Heaven and Earth’s activity is numinous because it is one, while without two there is no transformation. Earlier Heaven’s six children, each obtaining its counterpart, are what is meant by two. This twofoldness establishes constitution. Later Heaven’s changing and transforming to complete all things is what is meant by the transformation of the two. Their transformation is activity in operation. The unfathomable union of these two transforming powers is what is called the numinous.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:122 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:123 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** This chapter brings Fuxi’s and King Wen’s trigram positions together and praises them as a whole. The sequence of successive change is most apparent in Later Heaven; the principle of reciprocal change is clearest in Earlier Heaven. Successive change is transformation: moving, bending, drying, delighting, moistening, ending, and beginning the myriad things. Reciprocal change is the numinous: that through which changes and transformations proceed, Ways operate together without opposition, and things are nourished together without mutual harm. Transformation is the trace of creative activity, gathered under Earth; its visible achievements are therefore called completion. The numinous is the heart that generates things, gathered under Heaven; its unfathomable workings are therefore called subtle.

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:127 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:128 -->

**Kong Yingda:** This passage explains the names of the eight trigrams. Qian images Heaven, whose body turns without cease; hence vigor. Kun images Earth, which compliantly receives Heaven; hence compliance. Zhen images thunder, which stirs the myriad things; hence movement. Xun images wind, which enters everywhere as it travels; hence entry. Kan images water, which occupies dangerous hollows; hence entrapment. Li images fire, which must attach itself to something; hence clinging. Gen images mountain, whose body remains still; hence stopping. Dui images lake, which moistens the myriad things; hence delight.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:128 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:129 -->

**Master Shao:** Qian is odd, yang, and vigorous; nothing in the world equals Heaven’s vigor. Kun is even, yin, and compliant; nothing equals Earth’s compliance, by which it follows Heaven. Zhen is arising: one yang arises. Arising is movement, and nothing equals thunder’s movement. Kan is entrapment: one yang is trapped between two yin. Entrapment is below, and nothing equals water’s downward tendency. Gen is stopping: one yang stops here, and nothing equals mountain’s stopping. Xun is entry: one yin enters beneath two yang, and nothing equals wind’s entry. Li is clinging: one yin clings to two yang. The trigram’s interweaving forms a pattern of brilliant beauty. Nothing equals fire’s brilliance; thus the word also means attachment and clinging. Dui is delight: one yin emerges on the outside and delights things. Nothing equals the lake’s delight.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:129 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:130 -->

**Master Zhang:** Yang trapped in yin is water; yang attached to yin is fire.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:130 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:131 -->

**Master Zhang:** The one that is submerged and cannot emerge is Kan; the one that clings and cannot depart is Li.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:131 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:132 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu:** “Communicating with the virtue of the numinous powers and classifying the dispositions of the myriad things are exhausted in the eight trigrams. <!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:132 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:133 -->

**Xiang Anshi:** Vigor begins in movement and ends in stopping; compliance begins in entry and ends in delight. Yang’s movement aims to find where it can rest; yin’s entry aims to find what delights it.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:133 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:134 -->

**Cai Qing:** From Zhen to Gen, yang proceeds from movement to stillness. From Xun to Dui, yin proceeds from stillness to movement. Kan and Li stand between: Kan turns from movement toward stillness, Li from stillness toward movement.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4709:134 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4709:135 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** The eight trigrams’ names and meanings were first determined from their strokes; images such as thunder, wind, mountains, and lakes were taken afterward. Kong’s explanation is therefore inferior to Shao’s. Yet while Shao correctly explains the three yang trigrams, his account of the three yin trigrams—Xun as yin entering yang and Li as yin attaching itself to yang—does not seem to accord with the classic. When yin is within, yang must enter and disperse it; when yin is in the middle, yang must attach itself and disperse it. Entry and clinging both belong to yang. They are called yin trigrams only because yin substance is present first and governs them. Master Zhang alone says, “Yang trapped in yin is water; yang attached to yin is fire.” He also says that when yin is within and yang outside cannot enter, yang circles unceasingly and becomes wind. This truly exhausts the subtle principles of things.

## Translator’s Endnotes

The base is the repository’s unchanged 易學網 text. Selective comparison used [the Siku Quanshu transcription, fixed revision 759595](<https://zh.wikisource.org/w/index.php?title=御纂周易折中_(四庫全書本)/卷17&oldid=759595>), additional primary texts, and the historical page images identified below. Attested corrections, repunctuation, historical proposals, and unresolved readings are distinguished. This is not a systematic facsimile collation. Descriptive chapter headings are navigational additions, not source headings. The base omits Chapter Six’s summary; its parallel wording is translated only in an endnote.

[^j17-title]: 說卦 (Shuogua) is rendered “Discussion of the Trigrams.” 卦 can name either a three-line trigram or a six-line hexagram; the English distinguishes them where the context does. This juan includes the complete eleven-chapter text and its commentaries, not merely a list of correspondences. Traditional attributions to Fuxi and Confucius are translated as the commentators present them, not independently established here.

[^j17-yarrow]: 生蓍, literally “bring forth the yarrow,” is intentionally left open in the canonical passage. Zhu Xi invokes the flourishing of the plant under good government; Kong Yingda, Xiang Anshi, and the compilers interpret it as instituting the method of yarrow divination. Cheng’s distinction concerns the priority of the figures, not a denial that stalks are used in consultation.

[^j17-guice]: The base misprints the title as 龜莢傳. The parallel Siku transcription reads 龜筴傳, the account of turtle and stalk divination in the Records of the Historian. Its claim about flourishing stalks is a traditional omen account. A zhang is ten chi, a historical length unit; no exact modern length is implied. The base’s 丈 is retained against the parallel transcription’s apparent error 文.

[^j17-three-two]: 參 and 兩 are read here as “assign three” and “assign two,” following the numerical explanations. The circle-and-square argument is a traditional model: the compilers explicitly reject circumference three for diameter one as an exact ratio. Their account of points and figures is translated as an illustrative construction, not as a complete Euclidean proof.

[^j17-eight-correction]: Zhu Xi’s final formula is misprinted 兩二一二 in the base. The parallel Siku transcription has 兩三一二: two threes and one two, 3 + 3 + 2 = 8. The English adopts that supported correction. The other totals are 3 + 3 + 3 = 9; 2 + 2 + 2 = 6; and 2 + 2 + 3 = 7. “Odd” and “even” here name the stalk procedure’s symbolic classes; see Juan Fourteen for the handling of the remainders.

[^j17-principle-nature]: 理 (principle), 性 (nature), 命 (the mandate), 道 (the Way), 德 (virtue), and 義 (rightness) are kept distinct even where a commentator identifies their ultimate ground. “Reaching the mandate” concerns understanding or embodying what Heaven endows, not merely predicting one’s personal fate. The recurring triad follows the terminology of Juan Thirteen.

[^j17-first-chapter-repairs]: Supported transcription repairs in this chapter include 義既理 → 義即理 in Xiang Anshi, and 面 → 而 twice, 已 → 己 in Xu Ji. Master Lu’s citation of Zhu is repunctuated 未用說到蓍數處, “there is no need yet to speak of stalk numbers,” rather than placing a break after 未用. The parallel transcription supports the continuous wording. Xu’s 大道, “great Way,” is retained from the base against the parallel 天道, “Heaven’s Way”: both make sense.

[^j17-qiandao]: He Kai cites the Qian zaodu, a separately transmitted apocryphal work on the Changes, as authority for the antiquity of stalk use. This is his historical argument; its citation is not independent proof that Fuxi personally instituted the method. “Five Classics” is the traditional canonical grouping invoked by the compilers.

[^j17-three-powers]: The Three Powers (三才) are Heaven, Earth, and humanity. The passage connects each with a complementary pair. Odd and even positions are not the same as the firm and yielding lines that occupy them; either kind of line can occupy either kind of position. The compilers further distinguish describing cosmic differentiation from prescribing a drawing operation.

[^j17-virtue-order]: Zhu Xi explicitly questions the sequence 仁與義: since the preceding pairs begin with yin or yieldingness, he thinks “rightness and humaneness” would align them more closely. His proposal is retained as commentary; the canonical order “humaneness and rightness” is not altered. His illustrations of rewards and punishments explain a cosmological classification, not a modern psychological generalization.

[^j17-earlier-heaven]: The arrangement attributed here to Fuxi is conventionally called Earlier Heaven (先天). Read the directions as the commentator gives them: Qian south, Kun north, Li east, Kan west, Dui southeast, Zhen northeast, Xun southwest, Gen northwest. In a traditional south-at-top diagram, east is on the reader’s left. The diagram’s historical attribution is the commentary’s, not a dating established by this translation.

[^j17-reverse]: 順 and 逆 are rendered “forward” and “reverse” in this discussion, but they do not simply mean clockwise and counterclockwise. The commentators distinguish retrospective tracing, prospective generation, the linear diagram, and the two sides of the circular diagram. The compilers explicitly separate Shao Yong’s explanation from Zhu Xi’s; their accounts have not been harmonized into one procedure.

[^j17-kun-repair]: The base ends Zhu’s sequence Xun–Kan–Gen with 神, “spirit.” The parallel transcription reads 坤, Kun, which restores the stated trigram sequence. The English adopts 坤.

[^j17-chen-diagram]: Chen Zhi’s compressed numerical sequence “one, two, four, sixteen … sixty-four” is retained: both consulted transcriptions have sixteen here. It should not be silently rewritten as a step-by-step doubling series. Both also put the spring equinox “between Li and Zhen” and the autumn equinox “between Gen and Dui.” Those phrases do not transparently match the eight-trigram positions listed above, and remain unresolved here; no alternate reading has been substituted. They must not be treated as an unambiguous instruction for drawing a diagram.

[^j17-weather]: Zhang Zai’s explanation is historical qi cosmology, not a statement of modern meteorology. Both Zhezhong transcriptions read 其勢均散, rendered “their forces are evenly dispersed.” The same wording appears in the separate [Zhengmeng, chapter 2, fixed revision 2589370](<https://zh.wikisource.org/w/index.php?title=正蒙/02&oldid=2589370>). Its precise relation to the ensuing balance of yin and yang remains awkward; it has not been silently changed to “evenly matched.”

[^j17-great-beginning]: Both consulted Zhezhong transcriptions misquote 乾知大始 with 犬 (“dog”) in place of 大 (“great”). The English restores “governs the great beginning” by comparison with the canonical sentence and its commentary in [Chinese Juan Thirteen](../source/juan-13.md). This is a repair of a misquotation shared by the transcriptions, not a recovered variant from the parallel Zhezhong text.

[^j17-two-changes]: 交易 is rendered “reciprocal change,” 變易 “successive change”: the first concerns interaction between paired powers, the second their changing operations in sequence. These are the compilers’ distinctions. Their summer association for Dui here belongs to their reading of Earlier Heaven and should not be substituted for Dui’s autumn position in the following Later Heaven chapter.

[^j17-sovereign]: 帝 is “the Sovereign,” rather than the unqualified English “God” or an earthly emperor. Zhu defines it as Heaven’s governing power; later commentators distinguish 帝, the governing aspect, from 神, its numinous working. Their explanations range from rulership imagery to an immanent cosmology, and are not reduced here to a single theology.

[^j17-later-heaven]: This chapter presents the arrangement attributed to King Wen, conventionally Later Heaven (後天): Zhen east, Xun southeast, Li south, Kun southwest, Dui west, Qian northwest, Kan north, Gen northeast. The text associates Dui with 正秋, rendered “the height of autumn,” not with a Gregorian date. In 說言 and 成言, 言 is a particle, not an instruction to speak.

[^j17-kan-labor]: 勞 is translated “labor” in the canonical sequence and in explanations that stress water’s ceaseless work. The compilers later place the resting of workers here and speak of Kan’s charge over labor; this allows a nuance of tending or bringing labor to rest. These interpretations remain distinct instead of forcing every gloss into one English sense.

[^j17-seasonal-model]: The identifications of wood, fire, earth, metal, and water belong to five-phase cosmology. Seasonal and calendrical claims are translated as the commentators make them, not as measurements verified by this project. Xiang’s 四季 refers to the closing portions of the four seasons, not merely to the four seasons collectively.

[^j17-phase-punctuation]: Hu Bingwen’s account of generation and conquest is repunctuated at 土生金，克者又順以相生 and 土克水，生者又逆以相克. 克者 and 生者 introduce the following clauses; they are not attached to the preceding phase names. Both transmissions preserve this sequence. His counterbalancing account is retained rather than simplified to the usual generating cycle alone.

[^j17-wen-fuxi]: In the compilers’ concluding account of Later Heaven, the base reads 文之位變乎義. The parallel reads 文之位變乎羲: King Wen’s positions differ from Fuxi’s. The English adopts 羲. 文 and 羲 are abbreviated names here, not “pattern” and “rightness.”

[^j17-earth-months]: The closing argument compares the Lü Monthly Ordinances’ single earth placement with Jing Fang’s four seasonal earth placements, then with Kun and Gen’s two positions. 辰戍丑未 is read 辰戌丑未, Chen–Xu–Chou–Wei, supported by the parallel. These are Earthly Branch names, not month numbers in the modern civil calendar. The reference to three yin and three yang concerns the transitions toward Yin 寅 and Shen 申; it should not be turned into a claim that every figure associated with Chou or Wei has three of each.

[^j17-six-summary]: SUPPLEMENTARY SOURCE SUMMARY. After Zhu Xi’s Original Meaning, the parallel Siku transcription includes 此第六章, “This is Chapter Six.” The base omits it. It is translated here only, not inserted as a source-aligned block or counted among the base’s chapter summaries. The chapter division is also explicitly identified by Hu Bingwen below.

[^j17-ultimate-essences]: Xiang’s 無極之真，二五之精 evokes the language of Zhou Dunyi’s account of the Supreme Ultimate: the reality of the Nonpolar, the essences of the two powers and five phases. “Nonpolar” renders 無極 without treating it as a separate physical region. These are the commentator’s metaphysical terms, not a claim about a measurable substance.

[^j17-trigram-qualities]: The eight concise predicates describe characteristic activity, not eight rigid psychological types. 陷 is “entrapment,” not simply water; 麗 is “clinging,” with an additional play on brilliance or beauty in Shao’s gloss; 止 is “stopping,” distinct from 靜, stillness. The following disagreement concerns whether Xun’s entry and Li’s clinging belong to yin or to yang working upon a prior yin substance.

[^j17-animal-images]: 羊 is rendered “sheep” here as the conventional English category; the Chinese term also covers caprine animals more broadly. 龍 remains “dragon,” not a natural species substituted for the traditional image. These are analogical associations. The ensuing claims about animal posture, illness, and temperament are historical explanations, not diagnostic or veterinary advice.

[^j17-quanyu]: Xiang cites the Zaohua quanyu (造化權輿), whose title may be understood as Beginnings of Creative Transformation. The title is retained in transliteration without resolving its bibliographical attribution. The passage’s broad contrast of horses and cattle is preserved as transmitted.

[^j17-body-repair]: Kong Yingda’s 巽顧 in the base is corrected to 巽順, “pliant compliance,” following the parallel. The three commentator paragraphs here begin with a bold bullet in the source; that typographical accident does not make them canonical text. 股 is “thighs”; Yu and the compilers also allude to the concealed genital region between them. The explanation is anatomical imagery, not a medical claim.

[^j17-family]: “Seeking” (索) is left as the canonical image rather than replaced with a single casting procedure. Zhu’s Original Meaning takes it as seeking a line with the stalks; his reported conversations read it retrospectively from the completed figures. The phrase 加中男 in the base is corrected to 中男, “middle son,” following the parallel.

[^j17-parental-reversal]: The report that Qian seeks from Kun to obtain the sons, and Kun from Qian to obtain the daughters, is preserved exactly as a report of Zhu’s conversation. Both consulted transcriptions have it. Wu Cheng reverses the direction of the seeking: Kun takes a yang stroke from Qian to form a son, and Qian takes a yin stroke from Kun to form a daughter. The compilers explicitly endorse Wu and call the conversation’s record mistaken. This disagreement is not silently repaired in the quoted report.

[^j17-bo-horse]: 駁馬 can be read as a mottled or piebald horse, but Kong’s explanation invokes the bo, a fabulous horse-like creature said to have saw-like teeth and eat tigers and leopards. “Bo horse” preserves that possibility rather than silently treating the passage as a description of an ordinary coat color. Qian’s cold and ice follow the Later Heaven northwest position, as Zhu explicitly notes.

[^j17-nine-schools]: The supplemental lists are cited by Zhu Xi under 荀九家 or 荀《九家》, rendered “Xun’s Nine Schools.” This preserves the compilation’s citation label without independently resolving the collection’s authorship. Every added item belongs to the source’s commentary, not to the canonical list and not to a translator’s supplement.

[^j17-kun-images]: 布 is rendered “cloth” in the list, but Cui’s explanation plays on 布 as spreading or distributing. 均 is “evenness,” following the commentaries here, not an imported interpretation such as a potter’s wheel. 柄 is literally a handle and is explained as the support or basis on which things depend.

[^j17-zhen-images]: 旉 is spreading or unfolding. 蒼筤竹 is described by Kong as newly growing blue-green bamboo; no modern species identification is imposed. 萑 and 葦 are retained as two kinds of reeds. 馵足 is a white-marked hind foot; Kong does not specify left in this gloss. 的顙 is a white forehead. “Reversed growth” (反生) receives two explanations: emergence bearing the seed coat and the downward root followed by the upward shoot. Neither is silently substituted for the other.

[^j17-xun-physiognomy]: The bodily features and alleged temperaments in this list are traditional correspondences, not reliable inferences about an actual person’s character. The market phrase is rendered “drawn to gain, with a threefold return in trade”; it is an image, not a promise of investment results. Kong separates 近利 from 市三倍 in his exposition.

[^j17-xun-correction]: Zhai Xuan’s 上二陽其正一陰 is corrected to 上二陽共正一陰, “the two yang above together set the one yin straight,” following the parallel and the [historical page image 1850](https://www.eee-learning.com/eeebook/middleyi/1850.png). The canonical list itself is unchanged.

[^j17-kan-list]: The base’s 水, “water,” is retained against the parallel transcription’s apparent error 氷, “ice”; the accompanying commentaries themselves explain water. The category “in carriages” is taken to govern “many faults,” not all subsequent items down to moon and thief. The Chinese has no new category label there; the English marks the shift with a sentence break. The bodily and animal signs are traditional analogies, not diagnoses. 心病 allows both an ailing heart and distress of the heart-mind.

[^j17-ke]: The added item 可 is unresolved. Both transcriptions and the [historical page image 1852](https://www.eee-learning.com/eeebook/middleyi/1852.png) have 可. “Assent” is only a provisional lexical rendering of that graph, not a securely identified trigram image. No unattested replacement such as 河 or 柯 has been silently adopted. 律 is rendered “pitch standards,” while its broader sense of regulation remains possible; the source supplies no explanation of these added items.

[^j17-light]: Cai Qing’s contrast of outward and inward light belongs to his analogical cosmology of sun, fire, metal, water, and moon. The English retains the distinction without presenting it as a modern account of optics or celestial composition.

[^j17-li-dryness]: 乾 in 為乾卦 is read gān, “dry,” not Qián, the name of the first trigram. The commentary explicitly connects it with the sun’s drying action. 鼈 is a soft-shelled turtle, 蠃 a snail or similar shelled mollusk, 蚌 a bivalve, and 龜 a turtle or tortoise: the English uses familiar broad names, not a claim to precise modern species identification. 科上槁 is interpreted through Kong’s 科，空也: hollow within, withered above.

[^j17-gen-offices]: 閽 and 寺 here name palace offices: gatekeepers and eunuch attendants, not temples. 果蓏 distinguishes fruit borne by trees from fruit of herbaceous plants. 黔喙 is rendered “dark-muzzled creatures,” following Kong’s explanation in terms of mountain-dwelling beasts; “black-beaked birds” would unduly narrow his interpretation. In Yu’s quotation, 內人 refers to women of the inner palace; 女官, “female officials,” is retained from this compilation. The separately transmitted [Offices of Zhou, Tianguan](<https://zh.wikisource.org/zh/周禮/天官冢宰>) instead has 女宮. The main text is not silently conformed to that wording.

[^j17-dui-images]: 巫 is rendered “spirit-medium,” preserving a ritual office rather than importing the modern pejorative sense of “witch.” 附決 is the breaking away of something attached, explained through ripe fruits separating. The concubine image is elaborated through a younger sister accompanying an elder in marriage, a historical institution retained rather than recast as modern partnership.

[^j17-chang]: Both transcriptions and the [historical page image 1856](https://www.eee-learning.com/eeebook/middleyi/1856.png) read 為常 in the supplemental list. “Constancy” gives the ordinary lexical sense of 常, but the correspondence itself is unexplained and remains uncertain. It has not been changed to another graph. 輔頰 is rendered “jaws and cheeks.”

[^j17-final-caveat]: Zhu’s reservation and the compilers’ final question are part of the source itself. The list is not presented as an exhaustive, uniformly explained code: some earlier images are missing from it, and the compilers tentatively suggest that the earlier material reflects inherited transmission while this chapter reflects conventions of the Zhou Changes. The question remains a question.

[^j17-final-corrections]: In Kong’s Dui gloss, the base’s 門舌 is corrected to 口舌, “mouth and tongue,” supported by the parallel and historical page image 1856. In Hu Bingwen’s closing discussion, 水與月剛內明外暗 is read with the parallel’s 則 in place of 剛: “water and the moon are therefore bright within and dark without.” These repairs affect the commentary only. The Five Activities are demeanor, speech, sight, hearing, and thought, as enumerated in the [Documents, Hongfan, revision 5801741](<https://zh.wikisource.org/w/index.php?title=尚書/洪範&oldid=5801741>). Kong here associates speech with the west; the separate [Zhouyi zhengyi, Shuogua](https://ehipassa.org/classics/i/09/) makes this explicit with 五事為言.

[^j17-enlarged-count]: Hu Bingwen reports 112 images. His total is preserved as his count, not presented as an independently established count under a modern itemization convention. The text combines paired terms and categories in ways that make counting dependent on segmentation. His distinction between paired opposition, reversal of the three-line figure, and derivation from a prior image is retained.
