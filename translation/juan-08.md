## 56. Lü 旅 — The Wanderer

<!-- Source: https://www.eee-learning.com/book/4628 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:001 -->

![Lü: Gen below, Li above](../assets/eee-learning/4a8f8cd45910-yi56b.png) **Lü 旅 — The Wanderer** — Gen below; Li above.[^j08-wanderer]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:002 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Concerning The Wanderer, the *Sequence of the Hexagrams* says: “Abundance means greatness. What exhausts its greatness must lose its dwelling; therefore Abundance is followed by The Wanderer.” When flourishing abundance reaches its limit, one must lose the place in which one rests secure. This is why The Wanderer follows Abundance. The hexagram has Li above and Gen below. The mountain remains still and does not move; fire travels and does not stay. This is an image of departing and not remaining, hence The Wanderer. Moreover, attachment to what lies outside is itself an image of wandering.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:002 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:003 -->

### The Wanderer: modest success. For the wanderer, steadfastness brings good fortune.[^j08-small-success]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:004 -->

**Original Meaning.** *Lü* means sojourning away from home. The mountain rests below while fire blazes above: an image of leaving one's resting place and not remaining there. Hence The Wanderer. The fifth six is centered in the outer trigram and complies with the two yang above and below it; Gen keeps still, while Li attaches to clarity. Thus the prognostication permits modest success, and good fortune if one can preserve the correctness proper to a wanderer. A sojourn is not a settled dwelling, and might seem to permit makeshift conduct. Yet the Way is present everywhere: wandering, too, has its own correctness, from which one must not depart even for an instant.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:005 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** This speaks from the hexagram's capacities. With capacities such as these, one may attain modest success and, by attaining the steadfast correctness proper to a wanderer, good fortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:005 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:006 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:006 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:007 -->

**Hu Bingwen:** Success while wandering is success on a small scale. Yet although affairs may be small or great, the Way is present everywhere. Great success certainly benefits from steadfastness; one must not lose steadfastness merely because the success is small. Could the correct Way truly be abandoned even for an instant?

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:007 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:008 -->

### First Six: The wanderer is petty and trifling. This is how one brings disaster upon oneself.[^j08-menial-reading]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:008 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:009 -->

**Original Meaning.** In the time of wandering, yielding yin occupies the lowest position. Hence this image and prognostication.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:009 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:010 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Yielding yin in the time of wandering occupies a lowly place: a weak person, distressed by wandering and sunk in humble circumstances, whose concerns are base. Someone of low aspirations, already in the hardships of a sojourn, will stoop to every kind of meanness and petty conduct. This invites humiliation and brings disaster and blame. “Petty and trifling” describes such mean smallness. With this capacity and constitution in the distress of wandering, even assistance above can accomplish nothing. The fourth is yang by nature and belongs to Li; it is not inclined to descend. Moreover, in The Wanderer its position differs from the great minister's position that it occupies in other hexagrams.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:010 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:011 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:011 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:012 -->

**Wang Yinglin:** On The Wanderer's first six, “this is how one brings disaster upon oneself,” Wang Fusi comments: “performing this menial service.” Guo Jing of the Tang says that *si* 斯 should be written 㒋. In my judgment, however, the annotation to “the office is menial and its stipend slight” in Zuo Xiong's biography in the *History of the Later Han* explains: “斯 means lowly.” There is no need to change it to 㒋.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:012 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:013 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** The first line of the *Changes* often takes the image of a child or young person. In The Wanderer, it takes that of a young servant. Wang's explanation is correct.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:013 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:014 -->

### Six in the Second Place: The wanderer reaches lodging, keeps his resources close, and gains a young servant's steadfast loyalty.[^j08-servants]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:014 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:015 -->

**Original Meaning.** Reaching lodging gives security; keeping one's resources gives sufficiency; obtaining a young servant's steadfast trustworthiness means freedom from deception and someone to rely on. This is the most fortunate condition of wandering. The second has the virtues of yielding compliance, centeredness, and correctness. Hence this image and prognostication.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:015 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:016 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The second has the virtues of yielding compliance, centeredness, and correctness. Through yielding compliance it gains the company of others; through centered correctness it never acts inappropriately. It can therefore preserve what it has, while its servants give their full loyalty and trustworthiness. Although it lacks the fifth's cultivated clarity and assistance from above and below, it too handles wandering well. Lodging is where the wanderer finds security; goods and wealth are the wanderer's resources; servants are those on whom the wanderer depends. To reach lodging, retain one's resources, and obtain faithful, good servants is to fare well in wandering. The young and weak serving below are *tong*; the strong adults attending to matters outside are *pu*. The second's yielding compliance and centered correctness win the hearts of both. On a journey, one's close companions are one's servants. “Good fortune” is not pronounced, because while lodging away from home, simply escaping disaster and danger is already good.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:016 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:017 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:017 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:018 -->

**Hu Bingwen:** While traveling, one cannot dispense with servants' help, yet one often cannot escape their deception either. Only when they are steadfast and trustworthy does one have freedom from deception and someone to rely on.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:018 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:019 -->

**Zhao Yuquan:** The second meets the time of wandering with yielding compliance and centered correctness. Inwardly it does not lose itself, and so is everywhere at ease; outwardly it does not lose others, and so everyone joins with it. All the indispensable supports of a sojourn are thereby preserved.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:019 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:020 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** The second is correctly placed and centered, hence “reaches lodging and keeps his resources.” This differs from the fourth nine's merely finding somewhere to stay and obtaining resources and an axe. The first six adjoins it below, hence “gains a young servant,” unlike the third nine's “loses his young servant.” Considered in its own position, the first is the petty, trifling servant; considered from the second, it is the steadfast servant. These meanings do not conflict.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:020 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:021 -->

### Nine in the Third Place: The wanderer's lodging burns. He loses his young servant. Steadfastness brings danger.[^j08-traveler-third]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:021 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:022 -->

**Original Meaning.** Excessively firm and not centered, it occupies the top of the lower trigram. Hence this image and prognostication. Losing the young servant means more than merely losing the servant's goodwill. Accordingly, “steadfastness” belongs with the following phrase.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:022 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:023 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The Way of sojourning gives priority to yielding compliance and humility. The third is firm without centeredness and occupies both the top of the lower trigram and the top of Gen: an image of self-exaltation. Excessive firmness and self-exaltation while wandering lead to hardship and disaster. Self-exaltation prevents compliance with those above, so they withdraw their support and its lodging burns: it loses its place of security. Li above provides the image of burning. Excessive firmness makes it harsh toward those below, so they leave, and it loses its young servant's steadfast loyalty—that is, it loses the servant's heart. Such conduct leads to danger.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:023 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:024 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:024 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:025 -->

**Pan Mengqi:** Occupying firmness and employing firmness is inadvisable even in ordinary times; how much more so while traveling! Treating those below in this fashion naturally leads to burned lodging and lost servants. The third nine occupies the top of the lower trigram with firmness, and its lodging burns; the top nine occupies the top of the upper trigram with firmness, and its nest burns. The higher the position and the more overbearing the firmness, the deeper the disaster.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:025 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:026 -->

**Qiu Fuguo:** The third nine's statement is the exact reverse of the second's. The second reaches lodging; the third's lodging burns. The second gains a young servant; the third loses one. The second's steadfastness is without fault, whereas the third's steadfastness brings danger, because the second is yielding, compliant, and centered, while the third is excessively firm and not centered. How could excessive firmness be the Way of a wanderer?

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:026 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:027 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** The third is correctly placed and therefore also has the image of reaching lodging. Because it is excessively firm, however, the lodging burns. Of the six lines, only the second and third speak of lodging: both occupy their proper places.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:027 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:028 -->

### Nine in the Fourth Place: The wanderer finds somewhere to stay and obtains resources and an axe. My heart is not content.[^j08-axe]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:028 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:029 -->

**Original Meaning.** Yang occupies a yin place at the bottom of the upper trigram, employing yieldingness and able to humble itself. Hence this image and prognostication. Yet this is not its correct position; above it has no yang-firm ally, and below its correspondent is only yielding yin. Its heart therefore has reason for discontent.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:029 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:030 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Although the fourth's yang firmness is not centered, it occupies a yielding place at the bottom of the upper trigram, giving the image of employing yieldingness and being able to humble itself. This is fitting for a wanderer. With firm and clear ability, supported by the fifth and answered by the first, it handles wandering well. Yet its position is incorrect. Although it finds somewhere to stay, this is not equal to the second's reaching proper lodging. Its firm, clear ability wins support above and below: hence a wanderer obtaining financial resources and useful equipment. Although this is good in wandering, above it has no yang-firm ally, and below its correspondent is only yielding yin. It therefore cannot fully exercise its ability or carry out its purpose; its heart is not content. “My” speaks from the fourth's position.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:030 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:031 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:031 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:032 -->

**Jiang Tisheng:** In the hexagrams and lines generally, yang firmness surpasses yin yieldingness; The Wanderer is otherwise. The second and fifth both attain good fortune through yielding compliance, whereas the third and top bring misfortune through yang firmness. Of the six lines, the fifth six is best and the second next; the top nine is worst and the third next. Although the fourth nine finds a place, this suffices merely to secure its person. How could it fully realize its purpose?

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:032 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:033 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** The fourth's position is incorrect, so the text says not “reaches lodging” but “finds somewhere to stay.” As a wanderer it occupies a place of many apprehensions. Thus, although it gains resources like the second six, it must also add an axe for its own defense. Clearly it has not put vigilance aside. How could its heart be content and at ease?

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:033 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:034 -->

### Six in the Fifth Place: Shoot a pheasant; one arrow is lost. In the end, praise and appointment.[^j08-pheasant]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:034 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:035 -->

**Original Meaning.** The pheasant is a creature of patterned brilliance, an image of Li. The fifth six is yielding, compliant, and brightly cultivated; it also attains the central Way and governs Li. Thus one who obtains this line has the image of shooting a pheasant. Although there is the expense of losing an arrow, the loss is small; in the end there is praise and appointment.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:035 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:036 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The fifth six has the virtues of cultivated clarity and yielding compliance. It attains the central Way and receives support from above and below: the finest way of handling a sojourn. To accord with cultivated clarity while away from home is indeed good. When a wanderer acts and makes a mistake, hardship and humiliation follow; only action without error is good. Li is the pheasant, a creature of patterned brilliance. Shooting the pheasant means taking cultivated clarity as one's standard and invariably meeting it. It is like shooting a pheasant and bringing it down with one arrow: no shot misses, and in the end one obtains praise and appointment. Praise is a good reputation; appointment means blessings and emoluments. The fifth dwells in the position of cultivated clarity and possesses its virtue, so every action accords with its Way. The fifth is ordinarily the ruler's place, but a ruler does not wander; to wander would be to lose his position. Thus the ruler's meaning is not taken here.[^j08-wanderer-five]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:036 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:037 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:037 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:038 -->

**Zhu Zhen:** The fifth in The Wanderer does not take the ruler's image. Possessing cultivated clarity, its good reputation becomes known above, and it receives rank and appointment.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:038 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:039 -->

**Recorded Conversations of Master Zhu.** “Lost” has precisely the meaning it has in “Qin lost no arrow and left no arrowhead behind”; it is not as Yichuan explains it. Whenever the *Changes* speaks of good fortune in the end, the beginning is less favorable.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:039 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:040 -->

**Wang Shenzi:** “One arrow brings it down” speaks of the ease of hitting the mark.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:040 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:041 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Cheng's Commentary is correct that the fifth in The Wanderer does not take the ruler's meaning. In antiquity, men of service and great officers crossing a state's borders traveled with presentation gifts; men of service carried pheasants when presenting themselves. Shooting and obtaining a pheasant images having a means of advancing oneself. Trust among one's fellows brings praise; acceptance by those above brings appointment.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:041 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:042 -->

### Top Nine: The bird's nest burns. The wanderer first laughs, then cries and wails. He loses his ox through carelessness. Misfortune.[^j08-lost-ox]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:042 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:043 -->

**Original Meaning.** The top nine is excessively firm, at the top of The Wanderer and the utmost of Li. Arrogant and uncompliant, it follows a course toward misfortune. Hence this image and prognostication.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:043 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:044 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** A bird flies aloft and dwells high. The top nine is firm without centeredness, occupies the highest place, and also belongs to Li: its overreaching is evident. Hence the image of a bird. In the time of wandering, one can preserve oneself only through humility, yieldingness, and harmony. With excessive firmness and self-exaltation, losing one's security is only to be expected. The nest is where the bird rests secure. Its nest burns: it loses its security and has nowhere to stay. At the top of Li there is an image of burning. Yang firmness places itself at the greatest height, initially delighting in its own intentions; hence first it laughs. Then it loses its security and no one supports it; hence it cries and wails. Through heedlessness it loses the virtue of compliance, and so there is misfortune. The ox is a compliant creature. “Losing the ox through carelessness” means losing one's compliance through heedlessness. Li's fire tends upward, providing an image of impetuous heedlessness. Because this follows “the bird's nest burns,” the word “wanderer” is expressly added. Without “wanderer,” it would be the bird that laughs and cries.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:044 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:045 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:045 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:046 -->

**Wang Zongchuan:** Compared with the third nine, the top nine is still more rigid and overbearing. A bird's nest is something perched high, situated in an exposed and precarious place; therefore The Wanderer's top line takes it as its image. Extreme height necessarily brings danger, and Li's fire supplies the image of burning. Hence “the bird's nest burns.” First laughing means delighting in being above others; later crying and wailing is because the nest has burned. The ox is a compliant creature. The Way of wandering rests on yielding compliance and humility. The top nine loses this most compliant virtue: this is why it meets misfortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:046 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:047 -->

**Xu Ji:** The Wanderer values yielding compliance and centered correctness. All three yang lines fall short of these, and the most overbearing is the top nine.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:047 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4628:048 -->

**General Discussion — Fan Zhongyan:** Inwardly keeping still, without agitation of the heart; outwardly clear, without losing one's way: with these one undertakes a sojourn and thus attains modest success and good fortune through steadfastness. If the wanderer's aspirations are low, he humiliates himself; if high, he incurs hostility. One who can hold to the center may be called wise. Thus the first's petty trifling is lowliness that humiliates itself; the third's burned lodging and the top's burned nest are high-handedness that incurs hostility; the second's retained resources and the fifth's praise and appointment belong to yieldingness that does not lose its center.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4628:048 -->

## 57. Xun 巽 — Penetration

<!-- Source: https://www.eee-learning.com/book/4629 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4629:001 -->

㒋 — *si*, servant.[^j08-stray-si]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4629:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4629:002 -->

![Xun: Xun below, Xun above](../assets/eee-learning/f02cb1b0f422-yi57b.png) **Xun 巽 — Penetration** — Xun below; Xun above.[^j08-xun-title]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4629:002 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4629:003 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Concerning Penetration, the *Sequence of the Hexagrams* says: “Wandering and finding no place of acceptance, one is therefore received by Penetration. Penetration means entering.” A wanderer has few close connections. Without deference and compliance, where could one gain acceptance? With deference and compliance, even amid the hardships of wandering, where could one go without finding entry? This is why Penetration follows The Wanderer. The hexagram has one yin beneath two yang, deferential and compliant toward yang. Hence Penetration.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4629:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4629:004 -->

### Penetration: modest success. It is beneficial to have somewhere to go. It is beneficial to see the great person.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4629:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4629:005 -->

**Original Meaning.** Xun means entering. One yin lies concealed beneath two yang; its nature enables it to enter through deference. Its image is wind, likewise taking the meaning of entry. Yin governs, so its prognostication is modest success. Yin follows yang, so it is also beneficial to have somewhere to go. Yet one must know whom to follow before attaining correctness; hence it further says that it is beneficial to see the great person.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4629:005 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4629:006 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The hexagram's capacities permit modest success, benefit in having somewhere to go, and benefit in seeing the great person. Both Penetration and Joy possess firm centeredness and correctness, and deference and joy have related meanings. Yet Joy has success, while Penetration has only modest success, because Joy is yang's activity and Penetration yin's. In Joy, yieldingness is outside: yieldingness is employed. In Penetration, yieldingness is inside: the nature itself is yielding. This is why Penetration's success is modest.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4629:006 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4629:007 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4629:007 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4629:008 -->

**Guo Yong:** Xun means entering. Because it can enter, it is beneficial to have somewhere to go and to see the great person. Does this not also express mastering inward reserve through firmness?

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4629:008 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4629:009 -->

**Recorded Conversations of Master Zhu.** Xun has the meaning of entering. Xun is wind: like wind entering things, it can enter precisely because it is yielding and penetrative. Within rightness and principle, no subtlety lies beyond its entry.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4629:009 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4629:010 -->

**Zhao Rumei:** One yin arises below, and the two yang penetrate it from above. The hexagram takes its name from its firm lines: yin arises, and yang penetrates it.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4629:010 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4629:011 -->

**Cai Qing:** “Compliance” does not exhaust the meaning of Xun. Only sustained, earnest application of the heart gives its full meaning. Cheng's Commentary speaks only of compliance. Yet Confucius does not say “compliance,” but repeatedly retains the hexagram's name, Xun. There must therefore be a distinction between Xun and compliance. The *Great Commentary* says, “Xun means entering”; also, “Xun is the regulation of virtue”; and also, “Xun weighs things and remains concealed.” It never treats Xun as compliance alone.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4629:011 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4629:012 -->

**He Kai:** Whatever enables Penetration to bring success is yang's doing. The repeated promulgation of commands is a yang undertaking. Yang penetrates from above; hence modest success.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4629:012 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4629:013 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Xun means entering. Interpreters have generally taken this to mean one yin entering beneath two yang. That is incorrect. One yin is concealed within; yang must enter and disperse it. Yin's nature congeals and stagnates, and only after dispersal can it join its virtue with yang. In the transformations of the natural world, this is blowing away floating clouds and dispersing accumulated yin. In the human heart, it is discerning subtle beginnings and probing what lies concealed. In the state, it is removing treachery and correcting defective affairs. None of these three is possible without entering. This is why the hexagram is called Penetration. Its success is modest because, unlike Decay, where complete ruin is made new and success is great, Penetration merely repairs damage and restores what has fallen into neglect. The meanings of geng and jia in the hexagrams and lines make this clear. Once the world's affairs have been examined and understood, understanding must appear in action; hence benefit in having somewhere to go. Only someone of firm virtue can accomplish this; hence benefit in seeing the great person.[^j08-entering-yang][^j08-xun-readings]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4629:013 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4629:014 -->

### First Six: Advancing and retreating. The warrior's steadfastness is beneficial.[^j08-warrior]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4629:014 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4629:015 -->

**Original Meaning.** Yin below governs Xun and is excessively humble and deferential. Hence the image of indecisive advance and retreat. If one meets this with the warrior's steadfastness, one can remedy the deficiency and attain what is fitting.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4629:015 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4629:016 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Yielding yin occupies a humble place, deferential without centeredness, at the very bottom and supporting firmness. Its humility and deference are excessive. When a yielding person is excessively deferential, intention becomes fearful and unsettled: now advancing, now retreating, not knowing what to follow. What benefits it is the warrior's steadfastness. Employing the warrior's firm and steadfast purpose would be fitting. Striving for firm steadfastness removes the faults of excessive humility and fear.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4629:016 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4629:017 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4629:017 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4629:018 -->

**Wang Bi:** At the beginning of a command, one has not yet brought oneself to obey it; hence advance and retreat. Nothing is better than the warrior for carrying a command through and setting disorder straight. Thus the warrior's steadfastness is beneficial for bringing order.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4629:018 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4629:019 -->

**Hu Yuan:** The first six has a yielding yin constitution and also occupies the bottom of the hexagram. Thus it doubts whether to advance or retreat. Benefit lies in the warrior's correctness: only with courage in carrying out affairs can it obtain good fortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4629:019 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4629:020 -->

**Yu Yan:** Penetration is the hexagram of reiterating commands and carrying out affairs. Once a command is issued, it must be carried out. How could wavering between advance and retreat be fitting? The first six is humble and deferential but not centered, weak rather than martial. Hence it advances and retreats without being able to decide. If a warrior handled this situation, steadfast firmness would suffice to accomplish the task. Hence “the warrior's steadfastness is beneficial.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4629:020 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4629:021 -->

### Nine in the Second Place: Penetration beneath the bed. Employ scribes and spirit-mediums in profusion. Good fortune; no blame.[^j08-under-bed][^j08-scribes-mediums]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4629:021 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4629:022 -->

**Original Meaning.** The second is yang in a yin position and below, suggesting uneasiness. Yet in the time of Penetration, humility is not rejected; moreover, the second is centered and does not go too far. Thus the prognostication is that even exceeding ordinary deference, repeatedly and fully explaining oneself so as to make one's intention understood, can bring good fortune without blame. It is also an auspicious prognostication for offering sacrifice with the utmost sincerity.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4629:022 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4629:023 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The second meets the time of Penetration as yang in a yin position below: an excess of deference. The bed is where a person rests. To be deferential beneath the bed is to exceed deference and go beyond one's place of ease. Excessive humility in a person is either timidity or ingratiating flattery, neither of which is correct. The second, however, is firm and centered. Although its place in Xun and in a yielding position makes its deference excessive, it has no wrongful intention. Excessive reverent deference, though not proper ritual measure, can keep humiliation at a distance and prevent resentment and blame; this too is a path to good fortune. Scribes and spirit-mediums communicate sincere intentions to the spirits. “In profusion” means many. If, with utmost sincerity, one rests content in humility and can provide abundant means of communicating that sincerity, there is good fortune without blame: one's sincerity suffices to move others. **Collected Explanations.**

**Feng Yi:** In the *Offices of Zhou*, scribes oversee tortoise-shell and milfoil divination; spirit-mediums oversee purification and rites to avert harm. Divination determines good fortune and misfortune; purification and averting rites remove disasters and harm.

**Editorial Judgment.** Beneath the bed is where hidden malign influences lurk. Entering beneath the bed means examining them deeply. Having learned of them through the scribe's divination, one removes them through the medium's purification. Then, even if uncanny creatures and strange spirits are present, none can cause harm. “In profusion” images the frequency with which commands are reiterated and the minute thoroughness with which affairs are carried out. The second and fifth are both what is meant by “the firm penetrates through centeredness and correctness, and its purpose is fulfilled”: they govern the hexagram. Thus they fully realize the Way of reiterating commands and carrying out affairs.

### Nine in the Third Place: Repeated penetration. Humiliation.[^j08-repeated-penetration]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4629:027 -->

**Original Meaning.** Excessively firm and not centered, at the top of the lower trigram, it is not able to be deferential. It forces itself to do so and repeatedly fails: a path to humiliation. Hence this image and prognostication.

With its rigid, overbearing constitution in a time of deference and compliance, it is not naturally able to defer. It makes an effort and repeatedly fails. Yet in the time of Penetration, it dwells below while those above oversee it through deference; the fourth also draws near with yielding compliance, the line it rests upon is firm, and further firm lines stand above. Even if it wished not to defer, could it do so? Thus it repeatedly fails and repeatedly defers. This is humiliating.

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4629:030 -->

**Zhao Rumei:** Repeated penetration means penetrating, then penetrating again, like repeated return.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4629:031 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4629:032 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Xun means entering, yet it is also called “the regulation of virtue.” If one cannot make decisions and exercise control, the depth of one's penetration merely allows defects to multiply and leaves the treacherous with nothing to fear. ### Six in the Fourth Place: Regret vanishes. In the hunt, game is taken for three uses.[^j08-three-portions]

### Nine in the Fifth Place: Steadfastness brings good fortune; regret vanishes. Nothing is unfavorable. No beginning, but an ending. Three days before geng, three days after geng: good fortune.[^j08-geng-days]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4629:043 -->

**Original Meaning.** The fifth nine is firm, vigorous, centered, and correct, but belongs to Xun, so there is regret. Because it possesses steadfastness, there is good fortune; thus regret vanishes and nothing is unfavorable. Having regret is “no beginning”; its disappearance is “an ending.” Geng means changing: an alteration in affairs. Three days before geng is ding; three days after geng is gui. Ding points to earnest, repeated instruction before the change; gui points to deliberation and assessment after the change. One who is making a change and obtains this prognostication meets good fortune by acting in this way.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4629:044 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4629:045 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The fifth occupies the honored position and governs Penetration: it is the source of commands. Centered and correct, it exhausts the goodness of Xun. Yet Xun is the Way of yielding compliance, whose benefit lies in steadfastness. This warning does not arise from a deficiency in the fifth; it is appropriate within Xun. With steadfastness comes good fortune, regret vanishes, and nothing is unfavorable. Steadfastness means correctness and centeredness. In dealing with Penetration and issuing commands, both make for good fortune. Yielding deference without steadfastness brings regret; how could nothing then be unfavorable? A command is issued to change something. “No beginning” means that the beginning was not good; “an ending” means that only after change does it become good. If it were already good, what need would there be for commands or change? “Three days before geng, three days after geng: good fortune”—this is how commands and alterations should be undertaken. Jia is the beginning of an affair; geng is the beginning of alteration. Among the ten stems, wu and ji occupy the middle. Passing the middle brings change; hence geng. In altering affairs, one should trace the beginning and bring the ending to completion, as in the meaning of the days before and after jia. In this way there is good fortune. The explanation is given under Decay.

Before geng means reiterating the command; after geng means carrying out affairs after its issue.

## 58. Dui 兌 — Joy

<!-- Source: https://www.eee-learning.com/book/4630 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4630:001 -->

![Dui: Dui below, Dui above](../assets/eee-learning/1870eca748d0-yi58b.png) **Dui 兌 — Joy** — Dui below; Dui above.[^j08-joy]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4630:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4630:002 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Concerning Joy, the *Sequence of the Hexagrams* says: “Xun means entering. After entering, things find joy; therefore Penetration is followed by Joy. Dui means joy.” When things enter into one another, they delight in one another; delighting in one another, they enter into one another. This is why Joy follows Penetration.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4630:002 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4630:003 -->

### Joy: success. Steadfastness is beneficial.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4630:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4630:004 -->

**Original Meaning.** Dui means joy. One yin advances above two yang: gladness made visible outwardly. Its image is the lake, taking both its delighting the myriad things and the image of Kan's water with its downward outlet stopped. The hexagram is firm at the center and yielding outside. Firm centeredness gives joy and success; outward yieldingness makes steadfastness beneficial. Joy has a Way of success, yet wrongful pleasure cannot go unwarned. Hence this prognostication. Alternatively, outward yieldingness gives joy and success, while firm centeredness makes steadfastness beneficial. This too is a possible interpretation.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4630:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4630:005 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Dui means joy. Joy is a Way of attaining free passage. If one can bring delight to others, all will delight in one and join with one: this suffices to bring success. Yet the Way of giving delight benefits from steadfast correctness. Seeking delight by what is not the Way becomes corrupt flattery and brings regret and blame. Hence the warning that steadfastness is beneficial.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4630:005 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4630:006 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4630:006 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4630:007 -->

**Jiao Hong:** When people feel gladness, it must become visible outwardly. Yang borrows yin's harmony and yieldingness for its expression; gladness does not originate in yin. Thus, where there are two yin and one yang, yang governs. Where there are two yang and one yin, yin is not the governing source but merely the means of yang's activity.[^j08-joy-governing]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4630:007 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4630:008 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** The earth holds accumulated moisture; when the spring energy arrives, moisture rises to the surface. The human body has blood; when yang energy flourishes, fullness spreads into the complexion. These are the meanings of Dui as lake and joy. Although joy takes form through yin, that which employs yin is yang. If someone has a gentle, harmonious disposition but does not exercise it with a loyal, upright heart, correctness is lost and wrongfulness entered. Hence steadfastness is beneficial.

Yang firmness prevents servility; the low position permits deference; the body of Joy permits harmony; absence of a correspondent prevents partiality. Handling joy in this way brings good fortune.

### Nine in the Second Place: Sincere joy. Good fortune; regret vanishes.[^j08-trust-joy]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4630:017 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4630:018 -->

**Original Meaning.** Firm centeredness gives sincerity; dwelling in a yin position gives regret. If the diviner’s joy is grounded in sincerity, good fortune follows and regret vanishes.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4630:018 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4630:019 -->

Yet the second possesses firm centered virtue, with sincerity and trustworthiness filling it inwardly. Although close to a petty person, it preserves itself without loss. The noble person is harmonious without merely agreeing. Taking joy without losing firm centeredness therefore brings good fortune and the disappearance of regret. Without the second's firm centeredness, there would be regret; preserving itself makes that regret vanish.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4630:020 -->

Yet the second is firm at the center, with sincere, substantial virtue filling it within. Thus, although it shares a trigram with the third, it is not liable to lose itself. This is why its regret vanishes.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4630:021 -->

Adjoining the yang below, it bends itself contrary to the Way and approaches them to seek delight: hence misfortune. Movement inward is called coming. Though yang lies both above and below, it goes only inward because it shares the same trigram and yin tends downward. It loses the Way and proceeds downward.

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4630:026 -->

With an incorrect disposition between two Joys, it caters to both sides, making ingratiation its sole concern. This is the petty person's loss of correctness; hence misfortune in Joy.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4630:027 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4630:028 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** The third belongs to the inner trigram, hence “coming.” Yet this does not mean coming to please the two yang below. As the governing line of joy, its purpose is fixed on delight: everything outside that can give pleasure comes and stirs it.

### Nine in the Fourth Place: Deliberating over joy, not yet at peace. Firmly reject what is harmful: there is happiness.[^j08-deliberation-ailment]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4630:029 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4630:030 -->

**Original Meaning.** Above, the fourth supports the centered correctness of the fifth nine; below, it adjoins the yielding wrongfulness of the third six. Thus it cannot decide, but deliberates over what should please it, without settling the matter. Yet its constitution is yang-firm, so it can stand firmly in correctness and detest yielding wrongfulness. In this way there is happiness. Such are the image and prognostication: a profound warning.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4630:030 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4630:031 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The fourth supports the centered, correct fifth above and adjoins the yielding, wrongful third below. Although yang-firm, its position is incorrect. The third is yielding yin, what yang delights in. Thus the fourth cannot decide and deliberates without peace: it considers whom to follow but remains undecided, unable to settle. *Jie* means the interval between two things, a dividing boundary. The graph for a boundary in land adds the field component but has the same meaning. Hence a person with principled integrity is called *jie*. If one firmly preserves correctness, detests wrongfulness, and keeps it at a distance, there will be happiness. Following the fifth is correct; delighting in the third is wrong. The fourth is close to the ruler. If it preserves correctness with firm integrity and keeps wrongfulness at a distance, it will gain the ruler's support to carry out the Way, with blessings reaching others: hence happiness. For one like the fourth, attainment and loss are not yet settled; they depend on whom it follows.

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4630:032 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4630:033 -->

**Yang Jian:** The nine is firm and the fourth position yielding. Close to the flattering petty person at the third six, it knows inwardly that this is wrong yet genuinely delights in the person's gentle flattery. Thus it deliberates over what pleases it, rejection and acceptance contending within its breast without peace. Here the sage encourages it: firmly detest the petty person's wrongdoing, and there will be happiness.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4630:033 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** In the *Changes*, “ailment” is regularly paired with “happiness”: “an illness without falsity—do not medicate it; there is happiness,” and “reduce the ailment; bring it quickly to happiness.” By comparison, “ailment” here means illness, and “happiness” means its departure. The fourth adjoins the third; hence “amid an ailment,” meaning situated amid wrongful and harmful influences. If it rests content and sinks into them, their poison will be great indeed. Only if it deliberates over what gives delight and does not rest content in whatever is pleasing will there be happiness, even amid an ailment. The *Analects* says: “The noble person is easy to serve but hard to please. Pleasing him contrary to the Way does not please him.” Is this not deliberating over joy?

### Nine in the Fifth Place: Trust in what strips away. There is danger.[^j08-trusting-erosion]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4630:035 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4630:036 -->

**Original Meaning.** “Strips away” means yin's ability to strip away yang. The fifth nine is yang-firm, centered, and correct. Yet it occupies the honored position in the time of Joy and is very close to the top six. The top six is yielding yin, governs joy, and occupies its utmost: it can strip away yang through wrongful pleasure. Thus the prognostication simply warns that trusting the top six brings danger.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4630:036 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4630:037 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The fifth nine occupies the honored position with centered correctness, exhausting the goodness of the Way of joy. Yet the sage again gives a warning of danger. Even amid the flourishing rule of Yao and Shun, vigilance was never absent: they guarded against what required guarding. Even with sages and worthies above, the world has never been without petty people; they merely do not dare to give their evil free rein. The sages also take pleasure in their forcing themselves to change outwardly. Such petty people have never failed to understand what can please sages and worthies. The Four Evils at Yao's court concealed their wrongdoing and obeyed commands. The sage was not unaware of their persistent evil; he accepted their fear of punishment and their effort to act humanely. If the fifth sincerely trusts a petty person's pretended goodness as real goodness, unaware of what is concealed, this is a dangerous course. When precautions against petty people are incomplete, they harm the good. The sage's warning is profound. “Stripping away” names the diminishing of yang: yin diminishes yang. It points to the top six, so trusting what strips away is dangerous. Because the fifth is in the time of Joy and very close to the top six, this warning is given. Even sage Shun feared clever words and a pleasing countenance. How could vigilance be unnecessary? Delight's power to mislead enters so easily, and is so formidable.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4630:037 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4630:038 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4630:038 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4630:039 -->

**Wang Bi:** Adjoining the top six and finding accord with it, one occupies an honored, correct position yet takes delight and places trust not in yang but in yin. This is the meaning of trusting what strips away. Stripping away means the growth of the petty person's Way.

Their apprehension is possible because they are firm, centered, and correct. Treading includes the meaning of vigilance and fear. Its fifth nine occupies the honored place, what is meant by “treading the imperial position without distress”; thus, amid decisive treading, it can remain constantly alert to danger. Joy involves delight; its fifth nine is honored and also adjoins the top six. Thus, through trusting what strips away, its heart likewise feels danger.[^j08-danger-awareness]

### Top Six: Drawn into joy.[^j08-drawn-joy]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4630:045 -->

**Original Meaning.** The top six is the line that completes joy. Yin at joy's utmost draws the two yang below to share its delight, but cannot be certain of their following. Thus the fifth nine must beware, while this line does not state good fortune or misfortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4630:046 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** In other hexagrams, reaching the utmost brings change. Dui is delight, and at its utmost delight only increases. The top six completes joy and occupies its extreme: delight without knowing when to stop. Thus, when delight has already reached its limit, it draws it out still further. Yet why does this not reach regret or blame? Because the statement speaks only of delight that does not know when to stop; it has not yet shown whether what gives delight is good or bad. Moreover, below it rests upon the fifth nine's centered correctness, leaving no opening for wrongful delight. The third six, by contrast, both supports and rests upon lines that are incorrectly placed, and therefore has misfortune.

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4630:048 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Both the third and top are yielding yin governing joy. “Coming for joy” means that external things come and move me: what the *Mencius* calls being obscured by things, and the *Record of Music* calls being moved through contact with things. “Drawn into joy” means that things draw me away: what the *Mencius* describes as things contacting things and simply drawing them along, and the *Record of Music* as things arriving and the person being transformed into things. Beginning with their coming and ending with their drawing, these are the shallower and deeper stages of the heart's movement into desire.

## 59. Huan 渙 — Dispersion

<!-- Source: https://www.eee-learning.com/book/4631 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4631:001 -->

![Huan: Kan below, Xun above](../assets/eee-learning/0e507d805898-yi59b.png) **Huan 渙 — Dispersion** — Kan below; Xun above.[^j08-dispersion]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4631:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4631:002 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Concerning Dispersion, the *Sequence of the Hexagrams* says: “Dui means joy. After joy comes dispersal; therefore Joy is followed by Dispersion.” Joy brings relaxation and release. A person's vital energy gathers and contracts in anxiety, but relaxes and disperses in joy. Thus joy includes a meaning of dispersal, and Dispersion follows Joy. The hexagram has Xun above and Kan below: wind travels over water. Meeting wind, water scatters and disperses. Hence Dispersion.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4631:002 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4631:003 -->

### Dispersion: success. The king comes to his ancestral temple. It is beneficial to cross the great river. Steadfastness is beneficial.[^j08-temple-boat]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4631:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4631:004 -->

**Original Meaning.** Huan means dispersing. Kan below and Xun above give wind traveling over water: an image of scattering and dissolution. Hence Dispersion. In transformation, it comes from Gradual Progress: the nine comes to occupy the second place and attains the center; the six goes to occupy the third, obtains the nine's former position, and joins with the fourth above. Thus its prognostication permits success. Moreover, because the ancestral spirits have dispersed, the king should come to the temple to gather them. Xun is wood and Kan water, an image of boats and oars; hence crossing the great river is beneficial. “Steadfastness is beneficial” is a profound warning to the diviner.[^j08-huan-transformation]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4631:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4631:005 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Huan means separation and dispersal. People's separation begins within: when their hearts part, they scatter. Remedying dispersal likewise rests upon the center. If one can gather people's hearts, the scattered can be brought together. Thus the hexagram's meaning centers throughout on what is within. “Steadfastness is beneficial”: the Way of reuniting what has scattered lies in correct firmness.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4631:005 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4631:006 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Dispersion stands opposite Gathering. Coming to the temple gathers spirits that have dispersed; crossing the river unites human efforts that are not yet coordinated. When utmost sincerity moves and reaches others, nothing in the unseen or visible realm fails to respond. When people of Qin and Yue share a boat, their hearts and efforts cannot but unite. These are two great means of seeking reunion amid dispersal. Yet without correctness, they necessarily become offenses against the spirits and reckless encounters with danger. Hence steadfastness is beneficial.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4631:006 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4631:007 -->

### First Six: Bring rescue with a strong horse. Good fortune.[^j08-huan-horse]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4631:007 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4631:008 -->

**Original Meaning.** At the beginning of the hexagram, dispersal is only beginning. To bring rescue at its beginning already requires little effort; with a strong horse as well, good fortune is evident. The first six does not itself possess the ability to remedy dispersal, but can comply with the second nine. Hence this image and prognostication.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4631:008 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4631:009 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The six occupies the beginning of the hexagram, the beginning of dispersal. Bringing rescue at its beginning and also obtaining a strong horse gives good fortune. Of the six lines, only the first does not say “dispersion.” The tendency toward separation should be discerned early: rescue at its very beginning prevents it from reaching dispersal. This teaching is profound. A horse is what a person entrusts himself to. Entrusting oneself to a strong horse enables one to remedy dispersal. The horse means the second. The second possesses firm centered ability; the first is yielding and compliant. Neither has a correspondent, so they seek each other through neighboring affinity. The first entrusts its yielding compliance to firm centered ability to rescue its situation. Like obtaining a strong horse for a long journey, this must bring assistance; hence good fortune. Rescue at dispersal's beginning takes little effort: the timing is favorable.[^j08-huan-readings]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4631:009 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4631:010 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4631:010 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4631:011 -->

**Wang Zongchuan:** At dispersal's beginning, the time has not yet become one of full dispersal. To follow the tendency of the situation and promptly rescue it is to find the right means of rescue. Thus only a strong horse brings good fortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4631:011 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4631:012 -->

**Hu Bingwen:** Five lines mention dispersion; the first alone does not. Rescue is still early enough to prevent dispersal.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4631:012 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4631:013 -->

### Nine in the Second Place: Amid dispersion, hasten to your supporting bench. Regret vanishes.[^j08-support-bench]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4631:013 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4631:014 -->

**Original Meaning.** A nine occupying the second place ought to have regret. Yet in the time of Dispersion, it comes without reaching an impasse and can make regret vanish. Hence this image and prognostication: the nine is the hastening, and the second place the supporting bench.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4631:014 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4631:015 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The lines all say “dispersion,” meaning the time of Dispersion. In a time of separation, dwelling amid danger plainly brings regret. If one can hurry toward a place of security, regret vanishes. A supporting bench is something one bends down to lean upon for ease. Bending down means approaching what is below; hastening means going urgently. Although the second and first do not formally correspond, in the time of separation neither has a partner. As neighboring yin and yang, they seek each other and become mutually dependent. Thus the second calls the first its supporting bench, while the first calls the second its horse. By urgently approaching the first to find security, the second can make its regret vanish. Although the first belongs to Kan, it is not at the center of danger. Someone may doubt whether the first's slight yieldingness could provide support; yet in Dispersion, joined efforts are what prevail. Earlier scholars all took the fifth as the bench, but that is wrong. In a time of separation, how could the two yang join? If they could, their work of remedying dispersal would be great, not merely enough to remove regret. A bench means bending down to find support.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4631:015 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4631:016 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4631:016 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4631:017 -->

**Guo Yong:** The second nine's firmness comes inward from outside and attains the center, giving the meaning of leaving danger for security. Hence the image of hastening to its supporting bench. Attaining the center and finding security are why the *Commentary on the Judgment* says it is not brought to an impasse.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4631:017 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4631:018 -->

**Recorded Conversations of Master Zhu.** In human affairs, the second nine's “amid dispersion, hasten to your supporting bench” means coming to a secure place.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4631:018 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4631:019 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** To gather what has scattered, first strengthen the root. Firm centeredness within is an image of strengthening the root. A bench is something to lean upon while seated. Only after obtaining support and dwelling securely can one act without being brought to an impasse.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4631:019 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4631:020 -->

### Six in the Third Place: Disperse one's self. No regret.[^j08-dispersing-self]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4631:020 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4631:021 -->

**Original Meaning.** Yielding yin without centeredness or correctness gives an image of self-partiality. Yet it occupies a yang position and sets its purpose on helping its time; it can disperse its private interests and thereby attain freedom from regret. Hence this prognostication. Broadly speaking, the four lines from here upward all remedy dispersion through dispersion itself.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4631:021 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4631:022 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** In the time of Dispersion, the third alone has a correspondent and partner, and so has no regret arising from dispersal. Yet with its yielding constitution and uncentered, incorrect capacity, and with its upper partner occupying a place without office, how could it remedy its time's dispersal and benefit others? It can merely keep its own person free of regret. “Dispersion” is placed first to mean that, in the time of Dispersion, its own person has no regret from dispersal.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4631:022 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4631:023 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4631:023 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4631:024 -->

**Wang Shenzi:** The four lines from here upward all use dispersion to remedy dispersion. This means dispersing what should be dispersed, so that what should not be dispersed can gather.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4631:024 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4631:025 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** In the *Changes*, the third six's correspondence with the top nine seldom has an auspicious meaning. Only in Dispersion does correspondence above image forgetting oneself in service to those above. Limping's second says, “The king's minister meets difficulty upon difficulty, not for his own sake.” That too is because it meets a time of difficulty while corresponding to the fifth. This line's meaning is the same.

### Six in the Fourth Place: Disperse one's faction. Supreme good fortune. Through dispersion comes a gathering like a hill—not what ordinary minds conceive.[^j08-group-hill]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4631:026 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4631:027 -->

**Original Meaning.** Yin occupies its proper place and supports the fifth nine above, bearing responsibility for remedying dispersion. Having no correspondent below, it images one able to disperse private factions. A diviner who acts thus attains the good fortune of great goodness. The statement further says that one can disperse a small group to form a great gathering, bringing the scattered together like a hill. This lies beyond ordinary people's thinking.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4631:027 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4631:028 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Dispersion's fourth and fifth lines depend on one another in meaning and are therefore discussed together. Thus the *Judgment* says, “joining with what is above.” The fourth is deferential, compliant, and correct in the great minister's place; the fifth is firm, centered, and correct in the ruler's place. Ruler and minister join forces, firmness and yieldingness assisting one another to rescue the world's dispersal. At a time of scattering, firmness alone cannot win affectionate allegiance, while yieldingness alone is insufficient to provide dependable support. The fourth assists the firm, centered, correct ruler through the correct Way of deference and compliance. Ruler and minister share the work, and so can remedy dispersion. To bring the scattered world together into a group is indeed the good fortune of great goodness. “Through dispersion comes a hill—not what ordinary minds conceive” is praise. A hill means a great accumulation. To achieve a great gathering amid dispersal is an immense accomplishment, a difficult undertaking, and a most subtle application. “Ordinary” means commonplace. It is not something commonplace insight can conceive. Who but the worthy and wise could act in this way?

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4631:028 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4631:029 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4631:029 -->

When people's hearts are dispersed, they each form private factions and cannot be unified. Only the fourth six can disperse petty people's private groups and establish the impartial Way of the whole world. This is why there is supreme good fortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4631:031 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4631:032 -->

**Chen Chen:** The world's dispersal commonly arises because people's hearts turn from those above and each binds itself to private interests. Once private factions are dispersed, the impartial Way prevails, and their combined strength rises as one, like a hill. This is what is meant by scattering small groups to form a great gathering. Yet only someone of outstanding ability and insight can do this; it is beyond ordinary people's thinking.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4631:032 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4631:033 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Kong Anguo's preface to the *Documents* says, “A hill means an accumulation.” Thus “hill” itself signifies gathering. “Through dispersion comes a hill—not what ordinary minds conceive” means that ordinary people know only dispersal as dispersal, not dispersal as gathering. Could a gathering within dispersal lie within ordinary thought? Some join factions as a means of strengthening themselves, but this is merely private collusion and attachment through power, not true union. When such groups scatter, they turn against and overthrow one another, becoming worse than if they had never gathered. Only impartiality makes the public Way sufficient to win people over; only freedom from wrongfulness makes correct principle sufficient to move the multitude. This is gathering within dispersal, which ministers who identify themselves with the state should understand.

### Nine in the Fifth Place: Disperse the great proclamation like sweat. Disperse the king's stores. No blame.[^j08-sweat-command][^j08-royal-stores]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4631:034 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4631:035 -->

**Original Meaning.** Yang firmness, centered and correct, occupies the honored position. In the time of Dispersion, one who can broadcast commands and distribute accumulated stores can remedy dispersal without blame. Hence this image and prognostication. The fifth nine belongs to Xun, giving the image of commands. Sweat means something that issues forth and does not return. “Disperse the king's stores” is like Lu Zhi's idea of scattering a small reserve to form a great reserve.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4631:035 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4631:036 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The fifth and fourth, ruler and minister, unite their virtues and handle dispersal through firm centered correctness and deferential compliance. They have found the Way. What remains is to permeate people's hearts, so that they follow willingly. Commands should reach the people's hearts as sweat spreads through all four limbs; then people will trust and obey. Thus one can remedy the world's dispersal, fittingly occupy the royal position, and remain without blame. The great proclamation means great governmental commands: the great mandate that renews the people, the great policies that rescue dispersal. “Dispersion” is said twice. The first occurrence means the time of Dispersion; the second means that handling Dispersion in this way brings no blame. The fourth has already spoken of supreme good fortune; the fifth speaks only of being equal to its position. The fourth and fifth are treated together because separation is Dispersion's harm, and its remedy is reunion. Without ruler and minister sharing their work and combining their strength, how could it be remedied? Their meanings depend on each other because that is what the time requires.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4631:036 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4631:037 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4631:037 -->

**Hu Yuan:** Sweat issues through the skin's openings; its emergence releases obstruction and cures illness. Correctly placed and centered, it can issue commands and spread its beneficence, releasing the world's blockages and opening its pent-up stagnation. Everyone then trusts those above and finds someone to turn to. Thus it occupies its position without regret or blame.

The ruler's commands should issue from the center of his own heart, passing from within to without, from near to far, until even the most hidden and distant places are reached. This is like the body's sweat, issuing from within and permeating all four limbs.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4631:039 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4631:040 -->

**Yu Yan:** What disperses a person's illness and enables recovery is sweat. What disperses the world's difficulties and enables recovery is commands. “The royal dwelling” means the position the king occupies.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4631:040 -->

**He Kai:** The king takes the whole world as a single body. To spread sweat throughout that body, there must be a great proclamation that begins anew together with the world. Whenever a great command descends, a great policy is promulgated, or great resources are released, its dispersal is like a harmonious wind and its moisture like timely rain. **Editorial Judgment.** Throughout the *Changes*, “call” 號 should be read in the level tone, as in crying out. In an ordinary person, it is urgent grief pouring forth feeling and opening the heart. In a king, it is utmost, earnest sincerity issuing proclamations and commands. In “dispersion—the king occupies,” a pause should come after “dispersion.” The meaning is that his great call is like the dispersal of sweat, sufficient to open blockages between above and below and restore the vital energy of the whole body. Then, even in the time of Dispersion, one who occupies the situation as a king must be without blame.

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4631:043 -->

### Top Nine: Disperse the bloodshed; depart and withdraw far away. No blame.[^j08-blood-distance]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4631:043 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4631:044 -->

**Original Meaning.** Yang occupies the utmost of Dispersion and can emerge from it. Hence this image and prognostication. Blood means injury and harm. “Far away” should be written “fear,” as in Small Restraint's fourth six. The meaning is: disperse the bloodshed, and it goes; disperse the fear, and one comes out of it.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4631:044 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4631:045 -->

## 62. Xiaoguo 小過 — Small Excess

<!-- Source: https://www.eee-learning.com/book/4634 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4634:001 -->

![Xiaoguo: Gen below, Zhen above](../assets/eee-learning/6830fc2caaf7-yi62b.png) **Xiaoguo 小過 — Small Excess** — Gen below; Zhen above.[^j08-small-excess]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4634:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4634:002 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Concerning Small Excess, the *Sequence of the Hexagrams* says: “One who possesses trust must act upon it; therefore Inner Sincerity is followed by Small Excess.” What people trust, they must put into practice; action brings going beyond. This is why Small Excess follows Inner Sincerity. The hexagram has thunder above a mountain. Thunder resounds high above, its sound exceeding the ordinary; hence Small Excess. Moreover, yin occupies the honored position, while yang has lost its fitting place and is not centered: the small exceeds its ordinary measure. Thus it means the small exceeding, excess in small affairs, and an excess that is slight.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4634:002 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4634:003 -->

### Small Excess: success. Steadfastness is beneficial. Small affairs may be undertaken, not great affairs. A flying bird leaves its cry: it is not fitting to rise, but fitting to descend. Great good fortune.[^j08-bird-cry]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4634:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4634:004 -->

**Original Meaning.** “Small” means yin. The hexagram has four yin outside and two yang within. Yin outnumbers yang: the small exceeds. Having exceeded yang, it can attain success. Yet steadfastness must be preserved; a warning is therefore indispensable. The second and fifth both attain the center with yieldingness, so small affairs are possible. The third and fourth both have firmness without fitting position or centeredness, so great affairs are not possible. The hexagram is solid within and open outside, like a bird in flight. Its sound descends rather than ascends. Thus one who can realize the response imaged by the flying bird's lingering cry should descend and attain great good fortune. This belongs to the same principle as not undertaking great affairs.[^j08-xiaoguo-position]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4634:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4634:005 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Excess means going beyond the ordinary. When one straightens what is crooked by going beyond the straight, the excess is a means of reaching correctness. Affairs sometimes require this: there are cases where passage becomes possible only through going beyond. Thus Small Excess has its own meaning of success. “Steadfastness is beneficial” means that the Way of going beyond benefits from correctness. Not missing what the time requires is correctness; the excess seeks to reach the mean. What one exceeds in are small affairs. How could great affairs admit excess? This is discussed fully under Great Excess. “A flying bird leaves its cry” means that the passing is not far. “Not fitting to rise, but fitting to descend” means that compliance is fitting. Compliance brings great good fortune. Going beyond in order to approach this is following principle. When excess follows principle, its good fortune must be great.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4634:005 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4634:006 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4634:006 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4634:007 -->

**Wang Bi:** The flying bird leaves its cry, plaintively seeking a resting place. Higher up, it increasingly finds nowhere suitable; below, it finds security. Nothing illustrates increasing exhaustion through increasing ascent better than a flying bird.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4634:007 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4634:008 -->

**Kong Yingda:** Exceeding in small affairs is called Small Excess, as in conduct exceeding in respect or mourning exceeding in grief. Master Chu says that petty people's conduct contains slight deviations, and the noble person corrects them through conduct of exceptional generosity, like Yanzi's fox-fur robe. Going beyond in small matters allows the Way to find passage; hence “Small Excess: success.” “Steadfastness is beneficial” means that, in correcting the age and encouraging its customs, benefit lies in returning to correctness. “Small affairs may be undertaken, not great affairs”: slight deviations can be corrected only through small measures, not rectified through great undertakings. The flying bird's plaintive cry seeks a resting place. Going excessively upward increasingly leaves it nowhere to rest; going excessively downward does not deprive it of security. Similarly, when a noble person meets a time of deviation and acts to correct it by going beyond, compliance means preserving humility and remaining below, while defiance means affronting the ruler and overstepping those above. Thus compliance and defiance are compared to the bird's descent and ascent.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4634:008 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4634:009 -->

**Lü Dalin:** Small Excess is excess in what is small. The noble person's Way remedies deficiencies so as to meet the mean. Great Excess remedies great deficiencies; Small Excess remedies small ones. Such remedy is what brings success. For the flying bird, ascent is unfitting and descent fitting: above lies exhaustion, below a resting place. Excessive extravagance and arrogance bring misfortune—ascending is not fitting. Excessive respect and frugality bring good fortune—descending is fitting.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4634:009 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4634:010 -->

**Recorded Conversations of Master Zhu.** Small Excess is excess in such things as kindness and generosity. Great Excess has the bearing of firmness, strictness, resolution, and fortitude. Small Excess means both excess in small affairs and exceeding in the direction of the small: “in conduct, exceeding in respect; in mourning, exceeding in grief; in expenditure, exceeding in frugality.” All are excess in what is small, the meaning of taking a step back and lowering oneself.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4634:010 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4634:011 -->

**Yu Yan:** In the time of Small Excess, what may be exceeded concerns small affairs alone; great affairs admit no such excess.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4634:011 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4634:012 -->

**Lin Xiyuan:** Small Excess should not be understood in terms of classes of people, but classes of affairs. Consider the *Original Meaning* on the Great Image: “These three excesses are all excesses of the small. One may exceed in small matters, not great ones; one may go slightly beyond, not excessively beyond.” It further says, “The Judgment's small affairs that may be undertaken, and its fitting descent.” The intention is clear. “Small Excess: success” means success through going beyond in small affairs. “Steadfastness is beneficial” is a profound warning to the diviner. “Small affairs, not great affairs” and “not fitting to rise, but fitting to descend” further develop the meaning of beneficial steadfastness.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4634:012 -->

In the cycles of order and disorder, Peace and Obstruction are the two poles, After Completion and Before Completion their transitions. After Completion belongs after Peace and before Obstruction; Before Completion belongs before Peace and after Obstruction. Peace resembles summer; Obstruction, winter; Before Completion, spring; After Completion, autumn. Thus in the Earlier Heaven diagram, Qian and Kun occupy south and north as its two poles, while Li and Kan occupy east and west as their transitions. After Completion's meaning is less favorable than Peace because it is peace about to become obstruction. Before Completion's meaning is more favorable than Obstruction because it is obstruction about to become peace. Thus After Completion's “good fortune at the beginning; disorder at the end” is the same warning as Peace's “the wall falls back into the moat.” Before Completion's “almost across, wetting the tail, nothing beneficial” is the same concern as Obstruction's “Will it perish? Will it perish?”[^j08-completion-seasons][^j08-jiji-readings]

**Editorial Judgment.** Li has grasped the statement's meaning. Dragging the wheels is intentional; wetting the tail is not. How could it then intend to seek out and employ worthy talent below? Thus the second cannot carry out its action. At such a time, firm centeredness turns into inner self-satisfaction, and Kan and Li become opposed. Only one who recognizes the time and understands change can speak of the *Changes*. The second is yin, hence a woman. A screen is what shields a woman when she goes out; without it, she cannot proceed. ## 64. Weiji 未濟 — Before Completion

<!-- Source: https://www.eee-learning.com/book/4636 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4636:001 -->

![Weiji: Kan below, Li above](../assets/eee-learning/37e8e9a034a6-yi64b.png) **Weiji 未濟 — Before Completion** — Kan below; Li above.[^j08-before-completion]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4636:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4636:002 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Concerning Before Completion, the *Sequence of the Hexagrams* says: “Things cannot be exhausted; therefore it concludes with Before Completion.” After Completion is the exhaustion of things. If things reached exhaustion without changing, nothing could continue unceasingly. The *Changes* is change without exhaustion. Thus After Completion is followed by Before Completion, with which the book ends. Not yet completed means not yet exhausted; not yet exhausted contains the meaning of generation without end. The hexagram has Li above and Kan below. Fire above water does not join it in useful activity; hence Before Completion.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4636:002 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4636:003 -->

### Before Completion: success. The little fox is almost across, but wets its tail. Nothing is beneficial.[^j08-little-fox]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4636:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4636:004 -->

**Original Meaning.** Before Completion is the time when affairs are not yet accomplished. Water and fire do not interact or serve one another, and all six lines are out of their proper positions. <!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4636:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4636:005 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The time Before Completion contains a principle of success, and the hexagram's capacities also contain a Way of attaining it. Everything depends on careful handling. A fox can cross water, but cannot complete the crossing with its tail wet. An old fox is full of doubt and caution, listening as it treads on ice for fear of breaking through. A young one has not yet learned fear and care, and so boldly undertakes the crossing. *Qi* should read *yi*, describing bold strength, as in the *Documents*' “stout and valiant warriors.” The little fox is rashly determined to cross, wets its tail, and fails to get across. In seeking completion before it has been attained, utmost caution brings success. Rashness like the little fox's prevents completion. Unable to complete it, one gains no benefit.

Before Completion is a time when the ruler's Way faces difficulty. The fifth occupies the ruler's place with yieldingness, while the second possesses yang-firm ability in the corresponding place and is called to service. Without a Way of crossing or means of emerging from danger, advancing brings misfortune. Yet Before Completion contains a Way of completion, and danger at its end contains a principle of emerging from danger. Above lies a yang-firm correspondent. If it can traverse danger and go to follow that correspondent, it will get across. Hence crossing the great river is beneficial. Yet how could the third's yieldingness emerge from danger and go? <!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4636:025 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4636:026 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** This line's meaning is especially difficult to elucidate. The meeting of upper and lower trigrams includes a meaning of crossing. After Completion's third is firm and therefore can cross; Before Completion's third is yielding and therefore cannot yet cross. The *Commentary* says: “Its yieldingness is endangered; might its firmness be equal to the task?”[^j08-firmness-question] This is visible in these two lines. Moreover, none of the other line statements in After Completion or Before Completion names its hexagram; only this line says “before completion.” Elsewhere, completion or incompletion belongs to the time, which one simply follows. Here, the time permits completion but the line cannot accomplish it: incompletion lies in oneself, not in the time. Thus “before completion” exposes its failure to meet the time. Lacking the ability to cross, it meets misfortune in advancing; possessing a heart of fear and caution, it benefits from crossing the great river. One cannot cross the great river lightly; remaining not yet across does no harm. In this way, the sage warns against missing the time while also requiring careful judgment in meeting it.[^j08-weiji-readings]

### Nine in the Fourth Place: Steadfastness brings good fortune; regret vanishes. Rouse your strength to attack the Guifang. In three years, rewards from the great state.[^j08-campaign-rewards]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4636:027 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4636:028 -->

**Original Meaning.** A nine in the fourth place is incorrect and has regret. If it can strive for steadfast correctness, regret vanishes. Yet with an unsteady constitution, striving for steadfastness requires bringing yang firmness to its utmost and exerting effort over a long time. Hence the image of attacking the Guifang and receiving rewards after three years.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4636:028 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4636:029 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The fourth nine has yang firmness in the great minister's place, with a ruler above whose open center is clear and compliant. It has also emerged from danger, and incompletion has passed its middle: there is a Way of accomplishing the crossing. The world's difficulties cannot be overcome without firm, vigorous ability. Although the nine is yang, it occupies the fourth place; hence the warning that steadfast firmness brings good fortune and makes regret vanish. Without steadfastness it cannot accomplish the task and will have regret. “Rouse” is the utmost of movement. Among the ancients' most strenuous exertions was the campaign against the Guifang, and so that undertaking supplies the meaning. Only after diligent effort on a distant campaign, extending to three years, does achievement arrive and the great state's reward follow. Such effort is necessary for completion. The Way of helping the world should be this steadfast and firm. Because the fourth occupies yieldingness, this warning is established.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4636:029 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4636:030 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4636:030 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4636:031 -->

**Yu Yan:** “Rouse your strength to attack the Guifang” means striking awe into them. The *Odes*, in “Seasonal Progress,” says, “We rouse their awe; none fails to tremble.” Its “rouse” has the same meaning as here.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4636:031 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4636:032 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** This attack on the Guifang is the same as After Completion's, but differs by one position. “In three years he conquers them” means conquest already achieved. “Rouse your strength to attack the Guifang” means the campaign is underway. “In three years, rewards from the great state” means that during those three years, rewards honoring the armies never cease, not that rewards are assessed only after the affair is settled. It resembles The Army's “the king confers commands three times,” not The Army's “the great ruler issues a mandate.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4636:032 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4636:033 -->

**Further Editorial Judgment.** The third and fourth are not rulers' positions, yet Gaozong's affairs are used because the *Changes* sometimes discusses the time without discussing rank. Thus Peace considers the cycle of level ground and slopes and favors steadfastness amid difficulty; Revolution considers the Way of change and favors changing the mandate. Both speak through the timing and meaning at the meeting of upper and lower trigrams.

### Six in the Fifth Place: Steadfastness brings good fortune. No regret. The noble person's radiance is sincere. Good fortune.[^j08-radiant-trust]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4636:034 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4636:035 -->

**Original Meaning.** A six in the fifth place is also incorrectly placed. Yet it governs cultivated clarity, occupies the center, and responds to firmness. Opening its heart to seek assistance below, it attains correctness and thus good fortune without regret. Moreover, its abundant radiance is trustworthy and real, without falsity. Good fortune upon good fortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4636:035 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4636:036 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The fifth governs cultivated clarity, occupies firmness, responds to firmness, and attains the center. It opens its heart and receives yang's assistance. Although yieldingness occupies the honored position, its handling is utterly correct and good, lacking nothing. Having attained steadfast correctness, it has good fortune without regret. Correctness is already its own, not merely a warning. With this, nothing remains uncrossed. Because the fifth governs cultivated clarity, its radiance is praised. The noble person's virtue shines abundantly, and the substance of his achievement answers to it: this is sincerity. The first good fortune arises from correctness. Yielding yet able to be correct, it has the good fortune of virtue. The final good fortune arises from achievement. Radiant and sincere, it meets a time when completion is possible.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4636:036 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4636:037 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4636:037 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4636:038 -->

**Yang Wanli:** Why does the fifth six shine in a world of incompletion? The summer sun grows hotter under cloud; a fire burns more fiercely when banked at night. The fifth six turns incompletion into completion. What further doubt could there be about the abundance of its cultivated clarity?

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4636:038 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4636:039 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Where the *Changes* has both “regret vanishes” and “no regret,” regret must first vanish before there can be no regret. No regret advances beyond the disappearance of regret. The hexagrams that state these consecutively in the fourth and fifth lines are Influence, Great Strength, and this one. From the lower trigram to the upper, affairs have passed their middle and move toward completion. In terms of Gaozong, the fourth is his vigorous campaign against Jing and Chu, and the fifth perhaps his establishing good order and peace in the state of Yin. Whatever passes from obscurity into light, from stripping away into renewed life, or from disorder into order must shine with more than ordinary radiance. One may see this in sunlight after rain or the colors of a mountain after fire.[^j08-weiji-readings]

### Top Nine: With sincerity, one drinks wine. No blame. If the head is wetted, sincerity loses its right course.[^j08-wine-wet-head]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4636:040 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4636:041 -->

**Original Meaning.** Firmness and clarity occupy Before Completion's utmost. The time is approaching when action will be possible, and one trusts and sustains oneself while awaiting the mandate: the Way of no blame. If, however, one gives oneself free rein without returning, like a fox crossing water and wetting its head, self-confidence becomes excessive and loses its rightness.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4636:041 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4636:042 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The nine is firm at the top: firmness at its utmost. It is above clarity: clarity at its utmost. Extreme firmness joined to clarity becomes not impetuosity but resolution. Clarity illuminates principle; firmness decides what is right. Yet at Before Completion's utmost, it is not in a position to achieve completion and has no means of doing so. It should therefore delight in Heaven and comply with the mandate. At Obstruction's end there is overthrow, because the time changes. Before Completion, however, contains no principle by which reaching its limit automatically completes the crossing. Thus this is simply incompletion at its utmost. With utmost sincerity, resting content in rightness and the mandate and taking one's joy therein, one may be without blame. Drinking wine means taking one's joy. Discontent with one's situation would bring anger, agitation, and panic, leading into misfortune and blame. But indulging in pleasure beyond ritual measure until the head is wet is likewise not resting at ease in one's situation. Sincerity means self-trust within; “loses its right course” means losing what is fitting. In such a case, one's self-trust has gone astray. Someone who, amid difficulty, recognizes that nothing can be done and therefore gives desire free rein without restraint—could that be someone at ease in rightness and the mandate?

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4636:042 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4636:043 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4636:043 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4636:044 -->

**Liu Mu:** After Completion has yieldingness above: stopping leads to disorder, hence the danger of wetting the head. Before Completion has firmness above: exhaustion opens into passage, hence sincerity in drinking wine without blame.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4636:044 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4636:045 -->

**Shi Jie:** The top nine possesses the virtue of firmness and clarity, and therefore sincerity within. At Before Completion's end, the ending turns again toward completion, so it may drink wine and take joy. If joy forgets its limits and wets the head again, however, even sincerity must fail at this point. This is a warning.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4636:045 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4636:046 -->

**Qiu Fuguo:** Having said that drinking wine is blameless, why speak again of drinking until the head is wet? Drinking wine is permissible; indulging until the head is wet means that what formerly possessed sincerity has now lost it here.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4636:046 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4636:047 -->

**Li Jian:** At Before Completion's end, just as completion is reached, wetting the head is again warned against. Apprehension from beginning to end, with freedom from blame as the essential aim: this is what is called the Way of the *Changes*.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4636:047 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4636:048 -->

**General Discussion — Zheng Ruxie:** After Completion has good fortune at the beginning and disorder at the end; Before Completion has disorder at the beginning and good fortune at the end. In the hexagrams' structures, After Completion leaves clarity for danger, while Before Completion leaves danger for clarity. In their meanings, what is completed at the beginning must become disordered at the end, and what is disordered at the beginning must become completed at the end. Heaven's Way and the principles of things are naturally so.[^j08-ending-open]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4636:048 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4636:049 -->

**Qiu Fuguo:** The three inner lines are Kan's danger. The first speaks of the humiliation of a wet tail; the second of steadfastness in dragging the wheels; the third warns of misfortune in advancing and an improper position. All concern incompletion. The three outer lines are Li's clarity. The fourth speaks of attacking the Guifang and receiving rewards; the fifth of the noble person's radiance and sincerity; the top of drinking wine without blame. Thus Before Completion becomes After Completion.

**Wan Shan:** Peace transforms into After Completion, and Obstruction into Before Completion. After Completion proceeds from Peace toward Obstruction; Before Completion proceeds from Obstruction toward Peace. Thus After Completion's line statements contain no good fortune, because they move toward Obstruction; Before Completion's contain much good fortune, because they move toward Peace. Obstruction and Peace express the opposing principles of disorder and order. After Completion and Before Completion are the gradual changes between them.

**Wu Yueshen:** The meaning of *yi*, the *Changes*, includes what does not change, reciprocal exchange, and transformation. The purity of Qian and Kun is what does not change; After Completion and Before Completion are reciprocal exchange and transformation. Beginning and ending with these is the great meaning of the *Changes*.

## Translator's Endnotes

The base is the repository's [Chinese Juan Eight](../source/juan-08.md). “Parallel text” means the *Siku Quanshu* transcription at [Wikisource, fixed revision 528569](https://zh.wikisource.org/w/index.php?title=御纂周易折中_(四庫全書本)/卷08&oldid=528569), checked on September 26, 2026. Historical page images linked by 易學網 were also inspected at selected passages; the consequential readings are cited below. This is selective comparison, not a systematic collation of historical leaves. Agreement between transcriptions does not establish independent textual authority. The Chinese source has not been changed. Attested corrections, alternative transmissions, historical conjectures, and translation choices are distinguished in the notes. “Editorial Judgment” always denotes the imperial compilers, never the translator.

[^j08-wanderer]: **Sojourning, not a romantic ideal of wandering.** 旅 concerns a person away from a settled home and the relationships and resources that make such a condition bearable. The text extends this to office, service, and proper conduct without erasing the concrete traveler. As throughout the project, lines are numbered from the bottom: “six” designates yin and “nine” yang. “Centered” ordinarily means the second or fifth place; positional correctness ordinarily means yang in an odd place or yin in an even place. Ethical correctness and the prognostic verdict cannot, however, be inferred from position alone.

[^j08-small-success]: **Small success and the wanderer's correctness.** 小亨 is limited success, not unrestricted flourishing. Zhu explicitly rejects suspending proper conduct because a sojourn is temporary. 旅貞 is therefore rendered as the steadfastness appropriate to the wanderer's circumstances, not the proposition that staying permanently abroad is auspicious. The text's assertions about the Way are the commentators' claims, not empirical guarantees of an oracle's accuracy.

[^j08-menial-reading]: **斯 and 㒋.** The first-line oracle can read 斯 as “this,” while the quoted annotation uses it in the sense “menial.” Wang Yinglin reports Guo Jing's proposed substitution of 㒋 and argues that 斯 can already carry the required lowly meaning. Wang Fusi is Wang Bi, referred to by his courtesy name. The translation preserves the grammatical reading in the oracle and the low-status reading in the commentary, rather than silently replacing the character. The isolated 㒋 before the next hexagram is separately noted below.

[^j08-servants]: **Young servant, attendants, and loyalty.** 童僕 can be read as a young servant; Cheng instead distinguishes the young and weak serving within from stronger attendants serving outside. Both explanations are retained. 貞 here may describe their steadfast loyalty, not a separate instruction to cast an oracle. The social hierarchy belongs to the historical text; “servant” has not been softened into “friend,” “inner child,” or “personal resource.”

[^j08-traveler-third]: **Loss of a servant or of the servant's loyalty.** Zhu explicitly attaches 貞 to 厲, producing “steadfastness brings danger,” and understands the servant to be lost outright. Cheng speaks of losing the servant's 貞, loyalty, and then expounds danger. The oracle follows the base's separation of these clauses; Cheng's different construction is visible in his translation. This is a disagreement about syntax and circumstance, not merely a stylistic difference.

[^j08-axe]: **Resources and axe.** 資 supplies or resources and 斧 an axe remain concrete in the oracle. Cheng broadens them to financial means and useful equipment; the compilers emphasize an axe for protection in a precarious lodging. Penetration's final line later repeats 資斧, but the compilers propose 齊斧, an “ordering axe,” there. Their proposed distinction is not retroactively imposed on this passage.

[^j08-pheasant]: **A lost arrow or a successful shot.** Zhu reads 一矢亡 as the loss or expenditure of one arrow, a small initial disadvantage followed by praise and appointment. Cheng and Wang Shenzi read a single shot that brings down the pheasant. Their differing accounts are preserved. The quoted reference to Qin in Zhu's recorded conversation supports his lexical point about an arrow being lost, not a separate divination procedure. 譽命 joins favorable reputation to an appointment, commission, or honor conferred above; it is not simply inward self-esteem.

[^j08-wanderer-five]: **A fifth line need not be a king.** Cheng expressly declines to assign the ruler's role here: a ruler wandering would have lost his position. The compilers connect the pheasant with presentation gifts carried by men seeking recognition. Their reading preserves the relation between personal virtue, social recognition, and appointment. It does not turn the line into a literal instruction to hunt a pheasant before seeking employment.

[^j08-lost-ox]: **Carelessness and compliance.** The ox's traditional compliance underlies the commentaries' moral reading. Here 易 is treated as heedlessness or taking things lightly; the alternative “boundary” reading discussed under Great Strength in [Juan Five](juan-05.md#eee-4606) should not be silently substituted. Cheng also carefully distinguishes the bird's burned nest from the wanderer's laughter and tears: he explains why the text names the wanderer again.

[^j08-stray-si]: **An orphan character in the base.** The source page for Penetration begins with the isolated graph 㒋 before its numbered caption. It is included as a separate aligned block, with the brief gloss “servant.” It is not part of Penetration's oracle and is absent at this point in the parallel transcription. Its relation to the preceding discussion of 斯/㒋 makes carryover from that discussion plausible, but that origin has not been established. Retaining and labeling it is preferable to silently dropping material from the selected edition.

[^j08-xun-title]: **Why “Penetration.”** Xun includes entering, deference, and compliant access, but Cai Qing insists that “compliance” alone does not exhaust it. The compilers emphasize examining what is concealed and carrying understanding into decisive action. “Penetration” leaves this force audible without making every occurrence mean physical entry. 巽 and 順 remain distinguishable in the English even where a commentator closely associates them.

[^j08-entering-yang]: **Which principle enters?** Zhu explains one yin entering beneath two yang. Zhao Rumei, He Kai, and the compilers instead emphasize yang penetrating and dispersing concealed yin. The latter develop natural, psychological, and political analogies. These are different constructions of the figure, not a single diagrammatic explanation repeated in different words. “Governing” a hexagram may concern its defining structure, its effective agency, or its exemplary line; those uses must be read in context.

[^j08-xun-readings]: **Attested corrections in Penetration.** In the compilers' explanation of the Judgment, the base's 陰性疑滯 is read with the parallel text as 陰性凝滯, “yin's nature congeals and stagnates”; 窮陰伏 is likewise read as 窮隱伏, “probing what lies concealed.” In Wang Bi's fourth-line gloss, 以欺行命 is corrected to the parallel's 以斯行命, “carrying out commands in this way.” The first correction concerns a physical and cosmological description, not a charge that yin is psychologically doubtful; the last prevents a spurious recommendation to execute commands deceptively. These corrections affect the English only.

[^j08-warrior]: **The warrior's steadiness.** 武人之貞 supplies the decisiveness lacking in the first line's wavering deference. Wang Bi stresses obedience to an issued command; Cheng and Zhu stress a remedy for personal indecision. The phrase does not make martial force the general rule for every line of Xun: firmness must be appropriate to the situation, and the fifth line joins decision to careful consideration.

[^j08-under-bed]: **Two very different beds.** For Cheng and Zhu, being beneath the bed pictures humility carried beyond the usual place of rest or security. In the compilers' reading, the bed conceals malign influences and entering beneath it is searching them out. Their favorable interpretation of the second line therefore rests on thorough investigation followed by action, not on remaining indefinitely submissive. The top line repeats the image but loses the axe: investigation or deference without the power of decision has become disabling.

[^j08-scribes-mediums]: **史 and 巫.** “Scribes” here denotes ritual officers, not merely copyists; “spirit-mediums” preserves the explicitly religious function assigned to 巫. Feng Yi distinguishes their respective duties in divination and rites of purification or averting harm. 紛若 suggests profusion or repeated, elaborate activity. The compilers carry this ritual image into the reiteration of commands and the detailed execution of affairs. The supernatural language belongs to the commentary and has not been replaced by a modern psychological diagnosis.

[^j08-repeated-penetration]: **Repetition as failure or indecision.** Zhu and Cheng explain 頻巽 as repeated attempts at deference that repeatedly lapse because of excessive rigidity. Zhao Rumei gives the simpler sense of doing it again. The compilers instead emphasize repeated consideration without decision, pairing the third with the top line. Their quotation “thinking twice is sufficient” limits indecision; it does not forbid reflection. The oracle's compressed expression is left broad enough for these competing explanations.

[^j08-three-portions]: **Three uses of the hunting catch.** Zhu divides the catch between dried meat for sacrificial vessels, guests, and the kitchen. Cheng combines guests and kitchen into one portion and adds a distribution to footmen and charioteers. Both lists have three parts, but they are not the same list. 乾豆 is rendered by its ritual purpose rather than as “dry beans”: 豆 here is a vessel. The wider commentarial analogy concerns beneficial action reaching different ranks of society.

[^j08-geng-days]: **Before and after geng.** 庚 is the seventh of the ten celestial stems. Counting three places back gives 丁, ding; three forward gives 癸, gui. Zhu connects these names with 丁寧, earnest reiteration, and 揆度, deliberative assessment. Cheng instead emphasizes tracing beginnings and endings, referring back to the 甲, jia, discussion in Decay, [Juan Three](juan-03.md#eee-4566). These are the commentators' explanatory word-associations. “No beginning, but an ending” means an unsatisfactory beginning brought to a satisfactory conclusion, not the literal absence of a beginning. The distinction between restoring ruined affairs under jia and adjusting existing affairs under geng is important to this compilation.

[^j08-xun-axe]: **Resources and axe, or an ordering axe.** The base oracle has 資斧, as does the parallel transcription. The compilers themselves report older texts with 齊斧 and prefer that reading here, connecting 齊 with setting things in order. Their claim about older witnesses is translated as their claim; it is not represented as a fresh collation of those witnesses. The main oracle retains the base's “resources and axe.” Zhu and Cheng both connect the axe with cutting through a matter and deciding it. Their unfavorable 貞 warns that persistence—or even an otherwise correct intention—cannot redeem a situation in which decisiveness has been lost.

[^j08-joy]: **Joy, delight, and pleasing others.** 說 is read here as 悅, delight, rather than ordinarily as “speaking.” Its range includes experiencing pleasure, giving pleasure, and seeking another's approval. The English varies accordingly while retaining “Joy” as the hexagram's title. Its lake image does not dispense with the requirement that delight be governed by correctness. Zhu offers two different ways of distributing the Judgment's phrases between outward yieldingness and inward firmness; neither is suppressed.

[^j08-joy-governing]: **The defining line and the source of activity.** Jiao Hong denies that the single yin line is the ultimate source of joy, making it the means through which yang acts. Elsewhere Zhu calls the third or top yin the line that makes Dui what it is. These statements need not use “governs” in the same sense: structural definition and effective agency are being distinguished. The compilers' account of blood, complexion, and spring moisture belongs to their cosmological explanation, not a modern physiological claim.

[^j08-harmonious-joy]: **Harmony without surrender of judgment.** 和 is related explicitly to the *Doctrine of the Mean*'s feelings arising in due measure. The first line's absence of correspondence is favorable because it prevents exclusive attachment. “Harmony” therefore does not mean indiscriminate agreement. The second line makes the same distinction through 和而不同, harmonious association without merely agreeing.

[^j08-trust-joy]: **Sincerity as protection from flattery.** In 孚兌, sincerity is the basis on which joy is experienced and expressed. Cheng and Wang Zongchuan do not deny the second's proximity to the third; they explain why inward firmness prevents that proximity from becoming self-loss. Gong Huan similarly stresses that someone whose pleasure rests on sincerity cannot be won over by wrongful pleasure. The second's favorable 孚 contrasts with the fifth's trust placed in a harmful object.

[^j08-coming-joy]: **What comes, and toward whom?** Zhu and Cheng read 來兌 as the third's approach to the two yang below. Wang Zongchuan reads it through the succession of the two Joy trigrams. The compilers instead make external sources of pleasure come and stir the heart. The last reading anticipates their explanation of 引兌 at the top: first objects arrive and affect one; eventually they draw one away. These changes of agency are preserved rather than reduced to a single “seeking happiness.”

[^j08-deliberation-ailment]: **介疾: rejecting harm or being amid an ailment.** Zhu and Cheng take 介 as firm integrity or a dividing boundary and 疾 as detesting evil. The compilers take 疾 as an ailment and 介 as being situated amid its harmful influence, comparing the disease-and-joy language in Without Falsity and Decrease. The oracle initially follows the principal commentaries; the editorial alternative remains explicit. In neither reading does 商, deliberation, recommend dwelling indefinitely in indecision: its object is a sound distinction concerning what gives delight.

[^j08-trusting-erosion]: **Trust can have the wrong object.** 剝 means stripping or wearing away and evokes the hexagram Splitting Apart. The danger lies not in sincerity as such but in trusting what gradually undermines firmness and integrity. The principal commentaries identify that influence with the top six; Cheng's lengthy political example concerns apparent reform that may conceal continued harmful intent. His distinction between outward conformity and inward transformation is a historical interpretation, not grounds for assuming hidden malice in a particular modern person.

[^j08-danger-awareness]: **Danger and apprehension.** The compilers insist that 厲 can include both a dangerous situation and the subject's awareness of its danger. Thus they read the fifth's centered correctness as enabling vigilance, rather than treating the warning as proof that those virtues have disappeared. Their comparison with Treading and Breakthrough is integral to the interpretation. “There is danger” in the oracle is not silently expanded into either a guaranteed disaster or a guarantee of safety.

[^j08-drawn-joy]: **Three readings of 引.** Zhu makes the top six draw the yang below into delight. Cheng reads it as prolonging delight beyond its proper limit. Liu Mu and the compilers describe being drawn away by alluring objects. The oracle's “drawn into joy” follows this last, explicitly supported reading, while the other commentaries retain their different agents and actions. The lack of an express verdict at the top is also retained; a commentator's inference of misfortune is not inserted into the oracle.

[^j08-dui-readings]: **A connective corrected.** Mao Pu's gloss has 放曰引 in the base; the parallel reads 故曰引, “hence ‘drawing.’” The English adopts 故. This is not a new imperative to release or abandon something. The source file is unchanged.

[^j08-dui-summary]: **The concluding summary is not a literal tally.** Jiang Tisheng calls the four yang lines fortunate and both yin lines unfortunate. The fourth actually promises happiness, the fifth warns of danger, and the top gives no express verdict. His statement summarizes his view of firmness and yieldingness in this hexagram; it is preserved as his generalization, not used to alter the individual line statements.

[^j08-dispersion]: **Dispersion as both problem and remedy.** 渙 can name the scattering of a community and the dispersal of whatever prevents its reunion. The upper lines develop this paradox explicitly: disperse private interests or factions, distribute stores, and remove danger so that a larger unity becomes possible. The English does not force every occurrence to mean either dissolution or reunion. The contrast with Gathering is interpretive and structural, not a claim that the two figures are identical.

[^j08-temple-boat]: **Temple and river.** The temple gathers the dispersed ancestral spirits; the shared crossing gathers otherwise divided human efforts. Qin and Yue serve as a conventional contrast between distant peoples, not as the proposed route of the diviner's journey. The compilers make both undertakings depend on sincerity and correctness. 王假有廟 is translated here as the king's coming to the ancestral temple; the commentary explains the further sense of reaching and gathering the ancestors through ritual.

[^j08-huan-transformation]: **Gradual Progress transformed.** In Zhu's account, the yang at Gradual Progress's third place moves to the second, while the yin at its second moves to the third. The resulting arrangement is Dispersion. The movement into the center is used to explain success and freedom from an impasse. This is a traditional derivation of the figure, not an instruction that the diviner must change those lines whenever this hexagram is obtained.

[^j08-huan-horse]: **Early rescue and the strong horse.** The first line is interpreted through reliance on the firm second line. Its yieldingness need not itself become the horse. Cheng and Hu Bingwen explain the absence of 渙 from this line statement by the possibility of preventing full dispersal through early action. This differs from repairing a dissolution that is already complete.

[^j08-huan-readings]: **託, not 脫.** Cheng's first-line commentary has 馬，人之所脫也 in the base. The parallel reads 馬人之所託也, “a horse is what a person entrusts himself to.” The following sentence, 託於壯馬, supports that reading. The English adopts 託; it does not interpret the passage as removing or escaping a horse.

[^j08-support-bench]: **Where is the support?** 机 is a low support to lean upon, rendered “supporting bench,” not a modern machine or opportunity. Zhu associates the hastening with the nine and the secure support with its second position after transformation. Cheng identifies the support with the first six and expressly rejects the earlier identification with the fifth. The compilers emphasize strengthening the root before action. No one diagrammatic identification has been silently substituted for the others.

[^j08-dispersing-self]: **Self-interest dispersed, or the person kept safe.** Zhu and the compilers understand 渙其躬 through dispersing private self-concern and serving a larger need. Cheng instead reads it as the person's own freedom from the regrets of dispersal, without the capacity to rescue others. These readings differ substantially. “Disperse one's self” in the oracle is not an instruction to injure the body or deny personal existence; the commentary supplies its ethical and social applications.

[^j08-group-hill]: **Breaking a faction is not simply gathering the scattered.** Zhu's recorded conversation makes this criticism of Cheng explicit: 群其渙, gathering what has dispersed, is not the same verbal construction as 渙其群, dispersing one's group. Old Su, 老蘇, is Su Xun; compare his discussion of dispersing groups in [《仲兄文甫字說》, preserved in 《崇古文訣》卷二十一](https://www.shidianguji.com/zh/book/SK3202/chapter/1k0ir8gp9unn6). The hill, 丘, signifies an accumulation or larger gathering. In the favored interpretation, giving up a private faction makes a broader union possible; the compilers contrast this with coalitions held together only by advantage and power.

[^j08-sweat-command]: **A proclamation like sweat.** Zhu's main commentary emphasizes issuing forth without return; his recorded conversation and Cheng emphasize permeation from the center to the extremities. Hu Yuan develops an analogy with releasing bodily obstruction. These are the historical physiological images by which the commentators explain public commands. The compilers additionally require 號 to be read as a voiced call in the level tone, linking public proclamation with the earnest expression of the heart. The English retains proclamation in the oracle rather than turning the image into a prescription for producing sweat.

[^j08-royal-stores]: **王居: stores or royal position.** Zhu takes 居 as accumulated stores to be distributed, comparing Lu Zhi's small reserve dispersed to create a greater one. Cheng and Yu Yan take it as the royal position. The compilers prescribe a pause after the second 渙: roughly, “amid dispersion, the king occupies his place.” These are alternative constructions of the same unpunctuated words. The main oracle follows Zhu; the commentaries preserve the alternative punctuation and meaning.

[^j08-blood-distance]: **逖 and 惕.** The base and parallel oracle both have 逖, “far away.” Zhu proposes 惕, fear, by comparison with Small Restraint's fourth line; Cheng also explains the passage through blood and fear. Wang Bi and Zhu Zhen retain distance, and Qian Yiben distinguishes departure, remoteness, and emergence. The English oracle retains the base's distance reading, while “bloodshed” renders blood as an image of injury or harm, not necessarily a particular war. Zhu's correction remains an attributed historical proposal, not a silent change to the source. The final editorial discussion insists that departure and continued service are judged according to their different times.

[^j08-limitation]: **Limits that permit passage.** 節 includes measure, regulation, restraint, and the stopping of excess. Here the lake's capacity supplies a concrete image, but the compilers insist that successful limitation also permits circulation. “Limitation” should not be understood as an unconditional preference for less activity, less expenditure, or withdrawal: the second line is unfavorable precisely because it does not emerge when it should.

[^j08-bitter-limit]: **When steadfastness becomes the fault.** In 苦節不可貞, 貞 cannot simply be translated as an always-beneficial virtue. Cheng explains that bitter restraint cannot be maintained as a lasting rule; Zhu warns against preserving excess as correctness. This does not erase the favorable uses of 貞 elsewhere. The word's force must follow the proposition in which it occurs.

[^j08-door-courtyards]: **Door and gate.** 戶庭 is the courtyard outside the door; 門庭, in the following line, is the courtyard inside the gate. Both glosses are supplied explicitly by the commentaries. Translating both as merely “staying home” would lose the spatial distinction. More important, Zhu sees the first as able to restrain itself, whereas Cheng presents restraint as something it must be warned to practice. The two voices are not harmonized.

[^j08-gate-time]: **The same conduct, a different time.** The first's non-emergence is appropriate to initial containment; the second's is not appropriate to circulation after accumulation. Qian Zhili's banks and outlets make this distinction especially clear. Centeredness at the second place does not by itself guarantee a favorable verdict. The commentary explicitly judges firmness, position, neighbors, and timing together.

[^j08-jie-surplus]: **A difficult reading shared by the two transcriptions and the scan.** Cheng's second-line gloss reads 損過益有餘 in the base, in the parallel transcription, and in the [page image linked by 易學網, 0917](https://www.eee-learning.com/eeebook/middleyi/0917.png): literally, “reduce excess and increase surplus.” The English instead follows the transmission 損過抑有餘, “reduce excess and restrain surplus,” preserved in the quotation of this passage in [《分類經進近思錄集解》, 悔吝忿欲輕惰](https://www.shidianguji.com/zh/book/HY1435/chapter/1l3wfvpat8u80). This is a correction supported by another transmission of Cheng's words, **not** a reading found in the consulted 《周易折中》 witnesses. The textual difficulty is old: [《大易粹言》卷六十](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/大易粹言_(四庫全書本)/卷60) retains 益有餘 but reports the alternative 益不及, “increase what falls short.” The correction to 抑 best preserves the grammatical parallel with curbing anger and checking desire, but these witnesses do not establish with certainty what the compilers' copy originally read.

[^j08-sighing-blame]: **No blame, or no one else to blame?** Cheng concludes that the consequences are self-incurred and cannot be blamed on another. Master Zhang explicitly rejects the need for such a special rule here: remorse can lead to making good one's fault, and therefore to genuine freedom from blame. Li Yanzhang and Zheng Ruxie develop the latter reading. The unchanged oracle permits the disagreement to remain visible.

[^j08-jie-readings]: **悲歎 restored.** Feng Yinchu's third-line gloss has 形於悲歡, “appearing in sorrow and joy,” in the base. The parallel reads 形於悲歎, “appearing in sorrowful sighs.” The English adopts 歎, consistent with the line's 嗟 and the discussion of remorse. The name 寅初 is retained as given in both transcriptions.

[^j08-easy-limitation]: **At ease is not without discipline.** 安 contrasts here with 勉, effort or forced observance. The fourth follows an appropriate measure without continual coercion of itself. The compilers connect this with established rules and water flowing over level ground. The word does not mean that any action felt to be comfortable is therefore correct.

[^j08-sweet-limit]: **Sweetness and humane measure.** 甘 is both an actual taste and the image of restraint that does not damage resources or harm the people. Zhao Rumei places sweetness at the center of the flavors, capable of harmonious blending; the compilers contrast flowing spring water with stagnant saline water. These are their traditional sensory and cosmological analogies. The political point is that a measure can be effective without becoming bitter to those who live under it.

[^j08-bitter-regret]: **How can misfortune coexist with disappearing regret?** Zhu and Lü Dalin preserve a residual value in frugality even when restraint is excessive. Kong Yingda distinguishes imposing bitter restraint on others from applying austerity to oneself. Cheng gives a different construction: regret the excess, and the **misfortune** disappears. He expressly says that the same words 悔亡 have a different meaning here. The oracle is not rewritten to settle that disagreement.

[^j08-jie-summary]: **Position and the final contrast.** Qiu Fuguo first praises correct position, then explains the final pair through centeredness versus extremity. The top six is positionally correct but nevertheless bitter and unfavorable. His account therefore cannot be reduced to “every correctly placed line is good.” The concluding contrast of circulation and blockage, sweetness and bitterness, supplies the more comprehensive account of the hexagram.

[^j08-inner-sincerity]: **Inward truthfulness and outward trust.** The established title “Inner Sincerity” renders 中孚 without reducing 孚 either to private feeling or to another person's belief. Zhu's recorded conversation distinguishes what is present within, 孚, from its manifestation in affairs, 信. The commentaries repeatedly require sincerity to be correct: mutual confidence in a corrupt undertaking is not the virtue praised here.

[^j08-inner-empty-full]: **Open and solid centers.** Viewed as one six-line figure, the two central yin lines make an open center. Viewed as two trigrams, the yang in the second and fifth places makes each trigram solid at its center. Cheng therefore calls openness trust's root and solidity its substance. Zhu explains the ethical counterpart as absence of a private master within and absence of falsity in action. These are two complementary views of the figure, not competing counts of its lines.

[^j08-pigs-fish]: **Why the oracle says “reaches.”** The base separates the name 中孚 from 豚魚吉 with punctuation. Cai Qing and Wu Yueshen explicitly read the words together: sincerity reaches pigs and fish, and this is auspicious for the person possessing sincerity. “Reaches” in the English oracle makes that supported construction explicit. Both principal commentaries take 豚魚 as pigs and fish, not as a single animal or as an instruction about sacrificial food. Their claims about sincerity's power belong to the traditional interpretation, not to an empirical assurance that sincerity removes physical danger.

[^j08-hatching-trust]: **Hatching and the graph 孚.** Su Shi compares inward sincerity to the hatching of a feathered creature. Zhu cites a traditional explanation of 孚 through its “claw” and “child” components and a brooding bird. This image is preserved as historical interpretation; it is not presented as an independently established account of the character's earliest formation. Its point here is that an actual inner substance precedes effective transformation and trust.

[^j08-deliberation-rest]: **虞 as assessing or resting at ease.** Zhu and Cheng understand 虞 as deliberation about what deserves trust. Xun Shuang, Xiang Anshi, and the compilers understand it as resting securely in oneself without seeking an external attachment. The oracle initially follows the principal commentaries, while the alternative is translated in full. Cheng also insists that a formal correspondence cannot take the place of examination: trusting a line merely because it corresponds would not itself be 虞.

[^j08-sincerity-correspondence]: **A generalization about correspondences.** The compilers say at the third line that only the third and top have a correspondence; both consulted transcriptions contain this wording. Formally, however, the first and fourth also correspond, and the same compilation discusses that pair explicitly. The claim is best understood as concentrating on the tie still pursued after the first's inward repose and the fourth's severance of its mate, not as an accurate inventory of all potential correspondences. Likewise, the earlier claim that correspondences are unfavorable is a general interpretive tendency, not a replacement for the fourth line's actual “no blame.” Neither statement has been silently corrected in the main translation.

[^j08-crane-cup]: **爵 as wine-cup or honor, 靡 as sharing or attachment.** The English oracle follows the compilers' concrete reading: fine wine and drinking together. Zhu and Cheng read 爵 through fine honor or virtue and 靡 through binding attachment; Kong Yingda develops the sharing of rank and authority. The compilers instead gloss 靡 as intoxication. These different meanings remain explicit in the commentaries. The crane's response illustrates sincerity communicating without a quest for display; it is not an instruction to seek a particular bird omen.

[^j08-zhongfu-readings]: **Two transcription corrections.** At the end of Cheng's second-line explanation, the base has 知道若為能識之; the parallel has 知道者為能識之, “one who knows the Way can recognize it.” At the top line, Zhu's 故其象占如比 is corrected with the parallel to 故其象占如此, “hence this image and prognostication.” The fifth-line correction 孿 → 攣 is documented separately in the note on binding sincerity.

[^j08-crane-respondent]: **Who answers the crane?** Zhu identifies the responding figure with the fifth nine, which shares the second's inner substance. This is response through like virtue, not ordinary yin–yang correspondence. Zhang Jun and the compilers identify the young with the first line and emphasize cultivation of what is near. Cheng emphasizes the principle of sincerity's communication without assigning every image to a fixed line. The translation preserves this difference between a response that reaches afar and the compilers' stress on the nearby relationship.

[^j08-changing-moods]: **A counterpart, not necessarily an enemy.** 敵 can mean an opposing or matching party. Here the principal commentaries identify it with the third's correspondent at the top. What follows is dependence upon that other person's responses: now exertion, now cessation, sorrow, or joy. The oracle retains the concrete drumming and singing, while Cheng interprets the drum as arousal to action. No explicit verdict is added to this line; its troubling character is conveyed by the commentators.

[^j08-moon-horse]: **Severing a pair to preserve a single allegiance.** The horse's mate is identified with the first line, whose tie the fourth relinquishes in order to support the fifth. The moon nearly full expresses the proper flourishing of a subordinate without rivalry toward the ruler. Cheng's discussion of matching chariot horses explains 匹 as a pair. These images are not instructions to abandon an actual companion or a calendar rule inferred from the moon's phase.

[^j08-binding-sincerity]: **攣, not 孿.** The base's fifth-line oracle reads 有孚孿如, using the character for twins. The parallel reads 有孚攣如, and the source's own commentaries repeatedly use 攣 and 拘攣 for firm binding. The English therefore adopts the attested 攣 reading. The image concerns hearts bound together by sincerity, rather than a prediction of twins. The compilers distinguish the ruler's obligation to communicate sincerity to the world from the private person's preservation of inner virtue.

[^j08-winged-cry]: **A cock's call or reputation without substance.** Zhu calls the cock 翰音 and connects it with Xun; Zhu Zhen explains the shaking of its wing feathers before calling. Cheng and Wang Bi emphasize a sound rising while substance does not follow; Su Shi makes the contrast with the hidden crane explicit. “Winged cry” retains the shared image without imposing the cock identification on every commentator. The warning is against outward display or rigid confidence unsupported by inner substance and learning, not against audible prayer or song as such.

[^j08-small-excess]: **Three senses of the small exceeding.** Cheng explicitly distinguishes the small, associated with yin, exceeding its ordinary measure; excess in small affairs; and an excess that is slight. Lin Xiyuan and the compilers emphasize kinds of affairs rather than classes of people. The title “Small Excess” retains these possibilities. Going beyond the ordinary can restore an appropriate mean, but the text repeatedly distinguishes such correction from excess pursued for its own sake.

[^j08-bird-cry]: **A warning left by a bird.** The figure's solid middle and open outer parts are interpreted as a flying bird. Zhu stresses a cry descending; Cheng stresses a passing that is not far; Wang Bi hears a plaintive search for rest. The compilers treat the hexagram's omen as a cry left to its reader, borrowing ascent and descent to distinguish arrogance from humility. The English keeps the bird and its cry rather than replacing them in the oracle with an abstract instruction to be modest.

[^j08-xiaoguo-position]: **The third line's apparently lost position.** Both principal commentaries describe yang as lacking a fitting position in this hexagram, and Zhu includes both third and fourth under that description. Yet the third nine is formally correct as yang in an odd position, which its own commentary acknowledges. The wider claim concerns yang's ineffective situation amid yin's excess, or at least uses position more broadly than the ordinary odd/even rule. The wording is retained; it is not silently corrected to make the third line positionally incorrect. The tension itself cautions against treating every occurrence of 位 as a purely mechanical designation.

[^j08-bird-first]: **Flight from a place where one should remain.** The first's position below is not automatically favorable: it seeks to rise when it should rest. Xiang Anshi and Hu Bingwen identify the first and top with the outer wing-feathers, distinguishing them from the central body. Zhu also reports an animal-related divination in Guo Pu's *Donglin*. That remains a report within his commentary, not an independently verified case or an added instruction to regard an actual bird as a prediction of disaster.

[^j08-ancestor-minister]: **Passing beyond and stopping short.** The principal commentaries identify the grandfather with the fourth and the ancestress with the fifth. “Meeting the minister” is then explained as fulfilling the minister's proper role without encroaching on the ruler, rather than necessarily meeting a second person in a literal journey. Zhu's compressed account first reaches the fifth as ancestress and then does not reach it as ruler. Both consulted transcriptions have 如此, “in this way,” at that transition. The English retains it: the two images employ the fifth under different relationships, and the passage has not been silently rewritten into a single itinerary.

[^j08-generations-ritual]: **Ancestral rows and ritual discretion.** 昭穆 denotes the alternating organization of ancestral generations; 祔 here concerns ritual attachment to an ancestral position, not merely physical proximity between living relatives. The compilers consequently read 妣 more specifically as the husband's grandmother in relation to a granddaughter-in-law. Their allusion to the hemp cap and bowing below invokes the *Analects* discussion in which Confucius accepts an economical change in a cap but retains deferential bowing below. The example supports discriminating adjustment, not either absolute conservatism or unrestricted innovation.

[^j08-extra-precaution]: **Go beyond in care, not aggression.** The third is warned to take more than ordinary precautions, but Cheng expressly includes excessive rigidity itself among the faults to be guarded against. Rectifying oneself comes first. Thus the line cannot be reduced to a recommendation for heightened hostility toward others. 從或戕之 leaves the attacker unspecified: someone following may inflict harm. The conditional explanation in the commentaries is preserved; misfortune is not made inevitable regardless of conduct.

[^j08-meeting-exceeding]: **An ambiguity Zhu leaves open.** The principal explanation takes 弗過遇之 as not exceeding in firmness and meeting what is fitting. Zhu then records another possibility, patterned on the preceding line's 過防: fail to treat a matter with exceptional attentiveness, and danger follows. His recorded conversation favors that second reading, and the compilers adopt it. The oracle follows the first construction, while both are translated in the commentary. 勿用永貞 warns against an unchanging rule when circumstances require adaptation; it does not abolish the requirement of correctness.

[^j08-cloud-cave]: **An ineffectual alliance or a remedy through seeking the worthy.** Zhu and Cheng regard the fifth's seeking the second as two yin joining without the capacity to accomplish great affairs. Yao Shunmu and the compilers stress humbling oneself to obtain help, allowing withheld beneficence to become rain. The contrast with the clouds of Small Restraint remains explicit. 弋 is a corded shot, distinguished from ordinary shooting through the retrieval of the quarry. The cave's occupant is associated with the second line; the oracle itself does not supply a personal name.

[^j08-bird-caught]: **Failing to meet, not a command to fail.** 弗遇過之 describes passing beyond what is fitting. It is not translated as an imperative to avoid meeting and press onward. Zhu leaves open a proposed reversal of 遇過; Cheng takes 離 as having passed far beyond, whereas Kong Yingda, Yu Qishu, and Yu Yan take it as becoming caught. The English oracle follows the latter, attested interpretation of the bird entangled in a net or fowling cord. These are alternative explanations of the transmitted words, not a silently reconstructed new oracle.

[^j08-calamity-fault]: **Different accounts of 災 and 眚.** Cheng distinguishes a calamity arriving from Heaven from a fault arising through human action. The compilers compare this line with Return's top and describe calamity and fault together as arriving from outside, distinguishing the grammar of “there is” from “this is called.” Their account does not simply repeat Cheng's distinction. Both are retained as historical causal interpretations; neither warrants assigning personal moral blame to someone because a real-world misfortune has occurred.

[^j08-closing-sequence]: **The two Classics brought into parallel.** Xiang Anshi compares the close of the Upper Classic—Nourishment, Great Excess, Kan, and Li—with the Lower Classic's Inner Sincerity, Small Excess, and the two Completion hexagrams. His resemblance between a six-line figure and Kan or Li concerns patterns of enclosure, not identity with a three-line trigram. This prepares the final pair without implying that the work has ended: the compilation continues after Juan Eight with its treatment of the transmitted commentaries and supplementary material.

[^j08-after-completion]: **Accomplishment and crossing.** 濟 means both bringing an undertaking to completion and getting across, especially across water. “After Completion” retains the established title, while the commentary's concrete images of wheels, a fox, a carriage, and a boat preserve the crossing sense. All six lines are positionally correct, yet the text is full of warnings. Formal completion is not treated as immunity from decline.

[^j08-jiji-small]: **亨小 versus 小亨.** The base and parallel have 亨小. Zhu proposes reversing the words to 小亨, “small success”; Cheng expressly defends their transmitted order, explaining success in small matters after great matters are already accomplished. Gu Jiajie agrees with Cheng. The main oracle follows the transmitted order and its supported explanation, not Zhu's emendation. His proposal is nevertheless translated in full.

[^j08-completion-seasons]: **Transitions rather than absolute endings.** The compilers compare Peace and Obstruction to summer and winter, and the two Completion hexagrams to the transitions of autumn and spring. Their Earlier Heaven diagram likewise places Qian and Kun at the poles and Li and Kan at the intermediate directions. This is a traditional scheme for understanding order and disorder, not a prediction that a particular consultation necessarily refers to a named season. It also explains why the completed condition can be less promising than one poised to emerge from incompletion.

[^j08-jiji-readings]: **Three attested corrections.** The compilers' 兩瑞 in the Judgment discussion is corrected to the parallel's 兩端, “two poles.” In Cheng's second-line commentary, 唐太宗之用言 is read with the parallel as 唐太宗之用賢, Tang Taizong's employment of the worthy, not his use of words. In the fifth-line editorial judgment, 若以兩鄰為六二 becomes the parallel's 若以西鄰為六二, “if the western neighbor is identified as the second six.” The last correction matters because it is precisely the western neighbor's reception of blessing that the compilers discuss. None changes the Chinese source file.

[^j08-wheels-tail]: **Intentional restraint and an unintended wet tail.** Zhu and Cheng explain both images as checking advance, whereas the compilers distinguish them: drawing back the wheels is deliberate, wetting the tail is not. The English oracle is descriptive rather than commanding the reader to wet the tail. The first line's no-blame verdict therefore does not mean that every obstruction or incomplete crossing is fortunate; its interpretation depends on the possibility of stopping in time.

[^j08-carriage-screen]: **A screen needed for proper travel.** 茀 is explained as the covering or screen of a woman's carriage. Its loss removes a condition for proper departure, not necessarily the carriage itself. The commentators compare this with a worthy person whose opportunity to serve is withheld. Cheng takes seven days through the six positions followed by a renewed cycle; the text's actual “seven days” is preserved without converting it into a fixed modern timetable or deleting it in favor of “eventually.”

[^j08-gaozong-guifang]: **The campaign and its duration.** Cheng identifies Gaozong as the Shang ruler, and the compilers characterize him as a restorer who moved from incompletion to completion. Guifang is retained as a proper name, not translated as a supernatural enemy or assigned a modern ethnicity. The three years emphasize, in the commentaries, the labor and delay required by the campaign. Zhu warns against undertaking it lightly; Cheng distinguishes rescue of the people from greed, anger, and territorial ambition. The next hexagram returns to the campaign from a different stage of accomplishment.

[^j08-leak-rags]: **An adopted historical reading of 繻.** The oracle has 繻, associated with fine silk; Zhu reports Cheng's proposal to read 濡, wetting or seepage. The main translation follows that proposal because the collected explanations consistently develop rags prepared to stop a boat's leaks. It is an explicitly adopted historical emendation, not a reading newly found in the consulted base or parallel oracle. Hu Bingwen's point is twofold: lack of an apparent leak does not remove the need for equipment, and possession of equipment does not replace vigilance.

[^j08-two-neighbors]: **Timing, sincerity, and the recipient of blessing.** Zhu and Cheng associate east with the fifth and west with the second; Zhu also compares King Wen with the Shang king Zhou, 紂, not the Zhou dynasty, 周. Pan Shizao refuses to assign the neighbors to those two lines, and Yao Shunmu emphasizes sincere worship rather than abundant offerings. The compilers adopt those latter explanations and make the warning concern the fifth itself. 禴 remains the simple yue sacrifice discussed in earlier juans. The contrast does not establish that the cheapest offering is always best, irrespective of time and intention.

[^j08-wet-head]: **The end of a figure is not the end of vigilance.** At the first line the tail is wet; at the top, the head is submerged. Zhu Zhen distinguishes the order of drawing the figure, from bottom to top, from the head-and-tail orientation of the completed figure. The warning concerns confidence that a crossing is already secured when danger remains. The oracle says danger, 厲, not an express sentence of inevitable death; the stronger language in particular commentaries remains theirs.

[^j08-before-completion]: **Not yet, rather than never.** 未濟 names an undertaking not yet accomplished. Hu Bingwen expressly distinguishes this from an impossibility of accomplishment. All six lines are formally out of position, but their relations and circumstances can still permit success. Cheng's opening explanation makes ending with incompletion a way of refusing exhaustion: generation and change remain possible beyond the book's last hexagram.

[^j08-little-fox]: **Youth and nearly crossing.** Zhu reads 汔 as nearly. Cheng proposes 仡, vigorous or rashly bold, and contrasts a young fox with an older, cautious one. The compilers deliberately combine Cheng's explanation of the little fox with Zhu's reading of 汔; the English oracle follows that combination. Cheng's remarks about foxes listening on ice are retained as his traditional explanation, not substituted with modern zoology or treated as independently verified observation.

[^j08-weiji-wheels]: **Restraint before effective action.** The second's dragged-back wheels prevent impetuous advancement. Cheng develops the image through a capable minister's restraint in serving a weaker ruler; Pan Mengqi emphasizes assessing time and capacity. The compilers compare it with After Completion's first line and explicitly distinguish deliberate braking from accidental wetting of the tail. The same images recur one position later because the stages of the two hexagrams differ.

[^j08-weiji-crossing]: **The difficult third line is not repaired by inventing a negative.** The oracle says both that going brings misfortune and that crossing the great river is beneficial. Zhu records a suspicion that 不, “not,” has been omitted before 利, but does not establish it. Both the base and parallel lack that negative, and the English retains the tension. Zhu distinguishes travel by water from travel overland; Cheng distinguishes the possible opportunity from the line's inadequate capacity; the compilers distinguish rash advancement from the caution needed for crossing. None of these explanations licenses quietly changing “beneficial” to “not beneficial.”

[^j08-firmness-question]: **邪 as a final particle.** In the quoted 其柔危，其剛勝邪, 邪 is read as a question particle, not as “evil.” 勝 concerns being equal to the burden or prevailing: hence “its yieldingness is endangered; might its firmness be equal to the task?” The passage comes from the [lower *Appended Statements*](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/易傳/繫辭下), where third and fifth positions are compared. The compilers apply it here to the third lines of the two Completion hexagrams. Rendering the phrase as “firmness conquers evil” would introduce a different proposition.

[^j08-weiji-readings]: **A correction and two base readings retained.** In the fifth-line editorial discussion, the base's 嘉靖殷邦之侯 is read with the parallel's 嘉靖殷邦之𠉀: 候, a time or occasion, not 侯, a lord. Conversely, the third-line base reads 涉大川 and 未濟無傷也, crossing the river and remaining incomplete without harm. The parallel transcription has 步大川 and 未濟無陽也 there. Those less satisfactory readings have not displaced the coherent base. Agreement with another transcription is evidence to weigh, not a rule requiring every difference to be adopted. The compilers also connect Gaozong's campaign with 荊楚 in the fifth-line discussion; that allusion is retained without asserting that Guifang and Jing–Chu are geographically identical.

[^j08-campaign-rewards]: **After three years, or throughout them?** Zhu and Cheng explain the rewards as following prolonged effort and accomplishment. The compilers instead take 三年有賞 as rewards continually honoring the troops during the three years, expressly distinguishing this from distribution after the campaign. The English oracle says “in three years,” leaving that temporal distinction to the commentaries. Gaozong is not named in this line statement, although the compilers associate its campaign with his. 震 is vigorous arousal or awe-producing action here, not an added trigram in the figure.

[^j08-radiant-trust]: **A favorable fifth despite its formal position.** Yin at the fifth is formally incorrect, yet the line's open center and reception of firm assistance make correctness possible. Cheng treats its 貞 as a virtue already possessed, not merely an injunction. He distinguishes the first 吉, good fortune of virtue, from the final 吉, good fortune of accomplishment. The repeated verdict is therefore preserved rather than compressed into a single “auspicious.” The compilers likewise distinguish “regret vanishes” from the more established “no regret.”

[^j08-wine-wet-head]: **Enjoyment without surrender of judgment.** Drinking wine expresses self-sustaining enjoyment while awaiting what can be done. Wetting the head warns against indulgence or overconfidence. Zhu returns explicitly to the fox crossing water; Qiu Fuguo emphasizes drinking beyond measure. The English therefore retains both wine and the wet head rather than collapsing them into one literal incident. The second part is conditional, not an instruction to wet the head. The passage neither requires drinking alcohol nor promises that waiting alone will complete an undertaking.

[^j08-ending-open]: **The ending preserves a disagreement.** Cheng says at the top that Before Completion contains no principle of automatic completion simply because its limit has been reached. Liu Mu, Shi Jie, Zheng Ruxie, and the concluding summaries speak more confidently of reversal toward completion. Both positions remain visible. Wan Shan's claim that After Completion's line statements contain no 吉 is correct as a count of those six oracle statements, but does not mean they contain no possibility of benefit or release from blame. Wu Yueshen's final threefold account of 易—unchanging order, reciprocal exchange, and transformation—closes Juan Eight. This completes the compilation's commentary on the sixty-four hexagrams and their line texts; the remaining juans, including the separately treated Wings and supplementary discussions, are not thereby translated.
