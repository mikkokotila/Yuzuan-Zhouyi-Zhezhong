## Chapter 1 · Images, Movement, and Constant Principle

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:002 -->

### The eight trigrams are arrayed, and the images are within them. Extend them by doubling, and the lines are within them.[^j15-trigrams-lines]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:002 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:003 -->

**Original Meaning.** “Arrayed” refers to such an order as Qian first, Dui second, Li third, Zhen fourth, Xun fifth, Kan sixth, Gen seventh, and Kun eighth. “Images” means the configurations of the figures. “Extend them by doubling” means taking each trigram in turn and successively adding the eight trigrams above it, making sixty-four hexagrams. “Lines” means the six lines: only after the doubling does a figure have six lines.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:004 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:005 -->

**Han Kangbo:** The eight trigrams contain the principles of all under Heaven, but do not yet exhaust their changes. They are therefore extended by doubling to represent their activity and use. The meanings residing in the lines and in the figures then differ; hence “the lines are within them.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:005 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:006 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu:** The array of the eight trigrams arises progressively from the Supreme Ultimate, the Two Modes, and the Four Images, until it reaches this point. Only after the figures have been drawn does one see that they possess the images of the Three Powers. It is not that the sage first saw the Three Powers and then, through his own deliberation, drew three consecutive lines to represent them. Doubling likewise proceeds from the already completed trigrams, progressively generating further lines above each. If the lines are generated one after another, three further transformations are needed to make sixty-four hexagrams. If the whole trigram is generated at once, only one transformation is needed. Though these procedures differ in speed, both proceed by successive generation, each with its own rows and order. Only after the figures have been drawn does one see that they can exhaust the changes of all under Heaven.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:006 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:007 -->

**Chai Zhongxing:** Once the eight trigrams stand arrayed, all the world's images are included within them, not merely eight things. Everything listed in the Discussion of the Trigrams belongs here.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:007 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:008 -->

**Master Zheng:** The figures begin with three strokes; there are not yet lines in this fuller sense. When they are extended by doubling, their structure has upper and lower, their positions inner and outer, their time beginning and ending, their sequence before and after. Then the lines are within them.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:008 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:009 -->

### Firm and yielding press upon one another, and change is within them. Statements are appended to pronounce upon them, and movement is within them.[^j15-pushing-movement]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:009 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:010 -->

**Original Meaning.** As firm and yielding press upon one another, the changes of hexagrams and lines, coming and going and interweaving, can all be seen. Taking these changes as they are, the sage appends statements to pronounce their good fortune and misfortune. Thus the line-images encountered in consultation that are to move do not fall outside this.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:010 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:011 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:011 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:012 -->

**Yu Fan:** Firm and yielding press upon one another and produce change and transformation; hence “change is within them.” The statements of the Judgments, Images, nines, and sixes are appended; hence “movement is within them.” It is in the statements that the world's movements are stirred.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:012 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:013 -->

**Kong Yingda:** Chapter Two of the upper Appended Statements says, “Firm and yielding press upon one another and produce change and transformation.” Thus the Way of change and transformation lies within their pressing upon one another.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:013 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:014 -->

**Cai Qing:** Heaven's patterns, Earth's order, human affairs, and the kinds of things are exhausted in one firm and one yielding. Beyond these two, nothing remains. Everything firm is what the yielding has pressed forth; everything yielding is what the firm has pressed forth. Within the hexagrams of the Changes there are likewise only these two. What is not firm is yielding; what is not yielding is firm. Whatever is firm has been pressed forth by the yielding; whatever is yielding has been pressed forth by the firm.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:014 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:015 -->

**Su Jun:** Concerning “movement is within them,” Yu Fan cites “it is in the statements that the world's movements are stirred.” This is exactly right. “Movement” here has the same meaning as in “arise from movement” and “the world's movements” below. The statements of the Changes were originally appended by the sage after he saw the world's movements. Hence “it is in the statements that the world's movements are stirred.” This explains “movement is within them”; it does not refer to the hexagrams and lines that are to move.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:015 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:016 -->

### Good fortune and misfortune, regret and humiliation, arise from movement.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:016 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:017 -->

**Original Meaning.** Good fortune, misfortune, regret, and humiliation are all what the statements pronounce. Yet they become apparent only through the movement of the hexagrams and lines.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:017 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:018 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:018 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:019 -->

**Gong Yuan:** An image is a figure's completed structure; thus the world's intricacies reside in it. A line is the changing movement of the six positions; thus the world's movements reside in it. Firm and yielding pressing upon one another bring the lines into being. Since the lines speak of change, “change is within them.” Appending statements to pronounce upon them makes the lines clear. Since statements stir the world's movements, “movement is within them.” The figures first emerge in the array and become complete through doubling; the lines first emerge in change and become complete through movement. Thus good fortune, misfortune, regret, and humiliation arise.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:019 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:020 -->

**Su Jun:** The Commentary says, “Still and unmoving,” and also speaks of “the subtle beginning of movement, the first appearance of good fortune.” While there is no movement, there is not yet even what we call good fortune; how could there be misfortune? Only when movement begins in its subtlety does good fortune appear. When movement becomes tangled and confused, misfortune, regret, and humiliation begin to arise within it.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:020 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:021 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** This restates the meaning of appending statements to pronounce upon the figures, with movement within them. All the world's good fortune, misfortune, regret, and humiliation arise from movement in human affairs. Therefore the Changes contains statements of these outcomes, and movement is within them.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:021 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:022 -->

### Firm and yielding establish the foundation. Change and free passage answer to the time.[^j15-foundation-time]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:022 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:023 -->

**Original Meaning.** One firm and one yielding each have their established positions. Passing from this to that, they change in accordance with the time.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:023 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:024 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:024 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:025 -->

**Zhu Zhen:** The lines have firmness and yieldingness. Without the two, the one cannot stand: this establishes the foundation. Firm and yielding change into one another; carrying their changes through to free passage, so as to fulfill their benefit, answers to the time. Answering to the time means attaining the mean appropriate to that time.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:025 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:026 -->

**Zhang Jun:** Firm and yielding press upon one another, coming and going, advancing and retreating. Their changes have no fixed pattern, yet all follow the naturally fitting time. Hence “answer to the time.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:026 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:027 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu:** These two sentences are paired. Firmness and yieldingness are the constitution of yin and yang: an established structure that cannot be exchanged away. Hence they are called the foundation. Firm changing into yielding and yielding into firm are the activity of change and free passage.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:027 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:028 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu, continued:** Firm and yielding are images of day and night: this is establishing the foundation. Change and transformation are images of advance and retreat: this is answering to the time. The two, firm and yielding, are the foundation; change and free passage are simply their coming and going.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:028 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:029 -->

**Hu Bingwen:** Each hexagram has its time, and each line has its time. Establishing the foundation is Heaven and Earth's constant order; answering to the time is the rightness that runs through past and present.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:029 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:030 -->

**Liang Yin:** Firm and yielding establishing the foundation are the unchanging constitution, the closing and opening of the door spoken of earlier. Change and free passage answering to the time are the activity of changing, the inexhaustible coming and going spoken of earlier.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:030 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:031 -->

**Cai Qing:** Firm and yielding establishing the foundation are what is described as change in complementary opposition. Change and free passage answering to the time are what is described as change in circulation.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:031 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:032 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** This restates the meaning of firm and yielding pressing upon one another to produce change and transformation. Throughout Heaven and Earth, two principles stand in complementary opposition: thus they are not one-sided and can establish the foundation. The two act by turns: thus they are not exhausted and can answer to the time. Therefore, within the Changes, firm and yielding press upon one another, and change is within them.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:032 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:033 -->

### Good fortune and misfortune: what is constant prevails.[^j15-constant-prevails]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:033 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:034 -->

**Original Meaning.** Zhen means correct and constant. Things take what is correct for them as their constancy. In the world's affairs, what is not good fortune is misfortune, and what is not misfortune is good fortune. They constantly prevail over one another without cease.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:034 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:035 -->

### Heaven and Earth's Way is constant in its showing. Sun and moon's Way is constant in its illumination. The world's movements are constant in the one.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:035 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:036 -->

**Original Meaning.** “Showing” means making manifest. The changes of the world's movements are inexhaustible. Yet following principle brings good fortune and opposing principle brings misfortune. What is correct and constant in them is therefore also just one principle.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:036 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:037 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:037 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:038 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu:** Good fortune and misfortune constantly prevail over one another. Either good fortune prevails over misfortune or misfortune prevails over good fortune. Since the two constantly prevail over one another, the text says “constant prevailing.” Heaven and Earth's Way constantly shows; sun and moon's Way constantly illuminates. The world's movements are constant in the one. Though the world's movements are not uniform, there is always something that is right; hence “constant in the one.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:038 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:039 -->

**Gao Cui:** Heaven constantly shows people ease; Earth constantly shows them simplicity. Though yin cannot always avoid excess and yang cannot always avoid concealment, the principle of their constant showing remains what it is. The sun illuminates the day, and the moon the night. Though the sun at noon cannot avoid declining and the full moon cannot avoid waning, the principle of constant illumination remains what it is. The world's movements—advance and retreat, continuance and destruction—cannot be measured by a single formula. Yet following principle brings ample scope, while following desire brings danger: both are measured by the same standard. The good fortune of following the Way and the misfortune of opposing it do not constitute two principles. Thus the correctness and constancy of creative transformation and human affairs are precisely the constant prevailing of good fortune and misfortune. How could one seek to explain them through two separate principles?

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:039 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:040 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** From “good fortune and misfortune: constant prevailing” to this point forms one passage. It continues the meaning of good fortune, misfortune, regret, and humiliation arising from movement, and clarifies the unity of their principle. Master Zhang takes “constant prevailing” to mean prevailing through correctness; Zhu takes it to mean that the two constantly prevail over one another. Considering the wording, we should understand it as the constant being what prevails. In the world, the good may indeed encounter misfortune and the wicked obtain good fortune. But this is not the constant course. Good fortune in following the Way and misfortune in opposing it are the constant principle. The constant should therefore be taken as prevailing. Heaven and Earth show through what is constant; sun and moon illuminate through what is constant. Occasional anomalies are not sufficient grounds for the account. Do not all the world's movements constantly return to one principle?

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:040 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:041 -->

### Qian, firm and assured, shows people ease. Kun, yielding and unassuming, shows people simplicity.[^j15-qian-kun-show]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:041 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:042 -->

**Original Meaning.** “Firm and assured” describes vigor; “yielding and unassuming” describes compliance. This is the “constant showing” spoken of above.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:042 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:043 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:043 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:044 -->

**Han Kangbo:** Que describes firmness; tui describes yieldingness. Qian and Kun each maintain the unity of their virtue. Hence simplicity and ease.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:044 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:045 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** This passage likewise continues the meaning of firm and yielding establishing the foundation and change and free passage answering to the time, and clarifies the unity of their principle. Qian and Kun are the source of firmness and yieldingness. Once they have their positions, change and transformation are inexhaustible. Yet the foundation they establish returns wholly to the principle of ease and simplicity. This is what is meant by “Heaven has a manifest Way; its patterns are clearly displayed”: unchanging through all ages.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:045 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:046 -->

### The lines enact this; the images represent this.[^j15-enacting-images]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:046 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:047 -->

**Original Meaning.** “This” means the principle shown by Qian and Kun above. The single and divided strokes of the lines, and the waning and waxing of the hexagrams, enact and represent it.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:047 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:048 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** “The lines enact this” concludes the meaning of good fortune, misfortune, regret, and humiliation arising from movement and being constant in the one. “The images represent this” concludes the meaning of firmness, yieldingness, change, and free passage returning to ease and simplicity.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:048 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:049 -->

### Lines and images move within; good fortune and misfortune appear without. Accomplishment and work appear in change; the sage's intent appears in the statements.[^j15-within-without]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:049 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:050 -->

**Original Meaning.** “Within” means within the stalks and figures; “without” means outside them. “Change” is the change that moves within; “statements” are the statements that appear without.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:050 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:051 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:051 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:052 -->

**Han Kangbo:** Accomplishment and work arise through change; hence they appear in change. Each statement points toward its own direction; hence the word “intent.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:052 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:053 -->

**Master Zhang:** Through the movement that has already occurred in lines and images, good fortune and misfortune are made clear before they take form. Hence “lines and images move within; good fortune and misfortune appear without.” Following the changes of lines and images to bring their benefit into use makes accomplishment and work appear. The sage's intent lies simply in teaching people.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:053 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:054 -->

**Wu Cheng:** The sage's concern, shared with the people, is fully made manifest in the Changes. The sage's Way is given particular emphasis in the statements because this work is a commentary on the appended statements.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:054 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:055 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Lines and images move without visible form: hence “within.” Good fortune and misfortune become manifest and leave traces: hence “without.” This does not refer exclusively to stalk divination.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:055 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:056 -->

### Heaven and Earth's great virtue is called generation; the sage's great treasure is called position. How is position preserved? Through humaneness. How are people gathered? Through material resources. Ordering resources, rectifying statements, and restraining the people from wrongdoing are called rightness.[^j15-humaneness-people]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:056 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:057 -->

**Original Meaning.** The character ren in “through people” is written “humaneness” in current texts. Master Lü follows the old reading. It is what is meant by “without the multitude, there is no one with whom to preserve the state.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:057 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:058 -->

**Original Meaning — Chapter Summary.** This is Chapter One. It discusses hexagrams and lines, good fortune and misfortune, creative transformation, and accomplishment and work.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:058 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:059 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:059 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:060 -->

**Lu Ji:** Without material resources, people do not gather. Therefore the sage observes images and fashions implements, providing things and fulfilling their benefits, so as to give the myriad people their livelihoods and gather them together. This takes hold of the foundation of gathering people.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:060 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:061 -->

**Cui Jing:** In practicing the Way of the Changes, the sage must model Heaven and Earth's great virtue and treasure the great position of a ruler of ten thousand chariots. What is treasured is relieving the world through the Way: this is his great treasure. Wealth and goods are what people desire and love. Unless they are ordered through rightness, ruin must follow. Speech is people's pivotal instrument. Unless it is rectified through rightness, disgrace must follow. When the common people do wrong, unless they are restrained through rightness, they will not reform. All three depend on rightness. Acting through it attains what is fitting. Thus we know that humaneness and rightness are essential to the sage's treasuring of his position.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:061 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:062 -->

**Master Zhang:** Since the text is about to set out the ordering of resources and the nourishing of things below, it first describes Heaven and Earth's generation of things.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:062 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:063 -->

**Zhu Zhen:** Heaven and Earth's great virtue, called generation, is humaneness. The sage fulfills his position between the two through humaneness alone. Without it, he could not stand alongside Heaven and Earth. Rightness is the means of realizing humaneness; they are not two separate foundations. Hence “the Way of humanity is established through humaneness and rightness.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:063 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:064 -->

**Wang Zongchuan:** What enables the sage to match Heaven and Earth and rule the world is likewise only humaneness and rightness. Humaneness is virtue in use; rightness supports humaneness. Ordering resources includes making nets for hunting and fishing, making plow shafts and shares for tilling and weeding, and bringing people and goods together for exchange. Rectifying statements includes replacing knotted cords with written contracts, through which the officials govern and the myriad people examine matters. Restraining wrongdoing includes double gates and the striking of night-watch clappers against violent visitors, and pointed arrows and strung bows to awe the world.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:064 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:065 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu:** Rectifying statements is simply distinguishing right from wrong. He also said: Moral teaching is already included in rectifying statements.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:065 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:066 -->

**Xiang Anshi:** The sage's humaneness is Heaven and Earth's generation. “The great treasure is position” means the same as “nothing is more exalted than wealth and rank.” The following account makes this concrete through Baoxi, Shennong, the Yellow Emperor, Yao, and Shun: all sages possessing wealth and rank. “Resources” is the collective name for the hundred things, all of benefit to the people. Ordering resources means attending to water, fire, metal, wood, earth, and grain, and thereby benefiting the people. Rectifying statements means differentiating high and low with due measure, making taking and giving accord with rightness, and distinguishing names and realities so that there is trust. Wherever benefit is involved, people must be guided to understand rightness. Restraining wrongdoing means publishing prohibitions and carrying out punishments to regulate those who cannot be guided. First nourish them, then teach them, and then bring them into order: the sage's government is exhausted in these three. The intention from which his virtue issues is chiefly to treat the people humanely. Rightness is humaneness manifest in due ordering.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:066 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:067 -->

**Zhen Dexiu:** The Changes speaks of humaneness and rightness together only in this chapter and in the Discussion of the Trigrams' passage on establishing Heaven's Way. In Heaven and Earth it is called generation; in the sage it is called humaneness. The meaning of humaneness can be recognized from this.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:067 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:068 -->

**Li Xinchuan:** Cai Yong says, “Preserve position through humaneness; gather people through resources.” Thus the character for humaneness was already in use by the Han period.[^j15-han-reading]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:068 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:069 -->

**General Discussion — Kong Yingda:** This first chapter further explains the images, lines, firmness, yieldingness, good fortune, misfortune, regret, and humiliation discussed in Chapter Two of the upper Appended Statements, making the account fuller and more detailed.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:069 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:070 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** This chapter corresponds to Chapter Two of the upper Commentary. From Chapter Three onward, the upper Commentary expands that second chapter; from Chapter Two onward, the lower Commentary expands this first chapter. “The eight trigrams are arrayed” and “extend them by doubling” mean establishing the figures and observing their images. Appending statements of good fortune, misfortune, regret, and humiliation on the basis of the changes in which firm and yielding press upon one another within the lines and images means “appending statements to make good fortune and misfortune clear.” These four clauses proceed from images to statements: the sequence of making the Changes. What follows proceeds back from the statements' good fortune, misfortune, regret, and humiliation to the foundation in firm and yielding images. The Commentary was originally written to explain the appended statements, and the lower Commentary is especially detailed; hence this manner of exposition. The four outcomes arise from movement because firm and yielding operate by turns, changing in accordance with the time. Encounters with good fortune and misfortune are uneven, yet the constant principle prevails and the world's movements can be brought to unity. This is because the changes of firm and yielding never depart from Qian and Kun: Qian's ease and Kun's simplicity attain the principles of the world. The four clauses beginning “lines and images move within” bring the account together. In the Original Meaning, the passage on Heaven and Earth's great virtue belongs to this chapter. Many scholars, however, place it at the beginning of the next: the thirteen hexagrams invoked there concern nothing other than ordering resources, rectifying statements, and restraining wrongdoing. Their account may be followed.[^j15-chapter-boundary]

## Chapter 2 · Making Implements and Benefiting the People

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:071 -->

### In ancient times, when Baoxi ruled all under Heaven, he looked upward to observe images in Heaven and downward to observe patterns on Earth. He observed the markings of birds and beasts and what was fitting to the land. Near at hand he took examples from the body; farther afield, from things. Thus he first made the eight trigrams, to communicate the virtue of numinous illumination and to classify the dispositions of the myriad things.[^j15-baoxi]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:071 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:072 -->

**Original Meaning.** Wang Zhaosu says that many texts have the character “Heaven” between “and” and “Earth.” What was taken from above and below, near and far, was not all of one kind; yet it did no more than verify the two poles of yin and yang in their waning and waxing. The virtue of numinous illumination means such natures as vigor, compliance, movement, and stillness; the dispositions of the myriad things mean such images as thunder, wind, mountain, and lake.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:072 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:073 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:073 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:074 -->

**Zhu Zhen:** From this point onward, the text explains that providing things, bringing them into use, and establishing completed implements for the world's benefit all draw upon the Changes. All are humaneness. “Ruling all under Heaven” makes clear the preservation of position.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:074 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:075 -->

**Wang Shenzi:** Fuxi continued Heaven's work and established the ultimate standard, drawing the eight trigrams to guide the people's use. Later sages arose in succession and made implements through which people could sustain and nourish one another. All extended Heaven and Earth's virtue of unceasing generation: everything from nets to written contracts belongs to this.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:075 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:076 -->

**Cai Qing:** Of the two phrases “communicate the virtue of numinous illumination” and “classify the dispositions of the myriad things,” one concerns the subtle and the other the coarse; one concerns natures and dispositions, the other bodily forms. The images honored in the following thirteen hexagrams all arise from these two.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:076 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:077 -->

### He knotted cords to make nets for hunting and fishing. This was presumably drawn from Clinging.[^j15-inventions-images]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:077 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** This chapter explains “the Judgment gives the materials” and traces its principle back to unity. As soon as the eight trigrams are formed, they divide into yin and yang. A trigram with one single line is yang because it has one ruler and two subjects: the ruler's authority issues from one source, and the ruler governs. When the ruler governs, the noble person's Way is practiced; hence “the noble person's Way.” A trigram with one divided line is yin because it has two rulers and one subject: the ruler's authority issues from two sources, and it is as though the subject instead governs. When the subject governs, the petty person's Way is practiced; hence “the petty person's Way.” Past and present interpreters say that yang is the noble person and yin the petty person, treating these as designations of good and evil, virtue and wickedness. Do they not know that yin and yang cannot exist without one another, just as a ruler cannot be without subjects? Where, in that relation itself, is a division into good and evil? Only when the ruler's Way is unified and has a governing center do the many subjects join in obedience: is this not the noble person's Way? If the ruler's Way is divided between two and commands come from many doors, the subjects below each pursue their private ends: is this not the petty person's Way? Good and evil, virtue and wickedness arise from this; good fortune and misfortune, order and disorder begin here. These images are already present in the three-line figures. Extend this rule to six-line figures: Return, The Army, Modesty, Enthusiasm, Holding Together, and Splitting Apart all take one yang as their governing line and belong to the noble person's Way. Coming to Meet, Treading, and Breakthrough take one yin as governing and belong to the petty person's Way. Only the second line of Fellowship and the fifth of Great Possession are not treated as petty people, because their centered positions enable them to unite with yang and possess yang. Small Restraint's fourth is likewise not treated as a petty person, because its proper position enables it to restrain yang. Ultimately, yang remains the governing principle. Extend and adapt these interpretive rules, and a solitary yin or yang is not the only possible governing line. Whenever yang occupies the interior and has the time, it governs, as in Approach and Peace; whenever yin occupies the interior and has the time, it governs, as in Retreat and Obstruction. A yang trigram governing from within brings order; a yin trigram governing from within brings disorder, as in Peace, Obstruction, Decrease, and Increase. A yang trigram inside, preceding yin, is correct; a yin trigram inside, preceding yang, is improper, as in Following, Decay, Gradual Progress, and The Marrying Maiden. Sometimes inner and outer are not taken, but upper and lower are taken as noble and humble; sometimes before and after are not taken, but high and low are taken as leading and following. Sometimes yin represents the minister's Way and its compliance with yang is good; sometimes yin represents the ruler's Way and its response to yang is admirable. In sum, the intention to honor yang is one throughout. Through the eight trigrams the Master sets out general principles, so that people may extend them through the meanings of the sixty-four hexagrams. These are the materials of a hexagram, what its Judgment draws upon. Hence “the Judgment gives the materials.” Its conclusion is that the Way of yang must not be divided between two sources; thus we speak of the unity of principle.[^j15-trigram-rules]

**Editorial Judgment.** Considered together, these explanations are exceptionally detailed. Yet something remains to be supplied. They all take nearness to mean adjacent lines, which does not exhaust the interpretive rules of the Changes. Corresponding lines are distant, but the very fact of being called corresponding means that, though distant in place, they are near in disposition. Earlier scholars appear to have followed the previous chapter's “the fourth often fears, because it is near” and “the Way of the yielding does not favor distance,” and therefore insist on adjacency as nearness. But that passage compares second and fourth, which differ in distance. Here all six lines are considered together, and both adjacency and correspondence are near. Look at Unformed Understanding's fourth six: “alone it is far from substance,” because both its adjacent and corresponding lines are yin. Had it a correspondent, even without adjacency, it could not be called far from substance. Thus corresponding lines are sometimes called marriage partners, kin, rulers, or kindred: all names of closeness. For “far and near seek one another” to be complete, absence of both adjacency and correspondence must be distinguished as distance, and the presence of either as nearness.[^j15-nearness]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4706:497 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4706:498 -->

**Editorial Judgment, continued.** Distance and nearness among the dispositions of the Changes certainly arise from the lines' positions. But where do love and aversion, genuine and false, arise? One must know that the good fortune and misfortune of the lines are drawn from three terms: time, position, and virtue. Time changes according to the hexagram's meaning; with changing times come love and aversion. In the time of Peace there is intercourse; in Obstruction, separation; in Holding Together, harmony; in Contention, dispute. Thus love and aversion contending arise from time. Position differs among the six lines; with different positions come distance and nearness. Holding Together's inward and outward association, and Viewing's contemplation of the kingdom's splendor, are near. Unformed Understanding's entrapment in folly and Return's lost return are distant. Thus far and near seeking one another arise from position. Virtue differs according to the fitness of firm and yielding; with different virtues come genuine and false. Fellowship's fifth-line wailing and Enthusiasm's second-line firmness as stone come from centered correctness. Fellowship's third-line concealed troops and Enthusiasm's third-line upward-looking delight come from lack of centered correctness. Thus the genuine and false influencing one another arise from virtue. Time has the changes of waning and waxing, fullness and emptiness; position has the differences of noble and humble, upper and lower; virtue has the distinctions of firm and yielding, good and evil. These three are all roots of good fortune, misfortune, regret, and humiliation. Yet their activation always begins through engagement between oneself and another. Such engagement means adjacency and correspondence. Without adjacency or correspondence, there is no mutual contending, choosing, or influencing. All three concern these relations; hence the text singles out nearness without accord to show the rule. Nearness with accord means mutual love, mutual choosing, and genuine influence: the best of what is good. Lack of accord at a distance means that, despite aversion, there can be no mutual attack; without nearness there can be no mutual choosing; despite falsity there is no mutual influence. This is the next best. Those who should accord but are distant cannot draw close despite love, cannot choose one another without nearness, and cannot influence one another despite genuine feeling: this comes next. Only nearness without accord brings contention through aversion, choosing through proximity, and influence through falsity. The dangers and obstructions of human affairs are then complete. At their greatest comes misfortune, where aversion reaches its extreme, as with Fellowship's third line facing a firm opponent. Next comes harm, where the beginnings of falsity must be guarded against, as with Joy's boundary against sickness and trust in what strips away. Even the lighter case cannot escape regret and humiliation, as in the third lines of Enthusiasm and Gathering: though they follow the fourth because it is near, they are not of the same kind, hence regret and humiliation. The Changes teaches people to know danger and obstruction, so it particularly presents this case as an example; the others can be inferred as the remaining corners. Whoever studies the Changes must first understand the five terms time, position, virtue, adjacency, and correspondence. One must also know that the fitness or unfitness of time, position, and virtue becomes active through adjacency and correspondence. Nowhere is this meaning more fully given than in this chapter.[^j15-five-terms]

**Editorial Judgment.** This chapter likewise gathers the meaning of the preceding eleven and discusses them as a whole. ## Translator’s Endnotes

The base is the repository’s unchanged 易學網 text. Doubtful readings were compared selectively with [the Siku Quanshu transcription, fixed revision 759594](<https://zh.wikisource.org/w/index.php?title=御纂周易折中_(四庫全書本)/卷14&oldid=759594>). Corrections, repunctuation, historical proposals, and unresolved readings are distinguished below. Selected historical page images were also consulted, as identified in the notes. This is not a systematic facsimile collation. Added descriptive chapter headings are navigational, not translations of source headings.

[^j15-trigrams-lines]: 卦 can mean either a three-line trigram or a six-line hexagram. The English distinguishes them where the context does. 爻 here means the articulated roles of six-line positions, not a claim that a trigram literally lacks strokes. Zhu’s three successive transformations describe the generation of figures by adding strokes, not three extra rounds of a yarrow-stalk consultation. The source leaves Master Zheng’s personal name unspecified, and so does the translation.

[^j15-pushing-movement]: The base prints 相椎; the parallel Siku transcription reads 相推, supported also by the base’s own gloss and its quotation of upper Chapter Two. The English adopts 推, “press upon” or “push.” More importantly, Zhu reads 動 through the moving lines encountered in consultation; Su Jun and the compilers read it through movement in human affairs. Their disagreement is preserved rather than resolved by translating every occurrence as either “changing lines” or “action.”

[^j15-foundation-time]: 趣時 means moving toward, or answering to, the appropriate time, not opportunism. 體 and 用 are rendered through constitution or structure and activity or use, according to context. Firmness and yieldingness can remain stable categories even while individual lines change between them; the commentators are distinguishing those levels, not denying change.

[^j15-constant-prevails]: 貞勝 has three readings explicitly preserved here: Master Zhang’s “correctness prevails”; Zhu’s “the two constantly prevail over one another”; and the compilers’ “what is constant prevails.” The primary rendering follows the compilers without replacing Zhu’s account in his own commentary. Their admission that the good can encounter misfortune is important: this is a claim about a normative constant, not a denial of contrary experience. In the Conversations quotation the base’s 占曰 is corrected to 故曰 on the parallel transcription; that transcription itself has a disordered phrase later in the same paragraph, for which the coherent base is retained.

[^j15-qian-kun-show]: 隤然 here describes yielding ease, not collapse or moral decadence. The neighboring gloss explicitly defines it through 順. The claim of constancy concerns the generative and receptive principles; neither commentator is saying that weather and visible circumstances never vary.

[^j15-enacting-images]: The Chinese exploits sound and sense: 爻, “line,” with 效, “enact/model”; 象, “image,” with 像, “represent/resemble.” “Single and divided strokes” renders 奇耦 without making odd/even arithmetic the sole meaning.

[^j15-within-without]: Zhu locates inner and outer relative to the stalks and figures. The imperial judgment instead distinguishes unmanifest movement from visible effects and denies an exclusively divinatory reference. 情 here is the sage’s intent or concern, not necessarily a transient emotion.

[^j15-humaneness-people]: The base and parallel compilation print 仁, “humaneness,” while Zhu’s gloss recommends 人, “people,” following Master Lü. The source therefore preserves a genuine editorial disagreement. The primary translation retains 仁, and Zhu’s alternative is translated explicitly. 財 encompasses material resources and livelihoods, not merely money; 正辭 is broadened by the commentators into proper distinctions, public language, and written records. No change has been made to the Chinese source.

[^j15-han-reading]: Li Xinchuan’s wording 漢以前已用此仁字 is literally “this character was already used before/prior to the Han,” but his cited authority is Cai Yong of the Han. “By the Han period” conveys the evidential force without presenting a pre-Han dating as established. The chronology of this transmitted argument is not independently proven here.

[^j15-chapter-boundary]: The proposed relocation of the generation/resources passage is recorded, not carried out: all base blocks remain in order. The next chapter concerns twelve inventions associated with thirteen hexagrams, since clothing invokes Qian and Kun together. The base’s 變面從時 in this paragraph is corrected to 變而從時 on the parallel transcription.

[^j15-baoxi]: 包犧, rendered Baoxi in the canonical passage, is another transmitted name for Fuxi, whom the commentators name 伏羲 or 羲皇. The narrative is the tradition’s account of cultural origins, not an independently established chronology of inventions. The proposed 天 in 與天地之宜 is reported in Zhu’s note but not inserted into the primary text. 神明 is rendered “numinous illumination,” not identified with a single personal deity.

[^j15-inventions-images]: 蓋取諸 is kept as “presumably drawn from.” Hu Yuan explicitly denies that the statement must mean a historical inventor first consulted a hexagram and copied it. Kong and the compilers disagree with deriving implements from hexagram names; later glosses nevertheless sometimes use the name. That tension is part of this compilation and has not been harmonized.

[^j15-nets-gloss]: The paragraph beginning 古者網羅 has no named author in the base and follows the editorial judgment. “Further explanation” preserves that lack of explicit attribution rather than inventing a commentator. 目 can mean a net’s mesh as well as an eye, supporting the doubled Li image. The quoted Odes use 離 in the sense of being caught.

[^j15-market]: The wordplay in 噬/市 and 嗑/合 cannot be reproduced by English “Biting Through/market.” It is therefore explained in Zhu’s own gloss. Geng’s 得起所 is corrected to 得其所 on the parallel transcription; his account concerns exchange of surplus and need.

[^j15-robes-change]: “Let their robes and skirts hang down” retains the concrete expression 垂衣裳. The commentators interpret it through unforced government and ease and simplicity, not indolence. 神而化之 does not require miraculous intervention in this reading: their own explanations stress timely change without coercive contrivance. 倦 is weariness or exhaustion, not a claim that every novelty is beneficial.

[^j15-boats]: Zhu proposes deleting 致遠以利天下 here, presumably as a repetition of the following passage. He Kai instead explains both the nearby crossing and the distant journey. The complete base wording is retained; neither the proposed deletion nor the opposing interpretation is suppressed.

[^j15-gates]: 豫, the hexagram name rendered Enthusiasm elsewhere in this translation, also carries the sense of preparedness used in Zhu’s gloss. 柝 is a wooden watch-clapper. These images of protection belong to the text’s traditional institutional narrative, not a modern claim about the dates of invention.

[^j15-buildings]: 宇 is translated here according to Cai Yuan’s explicit gloss 椽, “rafters,” rather than its broader sense “building” or “eaves.” 上棟下宇 thus describes roof construction in the source’s own terms. Both physical configuration and hexagram name are used in the collected explanations, despite the earlier preference for images over names.

[^j15-burial]: 封 means raising a burial mound; 樹 refers to planting trees at the grave. 棺槨 distinguishes inner coffin and outer casing. The compilers’ 入而後說 also recalls lower Xun as entry and upper Dui as delight. Their belief that the dead find repose in earth is preserved as the commentary’s view, not recast as an empirically established statement about death.

[^j15-image-boundary]: The proposed division after this sentence would attach it to the inventions chapter. It is not implemented: the base’s chapter sequence and all blocks remain unchanged. 吳澄’s “opening passage of this chapter” reflects his different division, not a translator’s renumbering. Bao Ba (保八) is retained as named in the compilation’s commentator index.

[^j15-judgment-materials]: 材 means a hexagram’s constitutive material or capacities. The compilers explicitly compare it with building timber, so “materials” preserves their analogy. 彖 here is the hexagram Judgment, not the later Commentary on the Judgments as a separate Wing.

[^j15-odd-even]: Zhu’s base gloss has 一陰二陰 for the yang trigrams; the parallel reads 一陽二陰, which agrees with all three named figures. The English adopts the attested correction. His five and four “strokes” count the two separate halves of a yin line individually: 1+2+2=5 and 2+1+1=4. Han, Chen, and the compilers instead explain odd/even through the governing line. These are distinct explanations, both preserved.

[^j15-ruler-subject]: The source maps ruler/subjects onto yang/yin and defends unified hierarchical authority. This is retained, not silently modernized. Its editorial judgment also explicitly rejects equating yin as such with moral evil: the moral distinction arises from the configuration of relations. Wu Yueshen’s claim that yang rules even a yin trigram uses 君 in the ruler/subject symbolism; it should not erase the preceding account of a yin line as 主, the figure’s governing element.

[^j15-trigram-rules]: These are the compilers’ flexible interpretive rules, with their stated exceptions, not a single mechanical majority rule. Their pairing of Peace/Obstruction, Decrease/Increase and the following figures should be read as examples of contrasting configurations. The base punctuation 夫子以八卦發，凡 is corrected to the ordinary phrase 發凡, “set out general principles,” without changing characters. 君子 and 小人 can carry both social-role and moral meanings here; the source moves between them.

[^j15-thinking]: The two occurrences of 天下何思何慮 are retained. They do not abolish thoughtful discrimination: Xu Ji explicitly says that thought belongs to the human heart, and Zhu later asks how refining rightness could occur without thought. What is rejected is 憧憧, restless private calculation that confines response to one’s circle. “Anxious deliberation” brings out that context without making all 慮 inherently a fault.

[^j15-contraction-extension]: 信 here functions as 伸, “extend,” not “trust.” The English keeps contraction/extension consistent through the natural and ethical examples. Sun, moon, cold, and heat are presented in the source’s cosmological sequence; the accompanying claim about the seasons is not offered as a modern astronomical explanation.

[^j15-refinement-practice]: 精義 involves refining one’s apprehension of what is fitting; 入神 marks an attainment of subtle responsiveness, not necessarily spirit possession or trance. 利用 here is making application beneficial, paired with 安身, settling the person. Zhu and Yu describe an inward/outward reciprocity; Kong repeatedly emphasizes stillness preceding movement. Their formulations are retained rather than rewritten as one teaching.

[^j15-beyond-effort]: The base’s 身不能己 in Zhu’s gloss is corrected to 自不能已 on the parallel Siku transcription: the reciprocal process “goes on of itself without ceasing.” “No one yet knows” is not an excuse to stop learning; the following commentators repeatedly insist on thorough preparatory cultivation and reject forcing its culmination. Hengqu is Zhang Zai, labeled Master Zhang in the surrounding attributions.

[^j15-unlabeled-note]: This short source sentence follows Zhu’s commentary but lacks an explicit label. It is translated as source matter, not promoted to canonical text or silently counted as a translator’s addition.

[^j15-eleven-lines]: The eleven line passages in this chapter begin with Influence 31.4 and continue through Increase 42.6. The final two are Decrease 41.3 and Increase 42.6, explaining the compilers’ reference to unity and constancy. The added English chapter heading does not alter this source sequence.

[^j15-oppression-reading]: The base’s title 朱於語類 is corrected to 朱子語類, confirmed by the parallel transcription. The warning about death belongs to the quoted line’s traditional interpretation; it is not a diagnosis or personal prediction about the reader.

[^j15-ready-implement]: 藏器於身 deliberately joins an external implement with capacities cultivated in the person. The metaphor is retained rather than replaced by a modern slogan about preparation. 括 is explicitly glossed as binding obstruction, not a container or a literal fastening to be manipulated.

[^j15-stocks]: The renderings of 屨校滅趾 and 何校滅耳 follow Juan Three: the stocks or collar conceal the toes or ears. Other traditions understand 滅 through injury; that dispute is discussed with the original hexagram. The present chapter offers the ethical contrast of early restraint and accumulated wrongdoing, not an additional procedural account of punishment.

[^j15-security]: Kong’s first 心恒不志 is corrected to 心恒不忘 on the parallel transcription and the paragraph’s repeated pattern. 苞桑 retains the image of a firmly rooted mulberry clump. Gu’s main cord and meshes are a net metaphor for coordinated government, not omitted as a generic statement of “organization.”

[^j15-cauldron]: The quotation retains 其形渥 and the Juan Seven rendering “his body is drenched.” The alternative transmission involving punishment is not silently substituted here. 台鼎 and 三公 refer to the highest ministerial offices; their imagery is kept distinct from the cauldron itself.

[^j15-first-stirrings]: 幾 is the incipient movement through which an outcome first becomes discernible. Zhu reports the Han History’s 吉凶, “good fortune and misfortune,” whereas this base reads only 吉, and Kong explicitly defends that reading. The primary follows the base; the reported variant is not inserted. Kong’s 衛纖微 is corrected to 唯纖微, “only the finest subtlety,” on the parallel transcription. 慎獨 concerns vigilance over what is inward and not yet publicly apparent, not a demand for social isolation.

[^j15-yan-hui]: “Yan’s son” is Yan Hui, also called Master Yan in these glosses. Shuji, 庶幾, means “nearly” in Zhu’s account; he rejects making 幾 here another technical occurrence of “first stirrings.” Zhu’s surprising gloss 殆危也, “dai means peril,” is shared by the consulted transcription and is retained as his gloss. The English primary takes the whole phrase idiomatically as “very nearly there” rather than inventing a claim that Yan’s virtue itself is dangerous. The precise force of 殆 in this gloss remains a philological question.

[^j15-union]: 絪緼 and the later 姻緼 describe intimate mingling. The source distinguishes 氣化, generation through qi, from 形化, generation through embodied union. These are its cosmological categories, not a modern biological taxonomy. The singular companion in Decrease is related to the commentary’s account of undivided pairing, not a general prohibition on groups of three.

[^j15-three-preparations]: The three preparations deliberately parallel person, heart, and relationship. 與 has different senses in the first and third warnings, made explicit by Xiang: support versus giving. Guo construes fear as awareness of one’s own departure from principle. This is the commentator’s contextual ethical account, not a claim that ordinary fear or anxiety proves moral wrongdoing.

[^j15-unity-reading]: Wu Yiyuan’s base 各顯微之一致 is corrected to 知顯微之一致 on the parallel transcription, matching the repeated “knowing the unity of…” clauses. The contrasting examples are preserved in full; they do not collapse the practical differences between acting, waiting, correcting a fault, and accepting responsibility.

[^j15-gate]: 撰 is glossed as 事, “workings/affairs,” not literary authorship. 體 occurs first as a structure or embodiment, then as the verb “embody”; He Kai explicitly links the two. 門 continues the gate metaphor developed in the upper Appended Statements and is not replaced with an abstract “key.”

[^j15-names-decline]: 紂 is the Shang ruler Zhou, not the Zhou dynasty 周. The claims about Fuxi, King Wen, and the naming of hexagrams belong to the commentators’ competing accounts of textual formation. The question particle and Hou’s deliberate caution are retained; the translation does not turn the canonical question into a modern historical certainty.

[^j15-obscure-passage]: Zhu doubts the transmitted order 而微顯 and the wording 開而. The compilers instead defend 開而 through King Wen’s opening out the meaning in names and statements. The English follows that intelligible exposition without altering the source or claiming a recovered archetype. “Renders the manifest subtle” means tracing evident affairs to their subtle principles, as the following glosses explain; it does not mean deliberately making clear matters obscure.

[^j15-doubt-conduct]: 貳 literally suggests division or doubleness and is glossed as doubt. 濟 preserves the image of bringing across, made explicit by Cheng Jingcheng’s rescue from entrapment. 報 is rendered consequences here; the text is not automatically asserting a separate doctrine of supernatural reward and punishment.

[^j15-source-uncertainty]: The statement that many readings cannot be understood throughout is Zhu’s transmitted chapter summary. It is translated in full and labeled as commentary, not attributed to the canonical author or used to omit difficult passages. The English still represents every base block.

[^j15-makers-concern]: The source distinguishes the maker’s personal hardship from compassionate concern for the world. Gu and He deliberately emphasize the latter; Kong and Wu connect the account with King Wen’s imprisonment. All are retained. “Middle age” translates 中古 within this traditional chronology, not the European Middle Ages.

[^j15-nine-virtues]: The nine are Treading 10, Modesty 15, Return 24, Constancy 32, Decrease 41, Increase 42, Oppression 47, The Well 48, and Penetration 57. 基, 柄, 本, 固, 脩, 裕, 辨, 地, 制 are deliberately distinct: foundation, handle, root, firmness, cultivation, abundance, testing/distinction, ground, and discerning decision. The last cannot be reduced to passive yielding. 鄭康成 is Zheng Xuan, identified here by the source’s courtesy name.

[^j15-xun-weighing]: 權 is the weighing needed to judge exceptional circumstances, not arbitrary expediency or the abandonment of principle. Xun’s ability to enter the finest particulars grounds it in the following explanations. In Zhu’s colloquial 斷得殺, the final word intensifies thorough decision; the English does not manufacture an instruction to kill. The “resources and axe” image is discussed with Penetration’s upper line in Juan Eight.

[^j15-nine-qualities]: 稱 means weighing or fitting the measure, not merely naming. 遷 in the Well’s description is its benefit reaching others, rather than the physical well relocating. The nine paired qualities are translated in their original order so that their differences remain visible.

[^j15-ritual-witness]: The uncommon 禮非強世, “ritual propriety does not force the world,” is present in both transcriptions. It was also checked visually in the historical page image hosted at https://www.eee-learning.com/eeebook/middleyi/1698.png, which likewise reads 強世. It has therefore not been silently changed to the tempting 強制. The image is a selectively consulted witness; no printing date or complete facsimile collation is claimed.

[^j15-return-discernment]: Zhu reads Return’s small yang as distinct from the surrounding yin; Han and Lu read the discrimination of incipient moral movement. The primary wording remains capable of both, and the compilers expressly prefer Zhu. In Lu’s long gloss the base 故曰遷巽。稱而隱 is repunctuated as 故曰遷。巽稱而隱, so that 巽 belongs to the ninth hexagram rather than the Well. His earlier 故日益 is read as 故曰益, supported by the parallel transcription.

[^j15-nine-practices]: The third presentation turns each hexagram into an application through 以. Lu explicitly distinguishes cultivating virtue from demanding external rewards: reducing harm to virtue does not guarantee immunity from misfortune, and blessings do not increase the noble person’s intrinsic worth. These qualifications are preserved, not replaced with promises of favorable results.

[^j15-judgment-form]: Xiang’s 彖辭 here describes a pattern of explaining a name, the two constituent trigrams, and their application. “Judgment texts” leaves room for this broader use, rather than forcing every occurrence of 彖 into an identical modern editorial category. His threefold structure is his explanation of the three presentations, not an extra chapter division.

[^j15-sequence-counts]: Hu’s numerical account is retained as transmitted in both consulted texts. Several numbers are intervals rather than inclusive totals: 1→10 is nine steps, 10→15 five, 15→24 nine, 24→32 eight, and 48→57 nine. He also groups Decrease/Increase and Oppression/The Well as pairs. The phrase from Constancy to Decrease and Increase is not a uniform inclusive count of individual hexagrams; nor are Penetration through Before Completion alone eight forward steps unless the return to Qian is included. These compressed counting conventions should not be mistaken for a simple list-length calculation, and no numbers have been silently changed.

[^j15-three-presentations]: Ye’s base 二陳, “twice presents,” is corrected to 三陳, “three times presents,” on the parallel transcription. The three canonical presentations and Zhu’s chapter summary confirm the reading. The following editorial account of King Wen’s naming is explicitly an inference within the source, not an independently verified conclusion of this translation.

[^j15-six-vacancies]: The six vacancies are unoccupied structural positions, not a claim that the world is unreal. The commentators distinguish an enduring position from the changing yin or yang line that occupies it. 典要 is a fixed prescriptive rule or digest; the later 典常 names the constancy of principle within change.

[^j15-no-fixed-manual]: Zhu’s comparison with Yang Xiong’s 太玄 is translated as his polemical account, including its transmitted numbers. The important explicit qualification is that correct yin/yang position and correspondence do not mechanically guarantee good fortune. The translation does not turn this passage into a new formula for judging every cast.

[^j15-measure]: Zhu flags this sentence as textually doubtful. Han, Pan, and Cai offer coherent explanations through measured conduct and caution. The English gives that intelligible sense while retaining Zhu’s warning; no conjectural Chinese reconstruction is presented as an attested reading.

[^j15-tutor-parents]: 師保 are the traditional tutor and guardian. Zhu reads a literal absence of such instructors together with an inward sense of parental presence; the compilers read a change of perception from strict instruction to parental care. The compressed primary wording is left open enough to preserve both. The historical image 1707.png was consulted during locating the preceding passage, but no complete folio-by-folio review is claimed.

[^j15-person-practice]: 苟非其人 does not mean that only a person with an inherited status may read the book. In these glosses it means the person capable of embodying and practicing the principle. 道不虛行 denies that textual knowledge alone performs the Way’s work. The word “right” in the English marks fitness for that work, not a translator’s claim to such authority.

[^j15-whole-hexagram]: 質 is explicitly explained as the hexagram’s structural whole. 時物 joins the line’s time with what it represents; “matters” follows Kong’s gloss 事 without making the phrase refer only to physical objects. Gu and He weight lines and Judgments differently, and both accounts are retained.

[^j15-first-last]: 初 and 上 mean the first and top lines, not simply early and late passages in the book. The compilers dispute assigning 擬 and 成 to the text’s ancient makers and interpret the whole statement through the learner. The English primary avoids an inserted agent so that the alternatives remain legible.

[^j15-middle-lines]: The base’s 若夾 is corrected to 若夫 on the parallel transcription. 中爻 here means lines two through five, as Zhu explicitly states—not only the two centered trigram positions, second and fifth. 雜物撰德 is difficult compact language; the following editorial gloss supplies the translated sense of comparison and assessment rather than a conjectural change of wording.

[^j15-more-than-half]: 思過半 is “more than half” the work of understanding, not complete certainty. The later general discussion itself emphasizes the caution of 要, 多, and 過半: essentials, frequent tendencies, and more than half. The net and balance are the compilers’ own images for reading lines within the whole hexagram.

[^j15-second-fourth]: “Near” is near the fifth, conventionally the ruler’s position. 同功 first compares the yin positions, not an assertion that the actual occupants are always yin. The compilers explicitly criticize that restriction. 多 is translated “often,” preserving a tendency rather than a guarantee; the question and exceptions later reinforce this caution.

[^j15-sixteen]: Cheng Jiong’s sixteen refers to fixing the second line as nine and the fifth as six while the other four can vary: 2^4 gives sixteen figures. His fifteen auspicious cases are his interpretive claim, not independently certified outcome data. He is discussing ruler–minister responsiveness, not prescribing that every such cast is favorable.

[^j15-third-fifth]: The final question particle is retained. Hou explicitly says it leaves room for a yielding line to be auspicious at the right time. The base 則正 in Hou’s gloss is corrected to 剛正, “firm correctness,” on the parallel transcription. Wu, Hu, and Hou disagree whether the final clause concerns the fifth, third, or both; all three are translated.

[^j15-interpretive-caution]: The leather bindings breaking three times is the traditional image of repeated study of a bound text. He Kai invokes it to emphasize caution, and explicitly names the text’s qualified expressions: 要, 多, 過半. These should not be read as unqualified predictive laws. “Middle” in the following editorial discussion refers to the four interior lines, not only second and fifth as trigram centers.

[^j15-three-powers]: The Three Powers are Heaven, humanity, and Earth. Zhu assigns them to pairs of lines, not to three additional entities outside the hexagram. Xiang insists that doubling reflects their own twofold structure rather than an arbitrary numerical scheme.

[^j15-pattern-fitness]: 文 is patterned interweaving, not only a written text. Zhu and Xiang initially define fitness through yin/yang position; Wang, He, and Wu then expressly qualify or reject treating formal position as sufficient. Kong’s statement that good fortune and misfortune do not arise where there is no mutual harm is translated as he gives it, not silently replaced by an invented opposite clause.

[^j15-wary-careless]: 殷 is Yin, another name for Shang; 周 is the Zhou lineage, while 紂 names the Shang ruler. 易 here means careless ease, in contrast with the principled ease praised as Qian’s virtue below. The question-form of the traditional dating is retained. The passage is an ethical account of caution and negligence, not a guarantee that cautious people never suffer.

[^j15-causation-punctuation]: Zhao’s base punctuation breaks 使之不可作易。使之. The English repunctuates the unchanged characters as 使之不可作「易使之」: one must not construe it as “the Changes makes it happen.” His following explanation that wariness itself brings safety supports this reading. The parallel has the same character sequence, so this is a disclosed interpretive repunctuation, not a separately recovered textual variant.

[^j15-ease-danger]: 易 is principled ease in Qian, but careless ease in the preceding warning. The word must not be flattened into a single moral value. Zhu distinguishes danger below from obstruction above; Xiang also reads danger as treacherous human conditions. Zhu’s base 不陷不險 is corrected to 不陷於險, “does not fall into danger,” on the parallel transcription. Cai takes Qian and Kun here as names for human virtue; this is his interpretation, not an added universal subject.

[^j15-lords-deliberations]: The base and consulted Siku transcription both print 能研諸侯之慮, which literally includes “the deliberations of the lords.” Zhu explicitly deletes 侯之, producing 能研諸慮, “refine deliberation”; the following commentators use that shorter wording. The primary English retains the base’s extra words, while Zhu’s deletion and the shorter interpretation are translated in the commentary. This is a transmitted editorial proposal, not a silently established correction. 亹亹 denotes earnest, sustained endeavor, not idle curiosity about an oracle.

[^j15-signs]: 象事 and 占事 are kept distinct: representing affairs to know their implements, and divining affairs to know what is coming. He Kai’s 志一之動氣 recalls Mencius, Gongsun Chou I, where concentrated intent moves qi; compare the primary text in 四書章句集註/孟子集注卷三 (https://zh.wikisource.org/zh-hant/四書章句集註/孟子集注卷三). His application to auspicious signs is his own cosmological interpretation, not an independently demonstrated predictive mechanism.

[^j15-deliberation-before-divination]: Cai explicitly places consultation with people before consultation of the oracle, even for the sage. 鬼謀, “spirits deliberate,” is the traditional divinatory counterpart, not a translator’s assertion of independently observed spirit agency. 能 is capacity or effective ability; “completes” and “shares” deliberately preserve the distinction explained by Zhu and Hu.

[^j15-dispositions-statements]: 情 can mean the sage’s intent and the actual dispositions or relations embodied in the lines. The following discussion moves between these senses. Cui’s base 象謂卦下辭 is corrected to 彖謂卦下辭 on the parallel transcription, restoring “Judgment means the statement beneath a hexagram.” In Cai’s next gloss the parallel’s 離居 is not followed: the coherent base 雜居, “dwell intermingled,” agrees with the canonical passage.

[^j15-relational-outcomes]: 情 shifts between actual relations, dispositions, genuine feeling, and the sage’s intention. 情偽 specifically contrasts genuine and false. 比 is adjacency; 應 is correspondence, conventionally between first/fourth, second/fifth, and third/top. The commentators do not all restrict “near” to physical adjacency: the compilers’ extension to correspondence is central, not a minor verbal variant.

[^j15-three-relations]: Xiang organizes the three pairs of outcomes by stages of engagement: influencing, choosing, and contending. Cai later ranks them by gravity. These are different explanatory frameworks, both retained. Xiang’s 不以近相取, “failing to choose the nearby,” is present in both consulted transcriptions; its negative has not been removed to make it match the later formulation. Lin’s account of accord at a distance versus discord nearby is likewise retained rather than harmonized.

[^j15-nearness]: The compilers distinguish distance in place from closeness in relation. Their example 蒙六四, “alone far from substance,” refers to the absence of effective yang support both adjacent and corresponding; 實 here is the solid yang line. Their claim is not that all correspondents always cooperate: the point is that correspondence creates a relation in which agreement or conflict can become active.

[^j15-five-terms]: 時、位、德、比、應 are the compilers’ five interpretive terms: time, position, virtue, adjacency, correspondence. They insist that the first three become effective through the last two. “Time” is the situation expressed by the hexagram, not necessarily a calendar date; “virtue” includes the line’s mode of firmness/yieldingness and its ethical fitness. The quoted shorthand for Joy refers to its fourth and fifth lines; the third-line examples at Enthusiasm and Gathering remain distinct. The long explanation is source commentary, not an added translator’s method.

[^j15-speech]: 枝 literally branches; 游 wanders or evades; 屈 is constrained, bent, or unable to proceed freely. The images of speech are retained and unfolded by Wang’s gloss. This is a traditional ethical typology used to interpret the oracle’s language, not a reliable modern test for deception or a warrant to infer guilt from someone’s verbosity, hesitation, or anxiety.

[^j15-closing-reading]: Wu’s 巳以 in the base is corrected to 且以, “and also,” on the parallel transcription. He links the ending to the source’s opening statement about the sage’s intent. Translator’s notes remain separate from the imperial Editorial Judgments: neither the five-term account nor the final synthesis is the translator’s own commentary.
