## Imperial Preface to the Balanced Interpretations of the Zhou Changes

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4532:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4532:002 -->

The learning of the Changes is vast and all-embracing. Yet after the Qin and Han, its subtle essence was no longer attained! From the Song onward, Zhou, Shao, Cheng, and Zhang brought its profound meanings to light; Master Zhu alone united images and numbers with Heaven’s principle, establishing his conclusions against prevailing opinion. For more than five hundred years, there was no further contest over them.[^fm-scholars] From the Song, Yuan, and Ming down to our own dynasty, however, the subtle indications already disclosed by earlier scholars became occasions for arguments advancing individual views, and gradually gave rise to doubts among later readers. From my earliest years I have devoted myself to the meaning of the classics; for more than fifty years, I have never ceased, even briefly. I recognized the heterogeneous character of the various Complete Compendia, yet lacked the practiced mastery that comes from specializing in a single classic. Knowing well that Grand Secretary Li Guangdi’s long-standing scholarship had firm foundations and that his understanding of the Changes was exact and thorough, I specially commanded him to compile the Balanced Interpretations of the Zhou Changes. Tracing upward the roots and ramifications of the River Chart and Luo Writing, and extending downward to the critical determinations of the many scholars, it was to weigh these against what cannot be altered in a comprehensive understanding of the classic, and select accordingly.[^fm-preface] Through two cycles of cold and heat, I examined it during the first watch of the night, weighing every word and every stroke without slackening. Completed in the spring of Kangxi’s fifty-fourth year, it is now transmitted to the world and to later generations. Those who make sound learning their undertaking will surely find something here for themselves!

<a id="editorial-principles"></a>

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4535:001 -->

## Editorial Principles of the Imperially Compiled Balanced Interpretations of the Zhou Changes

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4535:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4535:002 -->

**Article One.** In antiquity, the two parts of the Classic of Changes and the ten parts of its Commentaries were not intermingled. Fei Zhi and Wang Bi attached the Commentaries to the classic, and Master Cheng followed them. Later, Lü Dafang, Chao Shuozhi, Lü Zuqian, and other scholars held that the old arrangement should be restored. The text underlying Master Zhu’s Original Meaning was Zuqian’s edition. At the beginning of the Ming, Cheng’s Commentary and Zhu’s explanations were used together; since Cheng came earlier, he was placed before Zhu. Thus, in compiling the Complete Compendium, its editors broke up Original Meaning to follow the arrangement of Cheng’s Commentary. Our judgment is that the study of the Changes should take Master Zhu as its principal guide. We therefore place Original Meaning first, and likewise follow its original edition throughout in the sequence of classic and commentaries. We hope that readers will thereby see the ancient classic again, rather than becoming so accustomed to recent arrangements that they forget its source.[^fm-arrangement]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4535:002 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4535:003 -->

**Article Two.** What the scholars have said about the composition and transmission of the Changes; the profundities of its principles and the main outlines of its meaning; how students should read it; and the general grounds of agreement and disagreement among its interpreters—all these later students should know at the outset. The Complete Compendium has one chapter of Guiding Principles, but preserves only the views of Cheng and Zhu. Our judgment is that Masters Zhou, Zhang, and Shao also penetrated deeply into the principles of the Changes. Although they left no continuous commentary on the whole classic, they often arrived independently at momentous meanings and subtle insights. Nor can we discard what scholars of successive ages have written about its origins and development or the direction of its teaching. These materials are therefore arranged in three chapters by historical succession and subject. They illuminate Cheng and Zhu’s words, and also show that Cheng and Zhu’s writings have both sources and subsequent developments. Bringing ancient and modern learning together establishes a common ground; it is not comparable to making one’s own mind the sole authority and setting up a novel doctrine.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4535:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4535:004 -->

**Article Three.** The words of the Changes have principles governing their interpretation. By examining the Master’s Commentary on the Judgments and Commentary on the Images, these can all be inferred. Since Master Wang’s General Remarks, scholars have elucidated them, but not comprehensively. We now set them out and distinguish them in some detail, to show students what matters in observing the images and dwelling upon the words. As they are the broad rules of the entire classic, they stand with the Guiding Principles in the preliminary juan.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4535:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4535:005 -->

**Article Four.** Among the various interpretations collected in the Complete Compendium, those of the Song and Yuan predominate. Our collection extends from the Han and Jin down to the Yuan and Ming, so that the sources and development of two thousand years of learning in the Way of the Changes may be surveyed. Zhu’s explanations stand first because Master Zhu uniquely grasped the original meaning of the Changes. Cheng’s Commentary follows because Master Cheng gives its moral principles their fullest exposition. These two masters truly continued the work of the four sages; their writings are therefore attached immediately after the classic.[^fm-four-sages] Among the other Han, Jin, Tang, Song, Yuan, and Ming scholars, insight varies in depth, and exposition in purity or admixture. We select whatever assists understanding of the classic and place it after Zhu and Cheng. Interpretations that differ decisively from theirs, yet deserve to be preserved as alternatives or to broaden the reader’s knowledge, are separately designated Supplements and placed last. To examine divergent accounts, leave doubtful matters open, and await later persons of learning—this too was Master Zhu’s intention.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4535:005 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4535:006 -->

**Article Five.** Interpreters of the Changes in the Han and Jin generally became absorbed in the later, derivative forms of image-and-number learning and strayed from its root. Thus, after the Sui and Tang, Wang Bi’s commentary alone prevailed: he could break through the crude practices of overlapping hexagrams, stem assignments, and manifest-and-hidden configurations, and devoted himself to principles in discussing the classic.[^fm-techniques] Yet the principles he attained were those of Laozi and Zhuangzi, not wholly in accord with the sages’ Way; once Cheng’s Commentary appeared, Wang’s explanations in turn fell out of use. Our judgment is that immersion in images and numbers which produces disjointed, rootless explanations may indeed be rejected. But the Changes was actually composed on the foundation of images and numbers; it is unlike other books that discuss moral principles alone. Since Jiao Gan and Jing Fang, however, forced interpretations became so excessive that scholars committed to principles came to dismiss images and numbers as beneath discussion. When Master Shao Kangjie appeared, his learning had a transmission, and what he elucidated concerning diagrams, hexagrams, and yarrow stalks was fundamental to the study of the Changes. How could one put it in the same category merely because it concerns images and numbers? Master Zhu therefore brought it to prominence and esteemed Shao alongside Cheng. His Original Meaning was in fact formed by drawing on both schools. Later students, whether speaking of principles or of images and numbers, need only use Master Zhu as their standard of judgment. Recent interpreters still gather remnants of numerological techniques and boast of their strangeness, unaware that they miss the truth of number. Others rehearse the dregs of practical reasoning and lapse into labored superficiality, unaware that they have lost principle’s subtlety. All such material has been omitted, to restore the teaching’s old purity, stillness, precision, and depth.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4535:006 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4535:007 -->

**Article Six.** Master Zhu’s learning came from Master Cheng, yet they often differed in interpreting words and meanings. This is true of all the classics, not only the Changes. Moreover, Cheng regarded the Changes as a book in which the sages explained principles, whereas Zhu regarded it as their teaching of divination: their orientations already differed. Nevertheless, everything Master Cheng expounds belongs to the Way of cultivating oneself, ordering the family, governing the state, and bringing peace to the world. Plain, exact, and substantial, it benefits students. Master Zhu himself said that Original Meaning was concise because Cheng’s Commentary had already fully treated moral principles. Our explanations of the classic and commentaries therefore take Original Meaning as their initial guide, while weighing its differences from Cheng’s Commentary. Beyond these two works, scholars of successive ages have elucidated their agreements and disagreements and supplied what they left untreated. We bring these accounts together, examine them closely, and append our considered judgments after the collected explanations. At times, where our predecessors have said nothing, I too put forward a thought of my own among them. Looking upward toward what is lofty, drilling into what is hard—how could I presume to be certain of myself? I hope only to embody our predecessors’ openness, impartiality, and freedom from self, and seek agreement with the one principle toward which different paths converge.[^fm-imperial-voice]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4535:007 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4535:008 -->

**Article Seven.** The Introduction to the Study of the Changes is a completed work by Master Zhu, complementary to Original Meaning. The few diagram explanations now printed in the Complete Compendium were brief summaries of essentials placed at the head of Original Meaning when that work was composed. The diagram of hexagram transformations was made to explain the transformations invoked under particular hexagrams in Original Meaning; its method differs from transformations in divinatory casting. Taken altogether, these are less complete than the Introduction. The Complete Compendium makes the diagram explanations its foundation and appends selections from the Introduction beneath them. Moreover, it draws only from Taking the Chart and Writing as the Foundation and Tracing the Origin of the Hexagram Strokes. From Explaining the Yarrow Stalks and Examining Change and Divination it prints neither text nor diagrams, substituting only the ceremonial procedure for casting and a different method of hexagram transformation. It therefore leaves students unable to see the full extent of Master Zhu’s discussion of images and numbers. Such treatment is unavoidably inadequate. We now include the Introduction in its entirety at the end of the book, approaching the ancient intention of having the text on one side and the diagrams on the other. Apart from my study of the classics, I have for many years given thought to the profundities of calendrical astronomy and the Nine Chapters, and have come to see clearly that they do not lie outside the Way of the Changes. I have therefore sketched and analyzed the origins of the River Chart and Luo Writing, the positions of Earlier and Later Heaven, and the Great Expansion’s methods of calculation and anticipation, unfolding their meanings. These are appended after the Introduction under the title Supplementary Discussions to the Introduction.[^fm-primer]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4535:008 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4535:009 -->

**Article Eight.** The Master’s Ten Wings conclude with the Sequence and Miscellaneous Hexagrams. Their order is subtle and close-knit; their varied interweaving forms a pattern. Scholars have long set them aside without exposition. Reflecting on Chen Xiyi’s indication concerning the reversal of nine hexagrams, I considered the meaning of the Sequence; through Shao Kangjie’s statement that the Four Images combine to form sixteen configurations, I apprehended the root of the Miscellaneous Hexagrams. Only then did I recognize the subtlety of the sages’ intention and the depth and precision of their words: they draw the bow but do not release the arrow. Like the array of stars and the interlacing of the seven moving luminaries, apparently uneven and disorderly, they await someone able to observe the heavens and calculate their motions to discover the reason. I therefore composed Clarifying the Meanings of the Sequence and Miscellaneous Hexagrams, placing it after the Supplementary Discussions to conclude the work.[^fm-seven-luminaries]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4537:001 -->

<p>
<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4537:002 -->We, your servants, respectfully note: the Balanced Interpretations of the Zhou Changes comprises twenty-two juan. In the fifty-fourth year of Kangxi,<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4537:002 -->
<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4537:003 -->it was imperially compiled; Grand Secretary Li Guangdi, your servant, and his colleagues received<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4537:003 -->
<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4537:004 -->the command to compile and collate it. From antiquity, interpreters of the Changes have divided between those who give priority to principle and those who give priority to number, each producing a work of his own. The Complete Compendium compiled during the Ming Yongle reign drew no further than the Song and Yuan, and was notably restricted and inadequate. Our<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4537:004 -->
<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4537:005 -->Sage Ancestor, the Humane Emperor, made a fresh selection from the many interpretations. In numerical learning, errors concerning stem assignments and manifest-and-hidden configurations were invariably rejected; in the exposition of principle, the empty doctrines of Laozi and Zhuangzi were invariably refuted. Analogies were extended and meanings developed, always with precision. The arrangement of the two parts of the classic and the Ten Wings was entirely restored to that of Original Meaning. Thus, the teachings of principle and number were united by Master Zhu; in this book, the tradition of Master Zhu attained its fullest expression. Respectfully collated and submitted in the ninth month of Qianlong’s thirty-ninth year.<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4537:005 -->

## Translator’s Endnotes

The base is the repository’s unchanged 易學網 front matter. Selective comparison used [the Siku transcription, revision 738673](<https://zh.wikisource.org/w/index.php?title=御纂周易折中_(四庫全書本)&oldid=738673>), [its Editorial Principles, revision 528517](<https://zh.wikisource.org/w/index.php?title=御纂周易折中_(四庫全書本)/凡例&oldid=528517>), and historical page images [0002](https://www.eee-learning.com/eeebook/middleyi/0002.png), [0003](https://www.eee-learning.com/eeebook/middleyi/0003.png), [0005](https://www.eee-learning.com/eeebook/middleyi/0005.png), and [0006](https://www.eee-learning.com/eeebook/middleyi/0006.png). The base’s shorter 1774 catalogue synopsis is not replaced by the parallel’s 1777 text. Supported corrections, retained variants, and an explicitly supplementary heading are distinguished. This is not systematic leaf-by-leaf facsimile collation.

[^fm-scholars]: “Zhou, Shao, Cheng, and Zhang” are Zhou Dunyi, Shao Yong, the Cheng brothers Hao and Yi, and Zhang Zai. “Master Zhu” is Zhu Xi. Names and styles in the register below explain the alternative appellations used throughout the compilation.

[^fm-preface]: The imperial preface speaks in the Kangxi emperor’s first person. Its claims about lost transmission and the authority of particular schools belong to this court-sponsored argument; they are not a neutral modern history of the Changes. “Balanced interpretations” renders 折中: adjudicating divergent readings by a governing standard, not merely splitting the difference. The Chinese itself later identifies Zhu Xi as that standard. The transcriptions’ 已見 is understood in context as 己見, an individual’s own view; no claim of unanimity among modern scholars is implied.

[^fm-first-watch]: 甲夜 is the first night watch. The preface’s dating is retained in the traditional calendar: spring, third month, eighteenth day of Kangxi 54 (1715). This is not March 18 in the Gregorian calendar.

[^fm-offices]: Office titles are translated descriptively, not equated with modern academic posts. 臣, repeated in the register, is the speaker’s formal designation as the emperor’s servant. Hanlin “compiler,” “examining editor,” “lecturer,” and “reader” distinguish 編脩, 檢討, 侍講, and 侍讀. The examination and student designations are likewise retained as distinct statuses. Personal names, including the Manchu names in the register, are romanized from the Chinese rather than reconstructed in Manchu. Zhongyun (中允) is rendered descriptively as “Companion”; no modern rank equivalence is intended.

[^fm-roster-variants]: The parallel Siku transcription has 少詹事, deputy head of the Household Administration of the Heir Apparent, for Jiang Tingxi, where the base has 詹事, head. The base’s title is retained. The parallel also inserts 分脩, “Sectional compilation,” immediately before Chu Zaiwen; that heading is absent from the base and is supplied here only as a supplementary reading. The English does not silently move the following eleven names into a new source section. Historical page images 0005 and 0006 confirm the deputy title and the separate Sectional Compilation heading. They remain recorded variants rather than unmarked changes to the base’s structure.

[^fm-wang-office]: For Wang Shichen’s office the base reads 編撰; the parallel Siku transcription has 修撰. “Senior compiler” follows the attested office 修撰, as distinct from 編脩, “compiler.” The Chinese source file is unchanged.

[^fm-name-register]: The register’s dynasty headings and order are preserved, including figures whom modern chronology would classify differently. A personal name is followed, where supplied, by other appellations: these may be courtesy names, literary names, posthumous designations, or place-names. The English preserves their order without assigning every item to a biographical category the source does not specify. 氏 is rendered “Master” when only a surname survives; the source’s special 子 is retained as “Master” with the full name. The repeated title at the beginning also belongs to the base.

[^fm-xuan]: The base writes 元 in Zheng Yuan, Zhai Ziyuan, Lu Yuanlang, Fang Qiao’s Yuanling, and He Kai’s Yuanzi. The parallel prints 玄 or a graphic form of 玄 at these places. These are name-taboo or transmission variants, not five independent discoveries of new people. The familiar forms include Zheng Xuan, Lu Xuanlang (Lu Deming), and Fang Xuanling. The register’s 元 is retained here and romanized Yuan; compare Zhai Zixuan in the preliminary juan. No Chinese name has been silently rewritten.

[^fm-lai-style]: The base and parallel register both print 矣鮮 after Lai Zhide. This is retained as Yixian rather than silently altered to another biographical form. Similarly, the register’s Feng Dangke followed by Shixing, and Zhang Huang written 章潢, remain as transmitted. This list is an edition witness, not a newly standardized biographical dictionary.

[^fm-arrangement]: The eight articles of 凡例 are the compilation’s own editorial principles. Their endorsement of Zhu Xi, assessment of other schools, and historical claims are translated as such. Original Meaning is Zhu Xi’s 本義; Cheng’s Commentary is Cheng Yi’s 易傳. The two parts of the classic and ten Wings are distinguished from the later juan divisions used to organize this compilation.

[^fm-four-sages]: The “four sages” are Fuxi, King Wen, the Duke of Zhou, and Confucius, according to the traditional authorship scheme explained and debated in the preliminary juan. 卜筮 encompasses divination with tortoise shells and with stalks; where the passage concerns the Changes specifically, stalk divination is central.

[^fm-techniques]: “Overlapping hexagrams” translates 互卦; 納甲, “assigning the stems,” correlates hexagrams or trigrams with the cyclical heavenly stems; 飛伏, “manifest and hidden,” distinguishes overt and latent configurations in associated divinatory systems. The criticism here is of what the editors regard as groundless applications, not a blanket exclusion of every overlapping construction: Juans Twenty-One and Twenty-Two make extensive use of such constructions.

[^fm-imperial-voice]: The first-person 朕 in Articles Six–Eight is specifically imperial, not the translator’s or Li Guangdi’s private voice. It is retained as “I.” This does not establish that the emperor personally drafted every unmarked editorial passage elsewhere; it records the voice under which these principles were promulgated. “The high and the hard” evokes the Analects’ description of the Master’s teaching as ever higher to look up to and harder to penetrate.

[^fm-primer]: The four parts named here are translated in Juans Nineteen and Twenty. The preliminary 卦變 diagram associated with Original Meaning concerns interpretive derivations between hexagrams; it is not interchangeable with the sixty-four-by-sixty-four casting outcomes in the primer. “Nine Chapters” is the mathematical work 九章算術, not nine chapters of the Changes.

[^fm-seven-luminaries]: The “seven moving luminaries” are the sun, moon, and five visible planets, as opposed to the stellar array. The astronomy is an analogy for an order that requires trained observation to discern. The two concluding works named are the contents of Juans Twenty-One and Twenty-Two.

[^fm-synopsis]: This is the base edition’s brief Siku catalogue synopsis, dated Qianlong 39, ninth month (1774), not Kangxi’s 1715 preface. The parallel Wikisource front-matter witness instead carries a substantially different, longer synopsis dated Qianlong 42, second month (1777). That later text even describes an order of Cheng’s Commentary before Original Meaning, at odds with Article One here. Neither its date nor its wording has been substituted for the base. The four source blocks of the opening sentence reflect honorific line breaks and are rendered as one continuous paragraph while retaining separate alignment markers.

[^fm-compound-surname]: Lu Feichi’s surname is the compound 陸費, romanized Lufei; the name is therefore Lufei Chi, not Lu Feichi with Feichi as the given name. The signatures are those of the later catalogue compilers, distinct from the Kangxi-period project staff listed earlier.
