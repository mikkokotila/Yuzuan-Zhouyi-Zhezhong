**Commentary on the Words of the Text · Wenyan.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:002 -->

**Original Meaning.** This chapter develops the meaning of the Commentaries on the Judgments and the Images, bringing out all that is contained in Qian and Kun. From these two hexagrams, the explanations of the others may in turn be inferred.[^j16-wenyan]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:004 -->

### Origination is foremost among the good; success is the gathering of excellence; benefit is the harmony of rightness; steadfastness is the trunk of affairs.[^j16-four-virtues]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:005 -->

**Original Meaning.** Origination is the beginning of the generation of things. Among Heaven and Earth's virtues, nothing precedes it. Thus in the seasons it is spring, and in human beings it is humaneness: foremost among all that is good. Success is the free unfolding of the generation of things. When things reach this stage, none is without excellence and beauty. Thus in the seasons it is summer, and in human beings it is ritual: the gathering of all that is beautiful. Benefit is the fulfillment of the generation of things. Each thing finds what is fitting for it, without obstructing or harming another. Thus in the seasons it is autumn, and in human beings it is rightness: the harmony of each receiving its due share. Steadfastness is the completion of the generation of things. Substantial principle is fully present, and wherever it resides, it is sufficient. Thus in the seasons it is winter, and in human beings it is wisdom: the trunk of all affairs. A trunk is the body of a tree, upon which the branches and leaves depend for their support.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:005 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:006 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The other hexagrams have only the Judgments and Images. Qian and Kun alone have the further Commentary on the Words of the Text to illuminate their meaning. Extending Qian's Way to human affairs, origination, success, benefit, and steadfastness are Qian's four virtues. In human beings, origination is the head of all goodness; success is the gathering of excellence and beauty; benefit is accord with rightness; steadfastness is the capacity to give affairs their support.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:006 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:007 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:007 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:008 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu:** Asked about “origination is foremost among the good,” he replied: “Origination, success, benefit, and steadfastness are all good, but origination is foremost among the four. It is where the beginnings of goodness first appear.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:008 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:009 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu:** Asked about “success is the gathering of excellence,” he replied: “Take plants and trees. By the time their growth reaches summer, all their excellences come together. ‘Excellence’ simply means what is good; ‘gathering’ means coming together at the appointed time.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:009 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:010 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu:** “‘Benefit is the harmony of rightness.’ Rightness might seem to lack harmony. Yet when matters are arranged so that each finds its proper place, there is harmony. Where rightness is harmonious, there is benefit.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:010 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:011 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu:** Someone asked: “Master Cheng said, ‘Where rightness rests at ease, there is benefit.’ Does this mean that what is fitting is thereby at ease?” He replied: “Yes. This explains ‘benefit is the harmony of rightness’ very well. Rightness draws a clear boundary that cannot be transgressed, and so seems unharmonious. Yet once distinctions have been made, the myriad things each rest in their proper place: that is harmony. Disharmony arises from unrighteousness. With rightness, nothing is without harmony; with harmony, nothing is without benefit.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:011 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:012 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu:** “‘Steadfastness is the trunk of affairs.’ Wisdom is that one thing which silently guides affairs through their changes. Thus it is their trunk.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:012 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:013 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu:** “‘Correct’ alone does not exhaust the meaning of steadfastness. Correctness and firmness must be spoken of together for its meaning to be complete. It is just what Mencius calls ‘knowing these two and not departing from them.’ ‘Knowing these’ gives the meaning of correctness; ‘not departing’ gives the meaning of firmness.” Gan asked: “What, then, of the expression ‘one must not remain steadfast’?” He replied: “The meaning is the same. One must not take that as correct and hold firmly to it.”[^j16-gan]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:013 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:014 -->

**Xiang Anshi:** Goodness, excellence, and rightness are different names for what is good. At an affair's beginning it is goodness; goodness in its abundant flourishing is excellence; each obtaining what is fitting is rightness; what rightness establishes is an affair. One principle has four names. Divided into four, it is said: “Origination is foremost among the good; success is the gathering of excellence; benefit is the harmony of rightness; steadfastness is the trunk of affairs.” Paired into two, it is said: “Qian's origination is what begins and succeeds; benefit and steadfastness are its nature and disposition.” Taken together as one, it is said: “Qian's beginning can benefit the world with beautiful benefit, without naming what is benefited. Great indeed!” In “the harmony of rightness,” harmony means being able to follow it. In “the trunk of affairs,” trunk means being able to establish them.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:014 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:015 -->

### The noble person embodies humaneness and so is able to lead others; brings excellence to every gathering and so accords with ritual; benefits things and so brings rightness into harmony; remains steadfast and firm and so gives affairs their support.[^j16-embodying]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:015 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:016 -->

**Original Meaning.** Taking humaneness as one's very embodiment leaves nothing outside the reach of one's love; thus one is able to lead others. Making excellent what one brings together leaves nothing out of accord with ritual. Enabling things to obtain their proper benefit leaves nothing in rightness unharmonized. Being steadfast and firm means knowing where correctness lies and holding firmly to it: what is meant by knowing and not departing. Thus one is able to be the trunk of affairs.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:016 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:017 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Embodying and modeling Qian's humaneness is the Way of a ruler and leader, and is sufficient to lead others. To embody humaneness is to embody origination. Comparing oneself with it and following its example is called embodying it. Attaining the excellence of meeting and free passage accords with ritual. What does not accord with ritual is not in accord with principle: how could it be excellent? Without principle, how could there be success? Only harmony with rightness can benefit things. How could anything benefit them without giving each what is fitting? Steadfastness and firmness are what enable one to sustain affairs.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:017 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:018 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:018 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:019 -->

**Li Dingzuo:** Heaven turns the four seasons to generate and complete the myriad things. The ruler models the Five Constants to teach and transform people. Origination leads the good, and so one can embody humaneness. Humaneness governs spring's generation: wood in the east. Free passage makes a gathering of excellence, sufficient to accord with ritual. Ritual governs summer's nourishment: fire in the south. Benefit is what is fitting for things, sufficient to harmonize rightness. Rightness governs autumn's completion: metal in the west. Steadfastness is the trunk of affairs and corresponds to wisdom. Wisdom governs winter's storing: water in the north. Trustworthiness is not mentioned because it governs earth. Earth occupies the central palace and shares its rule among the four seasons. Water, fire, metal, and wood could not be supported without earth.[^j16-five-constants]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:019 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:020 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu:** “Embodying humaneness does not mean taking humaneness and making it my body. My very embodiment is wholly humaneness.” He also said: “When the Original Meaning says ‘taking humaneness as one's embodiment,’ it is like saying that one's whole person was, from the beginning, entirely humaneness.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:020 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:021 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu:** “‘Excellent’ means beautiful. ‘Gathering’ means assembling everything together. So many beautiful excellences converge here at once; hence a gathering of excellence. Make excellent what is brought together, and every movement and bearing accords with ritual.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:021 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:022 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu:** “It seems that rightness means simply what is fitting. At first it is very strict: the man has his correct place outside, the woman her correct place within; inner and outer must indeed be distinguished. The ruler is honored above, the minister reverent below. High and low, greater and lesser are sharply distinguished, and their boundaries may not be crossed. This seems utterly unharmonious. Yet if each can be given what is fitting, what harmony could be greater?”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:022 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:023 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu:** “The trunk is like a tree's trunk; affairs are like its branches and leaves. Steadfastness and firmness mean holding firmly to what is correct. In affairs, they establish a supporting framework; thus they are the trunk of affairs. Try to undertake something without this steadfast firmness, and it cannot be set upright: naturally it collapses.” Asked why “steadfast and firm” differs in form from “embodying humaneness,” “gathering excellence,” and “benefiting things,” he replied: “What belongs to the north requires two words before its meaning is fully expressed.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:023 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:024 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu:** Asked about the Wenyan's passage on the four virtues, he replied: “The four clauses beginning ‘origination is foremost among the good’ speak of the natural character of Heaven's virtue. The four beginning ‘the noble person embodies humaneness and so is able to lead others’ speak of what is fitting in human affairs. Origination leads the good: the principle of life in the myriad things begins here, and all goodness and the hundred forms of conduct are gathered under it. Thus in the seasons it is spring; in people, humaneness. Success is the gathering of excellence. Excellence means beauty; gathering means coming together. In spring things are only beginning to grow. At this stage nothing fails to flourish freely. In people, the three hundred ritual observances and three thousand forms of dignified bearing bring every affair and thing to just its proper place. This is what is meant by every movement and bearing according with ritual. Thus in the seasons it is summer; in people, ritual. Benefit is the harmony of rightness. Here each thing fulfills its nature, and every principle of affairs finds its fitting realization. Thus in the seasons it is autumn; in people, rightness. Steadfastness is the trunk of affairs. Here things gather inward and form their ripened substance, and the principles of affairs are all precisely correct. Thus in the seasons it is winter; in people, wisdom. Such is the natural character of Heaven's virtue. The noble person must devote himself to this. Embodying means taking humaneness as one's embodiment. Humaneness is my supporting frame; I embody it, and it issues entirely from me. Thus nothing lies outside my love, and I can lead others. To desire excellence in what is gathered, one must make excellent the gathering itself. Relations of greater and lesser affection, closeness and distance, high and low, greater and lesser each have their measured forms. When none misses its proper measure, every gathering is excellent, and so accords with ritual. Enable affairs and things each to obtain what is fitting, without obstructing or harming one another: naturally there is no discord, and each finds the harmony of its allotted share. Know where correctness lies and hold firmly to it without departing: this is sufficient to be the trunk of affairs. The trunk is like the supports used in building with rammed earth.”[^j16-ritual-framework]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:024 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:025 -->

**Hu Bingwen:** Embodying humaneness preserves something within; gathering excellence makes beauty appear outside. Benefiting things gives the outer its proper form; steadfast firmness preserves what is within. Ritual is humaneness made manifest; wisdom is rightness held within. Embodying humaneness to lead others and steadfast firmness to sustain affairs proceed from principle to use. Gathering excellence to accord with ritual and benefiting things to harmonize rightness proceed from use to principle.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:025 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:026 -->

**Dong Zhenqing:** Master Zhu said that what belongs to the north requires two words for its full expression. As a child I heard my late father say: “The north is the ending and beginning of Heaven's qi, and has the meaning of differentiation. Thus in seal script the character for north shows two people back to back. Among the Four Beginnings, Five Viscera, and Four Creatures, what belongs to the north is always twofold, whereas what belongs to east, west, and south is single. Among the four seasons it is winter, which also joins with spring. Among the four virtues it is steadfastness, beneath which origination begins. Among the twelve branches it is Hai and Zi; among the sixty-four hexagrams, Kun and Return.”[^j16-northern-pairs]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:026 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:027 -->

**Lin Xiyuan:** The noble person masters himself and returns to ritual, so that humaneness fills what is within and appears outside. Within, not one thought is other than humane; outside, not one deed is other than humane. The noble person's whole person is thus one humaneness. He does not merely embody his bodily form: he embodies humaneness. Embodying humaneness is humaneness at its utmost. Hence nothing is outside his love, and he is able to lead others. Being at ease wherever one dwells and deepening humaneness so as to be able to love means precisely this.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:027 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:028 -->

**Lin Xiyuan:** The benefit in “benefit is the harmony of rightness” is the benefit naturally present in human beings. The benefit in “benefiting things is sufficient to harmonize rightness” is how people seek that natural benefit. The harmony in “the harmony of rightness” is the harmony naturally present in people. The harmony in “sufficient to harmonize rightness” is how people seek that natural harmony.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:028 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:029 -->

### The noble person practices these four virtues. Thus it is said: “Qian: origination, success, benefit, steadfastness.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:029 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:030 -->

**Original Meaning.** Without the noble person's utmost vigor, these cannot be practiced. Thus it is said: “Qian: origination, success, benefit, steadfastness.” **Section Summary.** This first section develops the meaning of the Commentary on the Judgment. It does not differ from Mu Jiang's words recorded in the Spring and Autumn Commentary. I suspect that this saying already existed in antiquity: Mu Jiang quoted it, and the Master also adopted it. Thus the following text separately introduces Confucius's words with “The Master said.” The transmitter evidently wished to make clear that this section contains an ancient saying.[^j16-mujiang]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:030 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:031 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Only by practicing these four virtues does one accord with Qian.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:031 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:032 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:032 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:033 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu:** Someone asked: “‘Qian: origination, success, benefit, steadfastness’ is like saying ‘nature: humaneness, rightness, ritual, wisdom.’” He replied: “That is a very sound formulation.” He also said: “In ‘Qian: origination, success, benefit, steadfastness,’ he takes ‘Qian’ as the noble person.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:033 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:034 -->

**Cai Qing:** In King Wen's text the four words origination, success, benefit, and steadfastness were simply a divinatory statement. Only with Confucius's Commentary on the Judgment came the explanation of four virtues. Yet these four virtues themselves have different applications. Heaven's four virtues concern its generation and completion of the myriad things. The sage's four virtues concern his ordering of an entire age. Here the four virtues concern only what the noble person practices in his own person. Personal practice is their constitution; ordering an entire age is their use. The four virtues are present everywhere. This also shows how widely the word Qian reaches.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:034 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:035 -->

### The first nine says, “Hidden dragon. Do not act.” What does this mean? The Master said: “One who has a dragon's virtue yet remains hidden. He does not change to suit the world or seek fulfillment in a name. He withdraws from the world without resentment; he goes unrecognized as right without resentment. What brings joy, he does; what brings sorrow, he leaves aside. Firmly rooted, he cannot be pulled away. Such is the hidden dragon.”[^j16-hidden-dragon]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:035 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:036 -->

**Original Meaning.** A dragon's virtue is the sage's virtue. Being below, he remains hidden. “Change” means changing what one holds to. In general, the Wenyan explains all six lines of Qian through the sage. There are differences of concealment and manifestation, but not of depth of attainment.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:036 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:037 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** From here onward the text speaks of Qian's use: the Way of Using Nine. The first nine is yang at its faint beginning, the hiddenness of the dragon's virtue: the sage or worthy person living in obscurity and humble circumstances. He keeps to his Way without changing with the world. He conceals his conduct without seeking recognition in his time. Trusting and rejoicing in himself, he acts when he sees what is possible and avoids what he knows to be difficult. What he holds to is firm and cannot be taken from him. This is the hidden dragon's virtue.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:037 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:038 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:038 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:039 -->

**Kong Yingda:** What the heart finds joyful, he practices; what the heart finds grievous, he avoids. Though his person moves and changes with circumstances, his heart and intention keep to the Way, firm and substantial, impossible to uproot.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:039 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:040 -->

**You Zuo:** Having a dragon's virtue yet remaining hidden, he does not change for the world. Not changing for the world means that acting or withdrawing rests with himself; thus he withdraws without resentment. Not seeking fulfillment in a name means not leaving blame and praise to external things; thus he is untroubled when his rightness goes unrecognized.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:040 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:041 -->

**Wu Cheng:** “What brings joy, he does” explains the two clauses about being without resentment. “What brings sorrow, he leaves aside” explains not changing and not seeking a name. Joy means being without resentment; doing means practicing it. Sorrow means what he does not enjoy; leaving aside means not doing it. Not seeking visibility in the world or recognition from others is what he enjoys, and so he does it. Changing for the world and fulfilling himself in a name are not what he enjoys, and so he does not do them.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:041 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:042 -->

**Jiang Tisheng:** Practicing the Way and aiding one's time are the sage's original intention. Thus “what brings joy, he does.” Remaining hidden because he is not employed is not what the sage desires. Thus “what brings sorrow, he leaves aside.” Nevertheless, whether advancing or withdrawing, he always seeks where utmost principle lies and never bends the Way to please others. Thus “firmly rooted, he cannot be pulled away.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:042 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:043 -->

**Cai Qing:** The two clauses about withdrawal without resentment go further than the two about not changing for the world. The three clauses beginning with joy and action go further still. They show that he has neither a private preconception nor an insistence on a predetermined outcome. Only by reaching this point is the hiddenness of the dragon's virtue fully explained.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:043 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:044 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Wu's and Jiang's explanations differ, yet both are intelligible.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:044 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:045 -->

### The second nine says, “A dragon appears in the field. It is beneficial to see the great person.” What does this mean? The Master said: “One who has a dragon's virtue and is precisely centered. Trustworthy in ordinary speech, careful in ordinary conduct, he guards against the crooked and preserves his sincerity. He brings goodness to the world without boasting; his virtue is broad and transforms others. The Changes says, ‘A dragon appears in the field. It is beneficial to see the great person.’ This is a ruler's virtue.”[^j16-centered]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:045 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:046 -->

**Original Meaning.** “Precisely centered” describes the time when he is no longer hidden but has not yet leapt. Even his ordinary words are trustworthy; even his ordinary conduct is careful. This is flourishing virtue at its utmost. Guarding against the crooked and preserving sincerity has the meaning of “even without weariness, preserve it.” Calling it a ruler's virtue explains that the great person is the second nine.[^j16-preserving]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:046 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:047 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** With a dragon's virtue he occupies the precise center. Being at the hexagram's proper center gives the meaning of attaining centeredness. Trustworthy and careful in the ordinary, he remains so even in haste. Already occupying ground free of fault, he has only to guard against the crooked. Once that is kept out, sincerity is preserved. Bringing goodness to the world without boasting means not claiming that goodness as his own. Broad virtue transforming others means making oneself correct so that things become correct. These are all the great person's affairs. Though he does not occupy the ruler's position, his is a ruler's virtue.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:047 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:048 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:048 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:049 -->

**Kong Yingda:** “Ordinary” means constant. He is truthful and trustworthy in constant speech, careful and attentive in constant conduct. Guarding against crookedness and evil, he preserves his sincerity and substance. He does good in the world without boasting of his achievement. His virtue can be broad and transform the world's customs. The first line is entirely hidden and withdrawn from the world; the second gradually manifests its virtue and conduct to transform custom.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:049 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:050 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu:** “Ordinary speech and ordinary conduct: flourishing virtue at its utmost. Even here one still guards against the crooked and preserves sincerity. This is ‘even without weariness, preserve it.’ Though there is no weariness, one should still preserve it. Preserving means holding and keeping.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:050 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:051 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu:** “‘It is beneficial to see the great person: a ruler's virtue.’ The text speaks of a ruler's virtue in two places precisely to make clear that the great person is the second nine.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:051 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:052 -->

**Lu Jiuyuan:** Trustworthiness and care in speech and conduct are how the second completes itself. Bringing goodness to the world without boasting is how it completes things. Its very trustworthiness and care are what enable it not to boast. Guarding against the crooked and preserving sincerity concern what is preserved within oneself. Broad virtue transforming others concerns virtue's reaching things. The very guarding and preserving are what make that breadth and transformation possible.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:052 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:053 -->

**Li Shunchen:** Qian's stroke is —; its fullness is sincerity. Kun's stroke is – –; its openness gives rise to reverence. Thus Qian's second nine speaks of sincerity, and Kun's second six speaks of reverence. These two words, sincerity and reverence, begin with Baoxi's heart-drawn strokes, yet are in truth Heaven and Earth's natural principle.[^j16-line-figures]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:053 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:054 -->

**Xiang Anshi:** “Centered and correct” names two things: the second and fifth are central, while yin and yang occupying their proper positions are correct. “Precisely centered” names one thing: simply attaining the central position exactly. It does not refer to correctness of position.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:054 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:055 -->

**Xiang Anshi:** Because it is in the lower trigram and is not a yang position, this is not the central position but centered virtue. The Wenyan twice calls it a ruler's virtue to make clear that it is not the ruler's position. Here it again speaks of centered dragon virtue, making clear that it does not mean the central position of the dragon.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:055 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:056 -->

**Feng Yi:** The Changes is the source of the learning of principle; Qian and Kun in turn are the source of the learning of the Changes. Zisi and Mencius call sincerity Heaven's Way. Earlier scholars call sincerity and reverence the source of sagely learning. All these teachings come from here.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:056 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:057 -->

**He Kai:** The Way rests in the center, and the center resides in the ordinary. “Ordinary” means constant: the name for what is level and without anything extraordinary. Words must have substance, not seek height for its own sake. Simply being trustworthy leaves no need for choosing grand words. Conduct must have a rule, not seek difficulty for its own sake. Simply being careful leaves no need for choosing extraordinary deeds. Trustworthiness and care are sincerity, Heaven's virtue: one undivided reality, nothing more.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:057 -->

### The third nine says, “The noble person is unceasingly vigorous throughout the day, and watchful at evening. Danger, but no blame.” What does this mean? The Master said: “The noble person advances in virtue and cultivates his work. Wholeheartedness and trustworthiness are how he advances in virtue. Cultivating his words and establishing his sincerity are how he abides in his work. Knowing what is to be reached and reaching it, he can participate in incipient workings. Knowing what is to be completed and completing it, he can preserve rightness. Thus in a high position he is not arrogant; in a low position he is not anxious. Unceasingly vigorous and watchful according to the time, he incurs no blame though in danger.”[^j16-advancing]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4707:058 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4707:059 -->

**Original Meaning.** Wholeheartedness and trustworthiness govern the heart: not one thought is insincere. Cultivating words appears in affairs: not one word lacks substance. Though one has a wholehearted, trustworthy heart, without cultivating words and establishing sincerity one has no means of abiding in it. Knowing what is to be reached and reaching it concerns advancing in virtue. Knowing what is to be completed and completing it concerns abiding in one's work. **Editorial Judgment.** Two of Qian's lines speak of learning. At the second nine, the text speaks of trustworthy words, careful conduct, guarding against the crooked, and preserving sincerity. At the third nine, it speaks of wholeheartedness and trustworthiness advancing virtue, and cultivated words establishing sincerity so as to abide in work. Two of Kun's lines speak of learning. At the second six, it speaks of reverence straightening the inner and rightness squaring the outer. At the fifth six, it speaks of the yellow center, penetrating principle, the proper position, and abiding in one's role. Separately, there are four accounts. Taken together, the sincerity preserved by Qian's second is the wholeheartedness and trustworthiness of Qian's third: both concern the heart's reality. The trustworthiness and care of Qian's second are the cultivating of words and establishing of sincerity in Qian's third: both concern the reality of speech and conduct. At the second, the figure is the great person, and the text speaks of completed virtue. Looking through his speech and conduct into his heart, one sees purity that never ceases. At the third, the figure is the noble person, and the text speaks of progress in learning. Rooted in the heart and reaching speech and conduct, one sees their unremitting mutual cultivation. Kun's second straightening the inner is Kun's fifth yellow at the center: both concern the heart's centered straightness. Kun's second squaring the outer is Kun's fifth proper position: both concern conduct's square correctness. The second speaks of straightness and the fifth of centrality. Nothing straight fails to be centered, and centrality is straightness at its utmost. The second speaks of squareness and the fifth of correctness. Nothing square fails to be correct, and correctness is squareness at its extreme. The second occupies a low position and simply has no doubt in its conduct. The fifth occupies an honored position and also has the beauty of issuing in affairs and work. This is the difference between the two lines. In Qian's two lines, sincerity predominates: a real heart embodying things is Qian's virtue. In Kun's two lines, reverence predominates: an open heart following principle is Kun's virtue. Yet there has never been sincerity without reverence or reverence without sincerity. Qian and Kun are one virtue; sincerity and reverence are one heart. The sage speaks of them separately because Qian's yang governs fullness and Kun's yin governs openness. The virtue of the human heart must embody both. Without fullness there can be no openness: only when Heaven's principle governs do human desires retreat and obey. Without openness there can be no fullness: only when human desires are stilled does Heaven's principle circulate. Speaking of its fullness, we call it sincerity; speaking of its openness, reverence. Both are the virtue of one heart, not the affairs of two different people. In the sage there is pure sincerity, and his reverence is natural reverence. At the next level one makes reverence one's governing orientation to arrive at sincerity. Thus Master Cheng says: “With sincerity, nothing is without reverence. Before one can be sincere, reverence must precede sincerity.” This is the basis for distinguishing Qian and Kun as the learning of sages and worthies.[^j16-one-heart]

## Translator’s Endnotes

The base is the repository’s unchanged 易學網 text. Doubtful readings were compared selectively with [the Siku Quanshu transcription, fixed revision 528613](<https://zh.wikisource.org/w/index.php?title=御纂周易折中_(四庫全書本)/卷16&oldid=528613>), additional primary texts, and selected historical page images identified below. Attested corrections, repunctuation, historical conjectures, and unresolved readings are distinguished. These checks are not a systematic leaf-by-leaf facsimile collation.

[^j16-wenyan]: 文言傳 is rendered “Commentary on the Words of the Text,” with Wenyan retained to identify this particular Wing. Its title is not a claim that it comments on every hexagram: here its subject is Qian and Kun. The translation keeps the compilation’s own layered voices; the chapter’s explanations are not additional oracle statements. Added section titles serve navigation only.

[^j16-four-virtues]: 元、亨、利、貞 are read here as four virtues: origination, success, benefit, and steadfastness. This is the Wenyan’s explicit interpretation, not a retroactive replacement of the divinatory readings in Juan One. 仁 is humaneness, 禮 ritual or ritual propriety, 義 rightness, and 智 wisdom. 幹 is literally a tree’s trunk and by extension the support that enables affairs to stand; the image is preserved rather than reduced to abstract “competence.”

[^j16-gan]: Both Zhezhong transcriptions read 幹問. The corresponding passage in 朱子語類, juan 68, has 榦問 and records the exchange under 榦. Thus Gan is retained as the questioner’s name, not treated as a stray repetition of “trunk.” Compare the explicit Huang Gan attribution later in this juan. The question concerns why some conditions should not be held to as correct: steadfastness is not persistence in error. See https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/朱子語類/068.

[^j16-embodying]: 體仁 keeps the bodily force of 體: humaneness is to become the very constitution of the person, not an external rule intermittently applied. 嘉會 is interpreted actively by Zhu Xi as making one’s relations and encounters excellent, while the primary phrase also echoes the preceding “gathering of excellence.” The English preserves that reciprocity rather than reducing it to social popularity.

[^j16-five-constants]: The Five Constants here are humaneness, ritual propriety, rightness, wisdom, and trustworthiness. Li Dingzuo correlates the first four with the seasons and directional phases, then accounts for trustworthiness through central earth. These are the commentator’s traditional correspondences; 通 in his gloss is retained as “free passage,” not silently changed to 亨.

[^j16-ritual-framework]: The large conventional numbers for ritual observances convey comprehensive ordering. The social distinctions in Zhu Xi’s examples are retained as the source’s historical commitments. The last image, 版築之有楨幹, concerns structural supports used in rammed-earth building and continues the concrete sense of 幹 as what holds an undertaking upright.

[^j16-northern-pairs]: Dong’s account belongs to traditional symbolic correlations. The Four Beginnings are the beginnings of moral capacities, the Five Viscera a traditional bodily classification, and the Four Creatures directional emblems. His north–winter pairing links completion with renewed beginning; it is not presented here as a modern anatomical or scientific claim. Hai and Zi are the last and first of the twelve earthly branches.

[^j16-mujiang]: Zhu Xi himself questions whether this opening is an older saying incorporated by Confucius; his historical hypothesis is preserved as a hypothesis. Mu Jiang’s oracle episode is recorded under Duke Xiang, year 9, in the Zuo Commentary. The present translation follows this compilation’s words, not a conflation with that parallel narrative. Section summaries are Zhu’s commentary, not part of the canonical Wenyan.

[^j16-hidden-dragon]: The primary rendering leaves 樂則行之，憂則違之 broad enough for the competing explanations. Wu finds joy in unrecognized withdrawal; Jiang stresses the sage’s wish to practice the Way publicly. The compilers explicitly accept both. 不成乎名 is not “failing to become famous”: it rejects making reputation the fulfillment of one’s life. You Zuo’s terse 非譽不在物 is retained in the sense that external judgments do not determine one’s worth; both consulted transcriptions have the same wording.

[^j16-centered]: 正中, “precisely centered,” must not be confused with 中正, “centered and correct.” The second place is central within the lower trigram but is formally a yin position occupied here by yang. Xiang’s two successive glosses emphasize position and virtue differently; the English does not remove that shift. The great person is identified by Zhu with the line itself, not invariably with another figure whom the diviner is to seek.

[^j16-preserving]: 無斁亦保 echoes the Odes’ exhortation to keep one’s virtue even without weariness. Zhu’s point is continued preservation at the height of attainment, not the eradication of a newly identified moral defect. In the separate 朱子語類, juan 69, the phrase also appears as 無射亦保; the base’s 斁 is retained. See https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/朱子語類/069.

[^j16-line-figures]: Li’s line sketches are retained typographically as an unbroken stroke and a divided stroke. “Heart-drawn” renders 心畫 and identifies the figures as expressions of the mind or heart; it does not imply literal anatomical writing. Fullness and openness are structural and ethical images, not claims that yin lacks moral substance.

[^j16-advancing]: 忠信 is rendered “wholeheartedness and trustworthiness”: Zhong here concerns an undivided, genuine heart, not merely allegiance to a ruler. 修／脩辭 means cultivating speech so that words have reality in conduct, not literary ornament. 居業 retains the sense of dwelling or abiding, developed in Zheng’s house analogy. 幾 denotes incipient workings, the subtle beginning at which a course becomes discernible.

[^j16-knowing-doing]: The commentators do not give one uniform account of 知至至之. Cheng emphasizes knowing first, then arriving; one Zhu conversation explicitly calls arriving practice, another says the heart arrives even before conduct does. These formulations are all retained. Cheng’s “beginning” and “completing the ordered sequence” draw on Mencius’s distinction between wisdom and sagehood; the translation does not smooth it into one generic notion of self-improvement.

[^j16-speech-readings]: The parallel Siku transcription supports 子細 for the base’s 予細 in Cheng’s gloss, 說 for 就 in “virtue is spoken of in relation to the heart,” and deletion of the stray 敏 in 被敏汨沒動盪. These affect intelligibility but do not change the argument. The English follows the supported readings while the Chinese source remains unchanged. The historical page image at https://www.eee-learning.com/eeebook/middleyi/1754.png was also inspected: it supports the absence of 敏 in the sentence on words submerging and disturbing sincerity.

[^j16-cheng-brothers]: Yichuan is Cheng Yi; Mingdao is Cheng Hao. The source’s general label 程子 is kept as “Master Cheng” unless the passage itself makes the identity explicit. Here Zhu names the two brothers by their sobriquets and prefers Mingdao’s formulation; it would be misleading to collapse this into agreement with Cheng Yi’s commentary.

[^j16-deep-recesses]: Both consulted Zhezhong transcriptions have 壺奧 in Lin Xiyuan’s phrase. The corresponding historical page image (https://www.eee-learning.com/eeebook/middleyi/1757.png) was inspected as well; it did not establish a different reading. “Deep recesses” renders the contextual sense. 壼奧, the secluded inner part of a building and by extension a profound interior, is a possible lexical explanation, not an adopted, independently attested correction of this witness.

[^j16-leaping]: The disputed 或躍在淵 is kept as “perhaps leaping in the deep.” Cheng understands a leap toward the dragon’s secure place, whereas Zhu emphasizes uncertain ascent from below. No single spatial reconstruction is imposed on both. Here “time” means the fitness of a situation, not merely a date or a scheduled opportunity.

[^j16-resonance]: 同氣, shared qi, carries the traditional account of resonance among things of a kind. “Clouds follow the dragon; wind follows the tiger” remains a concrete cosmological image, not a modern physical explanation. 萬物 can encompass all beings; Zhu expressly narrows it to people here, while Cheng makes the encounter reciprocal.

[^j16-seeing]: Cheng distinguishes a prospective instruction to seek a great person in Contention from the benefit of an accomplished encounter in Qian. His examples 人物、物論 are punctuated as two old expressions illustrating 物 used of people, not as one otherwise unexplained proverb. This is repunctuation, not a change to the Chinese characters.

[^j16-animal-classification]: Zhu’s contrast between upward-facing humans, horizontally oriented animals, and downward-rooted plants is transmitted here as his philosophical classification, credited to Kangjie (Shao Yong). The English preserves it without endorsing the zoological claim that birds and beasts lack wisdom. Zhu’s animals–plants division also differs from Cheng’s celestial bodies–earthly beings division immediately above.

[^j16-time-lodging]: 時舍 is deliberately not flattened into either “the world abandons him” or a modern timetable. The primary “lodging in its time” leaves room for Zhu’s not yet being put to use and Cheng’s stopping as the time requires. These terse parallel restatements are all translated; they are not duplicate text to be deleted.

[^j16-upper-rule]: Su Jun reads 上 evaluatively, “highest,” where Zhu and Cheng read it positionally, “above.” His Five Emperors and Three Kings are traditional exemplary rulers; the translation preserves the category without imposing one historical list.

[^j16-using-nine]: 用九 is kept as “Using Nine,” matching the special statement discussed in Juan One. The present commentators emphasize firmness capable of yielding, not simply six unbending strong lines. Their discussion ranges across rulership, change, and the cycle of origination; no additional divination procedure is supplied by the translator.

[^j16-patterned-light]: 文明 is rendered “patterned illumination,” retaining both 文, manifested order or pattern, and 明, light or clarity. Here it is not “civilization” in a modern historical classification. Su Shi expressly identifies its manifestation with transformative words and conduct.

[^j16-heavens-rule]: The parallel Siku transcription supports 天之法則 for the base’s 夫之法則 in Cheng’s gloss. Su Shi’s “no head” recalls Qian’s special Using Nine statement; it is not silently changed to the different idea of refusing leadership. Cheng’s divisions into Qian’s use, times, and meaning are retained as his own organizing interpretation, while Zhu’s section summaries simply stress repeated development.

[^j16-nature-disposition]: 性情 is rendered “nature and disposition,” not simply “human feelings.” The commentators relate still constitution to its active expression, and the inward gathering of autumn–winter to renewed generation. Zhu’s botanical account is translated as transmitted, not generalized into a modern rule for seed viability. Hu’s claim that this is the first pairing of the terms is his historical assertion.

[^j16-unnamed-benefit]: 不言所利 can mean not specifying a particular beneficiary or not asserting one’s beneficial achievement. Cheng and Mingdao develop these differently, and both readings remain. Zhu’s comparison with Kun concerns its explicitly qualified mare formula; the translation does not turn that brief remark into a new animal-specific prediction.

[^j16-seasonal-trigrams]: Xiang’s sequence follows the account later treated in the Discussion of the Trigrams: Zhen, Xun, Li, Kun, Dui, Qian, Kan, Gen. Qian here names both one member of the eight and, more broadly, their generative unity. “Battle” and “toil” are the inherited images in that sequence, not instructions for conflict or overwork.

[^j16-hanqi]: The two lines are translated as quoted here and attributed by Lin to Han Qi. 三農 is rendered collectively as “the farmers,” without forcing one of its traditional threefold classifications. The point is beneficial work that leaves no self-advertising trace; the note does not claim a separate collation of the poem.

[^j16-seven-terms]: Zhu groups 剛健中正 as four virtues and treats 純、粹、精 as progressively intensive descriptions; Cheng counts the first six terms and treats 精 as their utmost refinement. Both explanations are preserved. Zhu separately defines 純 by absence of yin-yielding mixture and 粹 by absence of moral evil: those two definitions should not be collapsed into the claim that yin itself is evil.

[^j16-six-dragons]: The dragon image shifts between cosmological activity and the sage’s governance. “Steers through Heaven” retains 御 as directing or driving, without implying that the sage arbitrarily controls the cosmos. Hu and the compilers center this passage on the fifth nine while retaining the other five lines as its full range of responsive capacities.

[^j16-completed-virtue]: The source distinguishes 成德, already completed virtue, from conduct not yet manifested in accomplished work. Its 不用 is therefore rendered here as not acting publicly rather than doing nothing at all. Zhu explicitly rejects reading Qian’s six lines as a simple ladder from imperfect to perfect character. 日 is retained as “day by day”; no emendation to 曰 is imposed.

[^j16-learning]: The pronoun 之 is intentionally retained as “it”: the commentators identify what is gathered, distinguished, inhabited, and practiced as principle and attained virtue. 寬 is breadth or spaciousness, not laxness. Cheng’s Yi and Fu are the exemplary ministers conventionally known as Yi Yin and Fu Yue; his contrast is between occupying the sovereign position and implementing the Way without becoming sovereign.

[^j16-double-firmness]: 重剛 is kept in both primary passages. Zhu defines it through a yang line in a yang position, then suspects “doubled” is surplus at the fourth, an even position. Yu Fan and Wu Cheng instead derive the doubling from the meeting of two Qian trigrams. Their explanation gives a reason to preserve the transmitted wording. The English does not silently adopt Zhu’s deletion.

[^j16-before-heaven]: 先天 and 後天 here are acting before or after Heaven’s manifestation or time. They are not the technical names of the Earlier Heaven and Later Heaven trigram arrangements. The commentators explain their accord through one principle and freedom from selfishness, not through an individual will commanding Heaven.

[^j16-guo-ziyi]: Zhu’s anecdote names Guo Ziyi and the Huihe, the historical name written 回紇. It is preserved as an attributed report of divination, not as independent evidence of predictive accuracy. Zhu explicitly distinguishes the historically admirable person from the full ideal described by Confucius.

[^j16-heaven-reading]: The parallel Siku transcription supports 我亦不能違乎天, “I too cannot oppose Heaven,” where the base ends with 人, “people.” The correction restores Wang’s reciprocal explanation of Heaven and the great person. Kong’s earlier 合共明 is likewise read as 合其明 with the parallel witness.

[^j16-sage-question]: The repeated 其唯聖人乎 is retained. Zhu hears a question followed by its own answer; the English therefore moves from question to emphatic recognition rather than deleting the repetition. His warning against mere private self-protection is important: timely withdrawal is not being praised solely as a strategy for personal advantage.

[^j16-oracle-and-sage]: The parallel Siku transcription supports 明吉凶之占 where the base has 明占凶之占. Zhu’s final letter distinguishes the oracle’s applicability to all kinds of people from the Wenyan’s interpretation through the sage. This prevents the preceding ideal portraits from becoming an eligibility requirement for consulting the Changes.

[^j16-kun-firm-square]: “Square” preserves 方 as both a form with defined bounds and the fitting order given to things. It is not a synonym for passivity. The opening explicitly gives firmness to Kun’s movement, while Wu’s lung analogy belongs to the source’s traditional qi model rather than modern physiology.

[^j16-kun-constitution]: The parallel Siku transcription reads 柔静者體也剛方者用也, supporting “yieldingness and stillness are constitution; firmness and squareness are use” against the base’s 承靜. Wu’s preceding analogy of lungs holding qi is retained as his own traditional explanatory model. The corresponding historical page image (https://www.eee-learning.com/eeebook/middleyi/1785.png) was inspected and also reads 柔靜者體也, confirming the correction.

[^j16-finding-lord]: 主 can name a lord or governing principle, or function as a verb, “govern.” Cheng’s proposed restoration of 利 would yield “governs benefit.” Yu Qishu defends the received phrase by comparison with the Judgment Commentary. The primary translation retains the base and does not insert the disputed word; Cheng’s reconstructed sense is confined to his commentary.

[^j16-kun-directions]: The compilers read southwest and northeast through the seasonal circulation of the four virtues, explicitly joining spatial and temporal symbolism. Their explanation is preserved without turning the directions into literal travel advice or importing a later technical divination scheme.

[^j16-governing-lines]: The parallel transcription supports 中正 in both governing-line descriptions, correcting the base’s 剛健小正 and 柔順中止. The compilers attribute 位乎天德 to the Image Commentary, although the exact wording has appeared above in the Wenyan; this source attribution is retained and identified rather than silently rewritten.

[^j16-frost]: 順 is retained in the primary text as “following onward.” Zhu proposes 慎, “caution,” whereas Cheng and Lü Zuqian make ethical sense of 順 as allowing a gradual tendency to continue unchecked. These are distinct readings, not a single undisputed correction. “Blessings/calamities to spare” preserves 餘; Cheng expressly extends the consequences to later generations.

[^j16-unattributed]: The paragraph beginning 坤道可謂至順 follows Zhang Zhenyuan without a fresh named attribution in the base. It is marked as a continuing collected explanation rather than assigned to the imperial editors or confidently to a new author. Its political and familial hierarchy is the source’s own.

[^j16-reverence]: 敬 is reverent attentiveness, rendered consistently here as “reverence.” 義 is the discerning rightness by which things receive their fitting treatment. The square image is preserved. 不習 is translated as “without practice,” while the commentators explain mature, unhesitating enactment; the surrounding demands for study and cultivation make this no recommendation to dispense with learning.

[^j16-buddhist-polemic]: The criticisms of “the Buddhists” belong to the named Confucian commentators. They are translated without softening, but are not the translator’s judgment or a neutral account of the range of Buddhist traditions. Their argumentative purpose here is to insist that inward preservation and outward ethical discrimination cannot be separated.

[^j16-jing-reading]: The parallel Siku transcription reads 敬主乎中, “reverence governs within,” against the base’s 教主乎中. The English adopts 敬. It also follows the parallel 表裏 for the base’s 表裹 in the same paragraph. Both corrections are supported readings, not silent conjectures.

[^j16-cinnabar-writing]: 丹書 is retained as the title “Cinnabar Writing.” Wang is invoking a traditional moral teaching associated with reverence and rightness, not instructions for handling cinnabar. “Lord Zhang Xuan” preserves the source’s posthumous form of address. The emphasis lies on the two kinds of effort completing one another.

[^j16-deputy-completion]: The commentaries distinguish finishing the work from claiming authorship or credit for it. “On another’s behalf” renders 代 and preserves the source’s hierarchy of Heaven/Earth, ruler/minister, and husband/wife. These comparisons are not silently modernized into equal social roles, nor offered as prescriptions for present-day relationships.

[^j16-tied-sack]: The fourth line’s withdrawal is not treated uniformly as failure: Zhang Jun expressly defends preserving inward virtue until a fitting time. His 內亢其德 is rendered “elevating one’s virtue within,” not the blameworthy overreaching of Qian’s top line. The same character can bear different evaluative force in different contexts.

[^j16-yellow-center]: Yellow is kept as a concrete color-image associated here with centrality. The three primary source blocks on the fifth line belong together and explain 黃裳, “yellow lower garment,” from Juan One. They are not new anatomical instructions or three separate oracle statements.

[^j16-position-role]: 居體 is rendered contextually as abiding in one’s role; Zhu grounds it in the body’s lower garment, and Cheng in the subordinate’s embodied station even while occupying a high position. The compilers instead emphasize ritual as the correct position. Their 禮以物躬 is retained in the contextual sense of ritual giving the person its measure; both consulted transcriptions have 物, and no different character is silently substituted. The historical page image at https://www.eee-learning.com/eeebook/middleyi/1796.png was inspected and likewise has 物躬.

[^j16-supplement]: 附錄 is the compilation’s own Supplement, not a translator’s addition. Hu’s explanation of 通理 through outward rightness differs from Cai Qing’s insistence that penetrating principle is also within. The separate placement and the disagreement are preserved.

[^j16-one-heart]: The long editorial synthesis insists that sincerity and reverence are complementary descriptions of one heart, not practices assigned permanently to two separate types of person. The translation retains the distinction between sage and worthy while also retaining this explicit unity. The parallel Siku transcription supports 以居業也 against the base’s 以居業世; the English follows the supported sentence ending.

[^j16-dark-yellow]: 疑 is interpreted here as rivaling or becoming an equal match, not merely psychological doubt. 嫌 carries the subsequent concern that yang might appear absent. 玄 is kept as “dark,” without selecting a modern color-code equivalent; the base’s full 玄黃 is retained where the parallel transcription sometimes omits 玄. The political valuation of yin and yang in these commentators remains their own.

[^j16-blood-reading]: The parallel Siku transcription supports 氣陽而血陰, “qi is yang and blood yin,” against the base’s 氣剛而血陰. It also supports Cheng’s 恐疑无陽, “lest one suppose yang absent,” where the base has 无傷, “no injury.” The latter is a consequential correction: Cheng is explaining why the dragon is named, not denying that the battle wounds both sides.

[^j16-tenth-month]: The tenth month belongs to the traditional hexagram–calendar correlation, not Gregorian October. Gan Bao associates Xu and Hai with Qian’s region; this does not mean the six-yin figure has become the six-yang hexagram. Cai’s “yang month” is his account of a traditional name and his proposed explanation for it, not an independently verified etymology.
