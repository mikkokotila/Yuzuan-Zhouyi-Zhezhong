## 31. Xian 咸 — Influence

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:003 -->

### Xian, Influence, means affecting and responding.[^j10-xian-heart]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:004 -->

**Original Meaning.** This explains the meaning of the hexagram's name.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:005 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:005 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:006 -->

**Liu Mu:** The hexagram is named *xian*, while the Judgment Commentary explains its meaning through *gan*, affecting. The sage's subtle intention is to show that one influences things without a calculating intention.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:006 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:007 -->

**Master Zhang:** The myriad things are originally one. Thus the one can unite what is different; because it can unite differences, this is called influence. Without difference, there would be no union. Heaven and Earth, Qian and Kun, are yin and yang. Being two poles, they can affect each other; being originally one, they can unite.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:007 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:008 -->

**Qiu Fuguo:** Xian means affecting. What affects is the heart: without a heart, one cannot affect anything. Thus adding “heart” to 咸 makes 感. Yet one who deliberately sets out to influence cannot influence all alike. Thus removing “heart” from 感 makes 咸. Xian also means “all.” Only when no calculating intention is allowed into one's responsiveness can nothing remain unaffected. The sage names the hexagram Xian and explains it through Gan in the Commentary so that each illuminates the other's meaning.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:008 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:009 -->

**Wang Yinglin:** Xian's influence has no calculating intention: it affects through openness. Dui's joy has no words: it pleases through sincerity.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:009 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:010 -->

### Yieldingness is above and firmness below. The two energies affect and answer each other, joining together. Stillness joined to joy; the man places himself below the woman. Thus there is success, benefit in steadfastness, and good fortune in taking a wife.[^j10-xian-structure]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:010 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:011 -->

**Original Meaning.** This explains the hexagram statement through the figure's structure, virtues, and images. Some explain yieldingness above and firmness below through hexagram transformation: Influence comes from The Wanderer, with yieldingness rising to the sixth place and firmness descending to the fifth. This too is intelligible.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:011 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:012 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Xian means affecting. In the figure, the yielding line is above and the firm line below. Yieldingness above changes firmness and completes Dui; firmness below changes yieldingness and completes Gen. Yin and yang interact: the meaning of man and woman affecting one another. Moreover, Dui, the woman, is above, and Gen, the man, below: this too is yieldingness above and firmness below. The two energies of yin and yang affect and answer each other, harmonizing and uniting: this is joining together. Stillness joined to joy means resting in joy, with the sense of steadfast earnestness. Gen's stillness below is sincere humility toward the other; Dui's joy above is a harmonious and joyful response. The man's placing himself below the woman is harmony at its utmost. Such a Way of mutual influence permits free passage and attains correctness. Taking a wife in this way brings good fortune. These are the figure's capacities. In general, the Way of influence benefits from correctness.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:012 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:013 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:013 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:014 -->

**Wang Su:** Mountain and lake communicate through vital energy; man and woman affect each other through ritual. The man's placing himself below the woman is what makes the beginning of marriage accord with ritual and the taking of a wife auspicious.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:014 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:015 -->

**Feng Dangke:** Yieldingness above and firmness below, affecting and answering each other to join together, account for success. Stillness joined to joy accounts for benefit in steadfastness. The man placing himself below the woman accounts for good fortune in taking a wife.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:015 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:016 -->

**Wang Shenzi:** Stillness joined to joy means that Gen remains still and unmoving, while intention and vital energy naturally harmonize in delight. This is what is called influence. Without stillness, movement proceeds from contrivance and is not influence. Thus all six lines call for stillness.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:016 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:017 -->

**Cai Qing:** The three explanations through structure, virtue, and image all return to the single meaning of Xian. The meanings of “success, benefit in steadfastness, and good fortune in taking a wife” arise from this. Hence the *Original Meaning* takes the passage as explaining both the name and the statement together.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:017 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:018 -->

### Heaven and Earth affect each other, and the myriad things are transformed and born. The sage moves human hearts, and the world is harmonious and at peace. Observe what they affect and answer, and the true dispositions of Heaven, Earth, and the myriad things can be seen.[^j10-xian-dispositions]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:018 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:019 -->

**Original Meaning.** This carries the principle of responsive communication to its fullest extent.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:019 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:020 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Having spoken of man and woman affecting each other, the text extends the Way of influence to its utmost, encompassing the principle of Heaven and Earth and the sage's activity. The two energies of Heaven and Earth interact and bring the myriad things into being through transformation. The sage, with utmost sincerity, moves the hearts of the multitude, and the world becomes harmonious and at peace. The hearts under Heaven find harmony and peace because the sage moves them. Observe the principle by which Heaven and Earth interact to generate the myriad things, and the Way by which the sage moves human hearts to bring harmony and peace, and the true dispositions of Heaven, Earth, and the myriad things become visible. One who knows the Way may contemplate silently this principle of responsive communication.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:020 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:021 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:021 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:022 -->

**Master Zhang:** One who can communicate with the purposes of the world can move human hearts. The sage is at one with others, without self-partiality. Thus nothing contributes more to the world's harmony and peace than moving human hearts.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:022 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:023 -->

**Zheng Ruxie:** Heaven, Earth, and the myriad things occupy different places, yet their vital energy is one. The sage and the multitude differ in circumstances, yet their sincerity is one. Their true dispositions become visible through observing their responses, because influence arises from those dispositions. Only when feeling issues from correctness does one understand the principle of responsive communication.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:023 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:024 -->

**Zhang Qingzi:** Still and unmoving is nature; affected and thereby communicating is feeling. Observe these responses, and the true dispositions of Heaven, Earth, and the myriad things can be seen.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:024 -->

## 32. Heng 恒 — Constancy

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:025 -->

### Heng, Constancy, means enduring. Firmness is above and yieldingness below; thunder and wind assist one another; deference is joined to movement; firm and yielding lines all respond. This is Constancy.[^j10-heng-structure]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:025 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:026 -->

**Original Meaning.** This explains the hexagram's name through its structure, images, and virtues. Some explain firmness above and yieldingness below through hexagram transformation: Constancy comes from Abundance, with firmness rising to the second place and yieldingness descending to the first. This too is intelligible.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:026 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:027 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Constancy means lasting a long time. Four features in the figure's capacities establish this meaning. Firmness above and yieldingness below means that Qian's first line rises to the fourth place, while Kun's first line descends to the first: the firm line goes above and the yielding line below. Their exchange of places completes Zhen and Xun. Zhen above and Xun below are likewise firmness above and yieldingness below. Firmness occupying the upper place and yieldingness the lower is the constant Way. Thunder and wind assist one another: when thunder shakes, wind arises. Each requires the other and strengthens its force. Hence they assist one another; this is their regular course. Deference joined to movement means Xun's deference below and Zhen's movement above: moving through deference. Heaven and Earth's creative transformations endure without cease through compliant movement alone. Deference joined to movement is the Way of lasting constancy. How could movement without compliance endure? Firm and yielding lines all respond: throughout the hexagram, every firm line answers a yielding one. Such correspondence is the regularity of principle. These four features constitute the Way of constancy and make the hexagram Constancy.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:027 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:028 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:028 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:029 -->

**Zheng Ruxie:** In both Influence and Constancy, firmness and yieldingness correspond. Influence does not bring out this meaning; Constancy expressly says that they all respond. Influence concerns freedom from deliberate intention; Constancy concerns established places. Where places are established, correspondence between firmness and yieldingness is their proper principle. Where freedom from deliberate intention is at issue, such fixed correspondence would be private attachment. Only one who recognizes how the meaning appropriate to the time changes can speak of the *Changes*.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:029 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:030 -->

### “Constancy: success, no blame; steadfastness is beneficial” means enduring in one's Way. The Way of Heaven and Earth endures constantly, without cease.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:030 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:031 -->

**Original Meaning.** Constancy can indeed bring success without blame. Yet correctness must be beneficial before this counts as enduring in one's Way. Without correctness, one endures in what is not one's Way. Heaven and Earth's Way lasts constantly through correctness alone.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:031 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:032 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The Way of constancy can bring free passage without fault or blame, but what is maintained must be correct. Once correctness is lost, it is no Way in which one should persist. Hence “enduring in one's Way”: that Way is a correct Way worthy of constancy. Neither failing to maintain one's virtue nor persisting in incorrectness can bring success; both incur blame. Heaven and Earth never cease because they possess a Way of lasting constancy. A person who remains constant in what merits constancy accords with Heaven and Earth's principle.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:032 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:033 -->

### “It is beneficial to have somewhere to go”: an ending gives way to a beginning.[^j10-heng-change]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:033 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:034 -->

**Original Meaning.** Enduring in one's Way is the ending; benefit in having somewhere to go is the beginning. Movement and stillness generate one another: the principle of cyclical return. Yet stillness must remain the governing ground.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:034 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:035 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Among the principles of the world, nothing can endure without movement. Through movement an ending becomes a beginning again; thus constancy is inexhaustible. Of all that Heaven and Earth generate, even mountains in their strength and mass cannot remain without change. Constancy therefore does not mean being fixed once and for all. What is fixed cannot endure. Only changing with the time is the constant Way. Hence benefit in having somewhere to go. The text makes this principle clear lest people cling to an unchanging routine.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:035 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:036 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:036 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:037 -->

**Zhu Zhen:** “The *Changes*: at an impasse, change; through change, free passage; through free passage, endurance.” Constancy is not being fixed and unchanging. One changes with the time, while one's constancy remains unmoved. Hence benefit in having somewhere to go.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:037 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:038 -->

**Recorded Conversations of Master Zhu.** Constancy does not mean being fixed once and for all; what is fixed cannot endure. The constancy of the underlying reality makes possible change in its activity; change in its activity is precisely what sustains the constancy of the underlying reality.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:038 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:039 -->

**Zhao Rumei:** Having somewhere to go is valued because, although an affair ends, my action does not cease. What has ended thus begins again. This is how one embodies the Way of Heaven and Earth.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:039 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:040 -->

### The sun and moon accord with Heaven and can therefore shine enduringly. The four seasons change and transform and can therefore bring things to completion enduringly. The sage endures in his Way, and the world is transformed and brought to completion. Observe what they hold constant, and the true dispositions of Heaven, Earth, and the myriad things can be seen.[^j10-heng-cosmos]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:040 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:041 -->

**Original Meaning.** This carries the Way of enduring constancy to its fullest extent.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:041 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:042 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** This fully expounds constant principle. The sun and moon are the refined energies of yin and yang. Only because they follow Heaven's Way in their coming and going, increase and decrease, can they shine enduringly without cease. According with Heaven means following Heaven's principle. The four seasons, too, are yin and yang's energies, coming and going, changing and transforming, generating and completing the myriad things. They likewise accord with Heaven and therefore endure without cease. The sage practices the enduring Way with constancy, and the world follows his transforming influence into fine customs. Observing what they hold constant means observing why the sun and moon shine enduringly, why the seasons bring things enduringly to completion, and why the sage's Way can last. In this the true dispositions and principles of Heaven, Earth, and the myriad things become visible. Who but one who knows the Way could recognize Heaven and Earth's enduring Way and the world's enduring principles?

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:042 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:043 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:043 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:044 -->

**Su Shi:** Whatever does not express one's deepest disposition becomes wearisome with time.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:044 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:045 -->

**Recorded Conversations of Master Zhu.** Each thing has its dispositions. Wherever there is a person, there are certainly feelings of compassion, shame and aversion, approval and disapproval, and courtesy and deference. Nature is one reality, but feelings take many forms, sometimes arising, sometimes subsiding. Yet their fundamental character remains the same, always so. This is the meaning of observing what is held constant and thereby seeing the true dispositions of Heaven, Earth, and the myriad things.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:045 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:046 -->

**Gong Huan:** “Steadfastness is beneficial: enduring in one's Way” embodies constancy. “Having somewhere to go is beneficial: an ending gives way to a beginning” fully realizes change. Only by embodying constancy can one fully realize change; fully realizing change is also how one embodies constancy. Heaven, Earth, and the myriad things endure because they can fully realize change.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:046 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:047 -->

**Chen Chen:** Observe the principle of their enduring constancy, and the true dispositions of Heaven, Earth, and the myriad things can be seen. The great vital energy fills everything as an undivided whole, while the Supreme Ultimate gives it order and direction. Energy varies, but principle is everywhere one. Thus Heaven is high, Earth low, and the myriad things dispersed in their differences. Not only do their sounds, colors, forms, and appearances endure in this way; their virtues, natures, powers, and activities also remain unchanged through all ages. Even a slight deviation becomes a strange and inauspicious anomaly. In this one sees that Heaven, Earth, and the myriad things all possess constancy in their dispositions.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:047 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:048 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Explaining beneficial steadfastness as enduring in one's Way means remaining in one's proper place without shifting. Explaining beneficial advance as an ending giving way to a beginning means inexhaustible movement and stillness. These two meanings proceed together without conflict. Though movement and stillness never cease, that which remains in its proper place never changes. Heaven and Earth's Way enduring without cease and an ending giving way to a beginning therefore have a single meaning. The ensuing discussion of Heaven, Earth, sun, and moon develops precisely this. The sun and moon according with Heaven and shining enduringly exemplify unceasing constancy; the seasons changing and bringing things to completion exemplify endings giving way to beginnings. The sun and moon are the underlying basis, the four seasons their activity. The seasons are what the sun and moon produce; together they are Heaven and Earth's Way. The sage's enduring in his Way resembles the sun and moon's enduring light; his transforming and completing the world resembles the seasons' enduring work of transformation and completion. These are the great manifestations of constancy's Way. Extend this further, and the true dispositions of everything between Heaven and Earth become visible.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:048 -->

## 33. Dun 遯 — Retreat

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:049 -->

### “Retreat: success”: through retreat comes success. Firmness occupies its proper place and has a response; it moves with the time.[^j10-dun-action]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:049 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:050 -->

**Original Meaning.** The meaning of success is explained through the fifth nine alone.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:050 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:051 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** When the petty person's Way is growing, the noble person's withdrawal is the free passage of his Way. The noble person withdraws into concealment so that the Way may find expression. This describes how to meet Retreat. From “firmness occupies its proper place and has a response” onward, however, the text considers the time and the figure's capacities, showing that action may still be possible. Even in Retreat's time, the noble person is not necessarily obliged to withdraw. The fifth possesses yang-firm virtue in a centered, correct position; below, the second six responds to it with centered correctness. Although yin is growing, someone with the figure's capacities should still follow the time's waxing and waning. Wherever effort remains possible, he does his utmost with complete sincerity to uphold his Way. He need not invariably hide and cease to act. Hence “moves with the time.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:051 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:052 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:052 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:053 -->

**Kong Yingda:** This explains how Retreat attains free passage. The petty person's Way is growing; without withdrawing, the noble person cannot find passage. Hence “through retreat comes success.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:053 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:054 -->

**He also said:** This explains why withdrawal can bring success. It is because the fifth nine, firm and properly placed, has a response in the second. Its withdrawal is not obstructed obstinacy. To withdraw without such obstinacy is to act with regard to the time, and therefore to attain success through retreat.[^j10-dun-readings]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:054 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:055 -->

**Guo Zhongxiao:** The sage's advance and retreat are both the Way; in every situation he is at ease with himself. Even withdrawing, he finds success. Moving with the time means stopping when it is time to stop and acting when it is time to act. This is Retreat's meaning.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:055 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:056 -->

**Recorded Conversations of Master Zhu.** Question: “‘Retreat: success; through retreat comes success’ clearly says that being able to withdraw brings success. What, then, does the further statement mean: ‘Firmness occupies its proper place and has a response; it moves with the time’?” Answer: “This explains why withdrawal brings success. Yin is still slight. Because firmness occupies its proper place and has a response, it recognizes the time and withdraws. This is moving with the time. Otherwise, it would be turning its back upon the time.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:056 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:057 -->

**Wu Yueshen:** Firmness properly placed and finding a response does not mean that success remains possible without retreat. Precisely because it is properly placed and has a response, it can follow the time and withdraw; hence success. Moving with the time means withdrawing when withdrawal is called for.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:057 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:058 -->

### “A little benefit in steadfastness”: it grows by degrees.[^j10-dun-small]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:058 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:059 -->

**Original Meaning.** The two yin below explain the little benefit in steadfastness.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:059 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:060 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:060 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:061 -->

**Hu Yuan:** The noble person cannot accomplish great things in the world and finds only a little benefit in steadfastness because the yin below grow by degrees and the petty people's faction gradually flourishes.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:061 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:062 -->

**Zhu Zhen:** The gradual growth of two yin still differs from Obstruction's “unfavorable to the noble person's steadfastness.” Yet steadfastness on a great scale is not possible; only a small measure is beneficial. Earlier scholars spoke of holding a minor office and managing small affairs: the harm is not yet severe, and one's purpose can still be carried out. Retreat does not mean hating the age, shunning its customs, and departing forever. Leaving or staying, delaying or hastening: everything depends on the time. Unless one keeps the ruler in mind, does not abandon one's fellows, and follows Heaven through waxing, waning, fullness, and emptiness, how could one fully realize Retreat's timely meaning?

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:062 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:063 -->

**Zhang Qingzi:** Two yang make Approach; two yin make Retreat. Retreat is Approach's reversed counterpart. Approach's Commentary says, “Firmness gradually grows.” Retreat's does not say, “Yieldingness gradually grows,” but only, “It grows by degrees.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:063 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:064 -->

### Great indeed is the meaning of Retreat's time!

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:064 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:065 -->

**Original Meaning.** Yin is gradually growing, and handling the situation is difficult. Thus the meaning of this time is especially great.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:065 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:066 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** When yin is growing, steadfastness on a great scale is impossible, yet a little remains beneficial because yin necessarily grows gradually and cannot at once become abundant. The noble person can still preserve his Way in a small measure. This little benefit in steadfastness means sustaining it so that it is not yet extinguished. Retreat marks yin's first growth. The noble person recognizes the subtle beginning and must therefore be deeply vigilant. Yet the sage's intention is not simply to stop at once; hence the teachings of moving with the time and finding a little benefit in steadfastness. Though sages and worthies know the Way is about to be abandoned, would they sit and watch disorder without seeking to relieve it? They must apply themselves while matters have not yet reached their limit, strengthening what is declining on this side and impeding the other's advance, seeking even temporary security. Whatever can be done, Confucius and Mencius would consider worth doing. Wang Yun in the Han and Xie An in the Jin exemplify this. Where there is a means of changing the situation and a prospect of success, the case needs no further argument. This is the Way of meeting Retreat's time. Hence the sage praises the greatness of its timely meaning. Whether one remains long or departs quickly, the meaning is great in either case.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:066 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:067 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:067 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:068 -->

**Guo Yong:** Those who do not understand Retreat's little benefit in steadfastness or Opposition's good fortune in small affairs take them as insignificant and give them no thought. Only after Confucius makes their greatness clear do we understand that great practical significance lies within them.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:068 -->

## 34. Dazhuang 大壯 — Great Strength

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:069 -->

### Great Strength: the great is strong. Firmness expressed in movement gives strength.[^j10-dazhuang-strength]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:069 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:070 -->

**Original Meaning.** This explains the hexagram's name. In its structure, yang grows beyond the middle: the great is strong. In its virtues, Qian's firmness and Zhen's movement account for strength.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:070 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:071 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The name Great Strength means that the great is strong. Yin is small and yang great. Yang grows and flourishes: the great is strong. With firmness below and movement above, one moves through Qian's utmost firmness. Hence Great Strength, meaning both the great becoming strong and the greatness of strength.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:071 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:072 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:182 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Cai's and Lin's explanations have been followed in succession. On reflection, neither fully accounts for the hexagram's meaning. Only through these do above and below communicate and share their purpose. ### Decreasing with sincerity brings supreme good fortune and no blame. One may be steadfast; having somewhere to go is beneficial. What is to be used? Two vessels may serve for the offering. <!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:183 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:184 -->

**Original Meaning.** This explains the hexagram statement. The time means the time when decrease is appropriate.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:184 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:185 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Decreasing with utmost sincerity brings the four benefits beginning with supreme good fortune: the complete goodness of the Way of decrease. The Master gives particular attention to “What is to be used? Two vessels may serve for the offering.” The Judgment states briefly and directly that superficial ornament should be reduced: what need be used? Two vessels can make the offering. This means strengthening the root and reducing the branches. Fearing that later readers would misunderstand and conclude that all ornament should be removed, the Master explains at length. Where there is a root, there must be branches; where substance, pattern. This holds for every affair. Without a root, nothing stands; without pattern, nothing proceeds. Affection governs father and son, yet there must be forms of authority and compliance. Reverence governs ruler and minister, yet there must be ceremonies of reception and attendance. Courtesy and deference reside within but require dignified forms before they can be practiced. Higher and lower have their order, but without differentiated objects and insignia that order is not distinguished. Pattern and substance require one another; neither may be lacking. Only when ornament predominates and the branches wander far, losing root and substance, is it time for decrease. Hence: what need be used? Two vessels suffice to present one's sincerity. One should attend to substance and reduce ornament. Fearing that people would cling to the words, the Master further says that the simplicity of two vessels has its fitting time. It is wrong to employ it where it is unsuitable—to reduce ornament before it is excessive or to reduce it too severely. There is also a time for diminishing firmness and increasing yieldingness. Firmness signifies excess and yieldingness deficiency; decrease and increase both diminish the firm to benefit the yielding. They must follow the time. Applied at an unsuitable time, either is wrong. **Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:186 -->

**Xu Ji:** The Judgment says “Decrease, sincerity”; the Judgment Commentary says “decrease with sincerity.” Adding the connecting word makes the meaning plain.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:187 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:188 -->

**Zhang Qingzi:** Time means what is fitting. Decreasing when decrease is appropriate meets the time; decreasing when it is inappropriate does not.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4638:188 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4638:189 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Cheng's explanation could be applied to Adornment. Here, however, decrease means that circumstances require economizing: a household adjusts to its wealth or poverty, a state spends more or less according to abundance or famine. Hence “decreasing with sincerity.” Though the time makes reduction unavoidable, sincerity must guide it, as when one cannot provide every sacrificial offering but presents what one has with utmost sincerity. The two vessels illustrate reduction. Yet the later phrase “diminishing firmness and increasing yieldingness” does not use diminished firmness to symbolize the two vessels. Firmness is the root, imaging sincere trust; yieldingness is the branches, imaging ceremonial forms and offerings. Let abundant sincerity make good the insufficiency of the offerings. Then even two vessels need not be judged inadequate. This is the meaning of diminishing firmness to increase yieldingness.

**Editorial Judgment.** Four yang in the center image a ridgepole; four yin outside image feathers. When the noble person undertakes great affairs, he becomes a ridgepole supporting the world. Because their yang or yin are excessive in number, they are called Great Excess and Small Excess. Some affairs use excess to attain the mean; there is no fault in such excess. Yet only excess that does not lose the mean returns to freedom from excess. In the ridgepole, what is disfavored is excessive rigidity that breaks and excessive weight that bends. It should therefore rise above, not sag below. In feathers, what is disfavored is softness without stability and lightness without restraint. They should settle below, not fly aloft. Great Excess says firmness exceeds while centered: not bending downward is the mean of firmness. Small Excess says yieldingness attains the center and should descend rather than ascend: this is the mean of yieldingness.[^j10-xiaoguo-ridge]

## Translator's Endnotes

The base is the repository's [Chinese Juan Ten](../source/juan-10.md). “Parallel text” below means the *Siku Quanshu* transcription in [Wikisource, fixed revision 528580](https://zh.wikisource.org/w/index.php?title=御纂周易折中_%28四庫全書本%29/卷10&oldid=528580), consulted on September 26, 2026. Comparison has been selective, not a systematic collation against historical facsimiles. Agreement between two transcriptions does not independently establish a reading. The Chinese base remains unchanged. Attested corrections, intelligible variants retained, repunctuation, and the commentators' own conjectures are distinguished below. The supplementary paragraph under Abundance belongs to the parallel witness, not the source-aligned main text. “Editorial Judgment” always means the imperial compilers, never the translator.

[^j10-layer]: **The second half of the Judgment Commentary.** 彖下傳 concerns hexagrams 31–64 and continues [Juan Nine](juan-09.md). Its 97 separately presented canonical passages are not additional oracle statements. The compilation attributes this Commentary to Confucius; that traditional attribution is preserved as the source's account, not presented as an independently established authorship finding. Lines are numbered upward from the bottom; six means yin and nine yang. Formal correctness ordinarily means yang in an odd place and yin in an even one, while centeredness refers to the second or fifth. Ethical correctness is not always identical to positional correctness.

[^j10-xian-heart]: **Influence without calculation, not without a heart.** The commentators play upon 咸 and 感: the latter adds 心, heart. Qiu expressly says that influence requires the heart, then distinguishes this from deliberately setting one's heart on influencing others. 無心 therefore does not mean unconsciousness, emotional numbness, or literal absence of mind. 咸 also means “all,” supporting the claim that uncontrived responsiveness reaches all alike. The discussion is traditional interpretation of the written forms, not a modern demonstration of their historical etymology.

[^j10-xian-structure]: **Two accounts of the figure.** Zhu permits an account in which The Wanderer's fifth and sixth lines exchange places to produce Influence. Cheng describes the formation of the constituent trigrams through exchanges involving Qian and Kun, then also reads the completed configuration as woman above man. These are explanatory constructions, not directions to change lines in a consultation. The marriage image specifically gives weight to the man's humbling himself before the woman; it must not be flattened into an undifferentiated claim about attraction.

[^j10-xian-dispositions]: **情 as feeling and disposition.** 情 can designate manifest feeling in people and the actual dispositions or characteristic responses of things. “True dispositions” accommodates the latter without making stones and seasons possess human emotions in a simple sense. Zhang Qingzi contrasts still nature, 性, with feeling as responsive manifestation. The compilers' tradition understands responsiveness through sincerity and principle, not merely through the strength of an emotion or an unexamined intuition.

[^j10-heng-structure]: **Constancy's four grounds.** Cheng identifies firmness above yieldingness, thunder and wind assisting one another, deferential movement, and three corresponding yin-yang pairs. His “Kun's first line descends to the first” concerns the original trigram line and its place in the resulting six-line figure, not a movement from a hexagram's first place to itself. Zheng contrasts appropriate correspondence in established roles with the private attachment that would compromise Influence's uncontrived responsiveness. One diagrammatic relation can thus carry different value in different hexagrams.

[^j10-heng-change]: **Constancy through change.** 體 and 用 distinguish an underlying reality or constitution from its activity. Zhu's formulation is reciprocal: constancy makes changing activity possible, and changing activity sustains constancy. Cheng rejects treating 恒 as a state fixed once and for all. Zhu nevertheless preserves stillness as the governing ground of the alternation. The translation retains both commitments rather than turning the passage into either a doctrine of total fixity or a celebration of change for its own sake.

[^j10-heng-cosmos]: **Order and variation.** Chen Chen's contrast between varying vital energy and unified principle allows diversity without abandoning enduring order. His assertion that small departures become ominous anomalies belongs to this inherited cosmology. It should not erase Cheng's preceding affirmation that mountains and every other generated thing change. The authors are discussing different senses of constancy: stable characteristic order, changing manifestations, and faithful conduct. Their claims are retained in their own voices.

[^j10-dun-action]: **Retreat or continued service?** Cheng explicitly leaves room for useful action before the situation becomes irretrievable, and later invokes Wang Yun and Xie An as historical examples. Zhu and Wu read the fifth's correct position and response as what enables timely withdrawal, not as grounds for staying. These are substantive alternatives, not two paraphrases of the same counsel. The histories are cited here as Cheng's analogies; the chapter does not independently establish that the cases vindicated every aspect of his reading.

[^j10-dun-readings]: **Corrections in Retreat.** In Kong Yingda's second gloss, 艮由 in the base is read as the parallel's 良由, “precisely because.” In Cheng's concluding commentary, the duplicated 雖雖 is read as the parallel's single 雖. 否亢 in Kong's gloss is retained and rendered “obstructed obstinacy”: the point is that withdrawal is not a defiant refusal to take account of circumstances. No new hexagram or line designation is inferred from the accidental 艮.

[^j10-dun-small]: **The subject of “small.”** Zhu relates 小利貞 to the two yin growing below. Other collected explanations address the noble person's remaining scope for limited steadfast action. The comparison with Obstruction matters: two yin have appeared, not yet the three of Obstruction, so the conditions are not treated as identical. “By degrees” emphasizes an emerging tendency, not a calendar prediction specifying when departure becomes compulsory.

[^j10-dazhuang-strength]: **Firmness before force.** 剛 and 壯 are not interchangeable: firmness is the quality through which movement becomes genuine strength. Xiang contrasts this with movement driven by 血氣, the impulses of blood and vital energy. The latter is not condemned as the body's existence, but as an insufficient basis for the strength being praised. The compilers likewise make correctness, rather than the mere power to prevail, the implication of the figure's virtues.

[^j10-dazhuang-dispositions]: **Greatness and correctness remain distinct.** Cheng explicitly prefers 正大 to a formulation that might be mistaken for one undifferentiated quality. Hu Bingwen links the passage to Mencius's great, firm vital energy nourished through uprightness. This is his account of the texts' relationship. The translation does not turn his statement that one doctrine arose from the other into an independently verified chronology of composition.

[^j10-jin-clarity]: **What attaches to what?** Cheng reads Kun's compliant minister attaching himself to Li's illuminated ruler. The compilers object that 麗, attachment, belongs to Li and argue instead that clarity requires compliance as its supporting ground. Both accounts remain explicit. 康侯 is rendered “the lord who brings security,” following the explanation here, rather than being fixed to an unnamed historical individual. The horses and repeated royal receptions remain concrete marks of favor, not substitutions for inward psychological states.

[^j10-mingyi-wen]: **Bearing darkness without becoming dark.** 蒙 means bearing or suffering here, not Meng as a separate hexagram. The historical frame is King Wen's imprisonment under the Shang ruler Zhou. Inward cultivated clarity and outward compliance are differentiated deliberately: the latter does not mean surrendering the former. These are the compilation's traditional historical identifications. Zhou 紂, the ruler, should not be confused with the Zhou 周 dynasty named in the book's title.

[^j10-mingyi-reading]: **“The last period of Shang.”** Wang Shenzi's 商之未造 in the base is corrected to the parallel's 商之末造. 末造 means the closing period, not a Shang order “not yet created.” The parallel itself contains an apparent error in Cheng's reference to Kun, so it has not been followed mechanically. The translation preserves the base where its meaning is coherent.

[^j10-mingyi-jizi]: **Adversity within the royal house.** Jizi is identified as a close relative of the ruler, and “within” therefore has both familial and political force. The comparison distinguishes King Wen's composed endurance, revealing virtue, from Jizi's humiliation, revealing steadfast purpose. Hu Bingwen's references to Fuxi's Changes and Great Yu's Categories place them within a traditional genealogy of the cultural inheritance. “Categories” refers to the ordered teachings associated with the Great Plan, not to another divination system introduced here.

[^j10-family-order]: **Historical roles retained.** The division between the woman's inner place and the man's outer place, and the authority of both parents, belong to the source's familial and political order. They have not been replaced with present-day egalitarian household roles or generalized “energies.” A reader can distinguish the text's claims from their own judgment of those claims. 嚴君 means an authoritative, respected head: it does not by itself specify cruelty or physical punishment.

[^j10-family-world]: **A household and the world.** Cheng extends the Way of one family to the world. Lin cautions against treating world peace simply as the effect of putting one household in order: his emphasis is on people everywhere cherishing their own parents and respecting their own elders. The distinction remains rather than being reduced to a modern slogan about domestic harmony automatically producing social harmony.

[^j10-family-lines]: **Six lines, several relationships.** 三陽四陰 in the compilers' discussion means “the third yang and the fourth yin,” not three yang plus four yin. The same line can signify a parent, spouse, or sibling according to the relation being considered. The second and fifth govern the interpretation, but the first and top complete another father-son image. These assignments are interpretive comparisons, not six permanently fixed human identities in every reading.

[^j10-kui-difference]: **Difference presupposes relation.** Cheng says that opposition here arises among things previously together. The women share a household before marrying into different households; this is not an assertion that any two women must quarrel. The compilers add their own theory of an eldest legitimate heir providing settled order. That historically specific explanation is preserved without being presented as a universal account of human relationships.

[^j10-kui-small]: **Why good qualities yield only limited success.** The compilers stress that the virtues here resemble those of The Cauldron, yet the time differs. Favorable qualities do not cancel opposition's circumstances. Cheng likewise treats the second and fifth's relationship as insufficiently settled, despite their correspondence. He Kai's enumeration of the eight figures with Li above explains the special standing of their yielding fifth lines; it does not negate every unfavorable judgment concerning such lines.

[^j10-kui-use]: **Distinctness can enable cooperation.** The concluding passage turns from discord to differences that make shared activity possible. Zhao Rumei calls distinction the constitution and union the activity. Heaven and Earth need not become identical to cooperate; human beings need not erase every difference to communicate. The translation therefore sometimes uses “distinct” rather than “opposed” for 睽 in this passage. The source's two senses remain related but not collapsed.

[^j10-jian-stopping]: **Prudent stopping and the duty to help.** Xiang distinguishes being stopped by an external obstruction from stopping because one has discerned danger. Wang Shenzi further distinguishes managing one's position within difficulty from assuming responsibility for remedying difficulty: the second and fifth must not simply withdraw from their responsibilities. Thus “seeing danger and stopping” is not a universal instruction to avoid all difficult action.

[^j10-jian-center]: **Direction and the mean.** Cheng explains southwest through Kun's level compliance and northeast through Gen's obstruction; the compilers prefer appropriate advance and retreat without forcing them into trigram correspondences. The fifth's central position and the ethical mean are likewise not identical in every gloss. The discussion does not require substituting a modern geographical location for either traditional direction. Its disagreement continues the fuller treatment in [Juan Five](juan-05.md).

[^j10-jie-release]: **Two kinds of reversal.** He Kai compares Release with Limping by reversing the six-line figure, and with Zhun by exchanging the two component trigrams. These are different operations. His progression from stopping beneath danger, to moving within it, to moving beyond it compares the three situations rather than prescribing a sequence of divination casts.

[^j10-jie-return]: **After rescue, what remains to do?** Cheng distinguishes emergency relief from settled good government; returning means restoring the latter. Zhu maps the phrases onto the figure's transformed lines, while the compilers read them through appropriate withdrawal and renewed foundations. Qiu's qualification is important: do not create needless work after trouble is relieved, but do not leave unfinished dangers unattended merely because circumstances have improved.

[^j10-jie-shells]: **Breaking coverings.** 甲坼 refers to germinating life splitting its enclosing coverings. “Shells” here includes seed coats and bud coverings, not only hard nutshells. The thunder-and-rain image is extended to pardon, nourishment, and the ruler's concern for living things. It remains an analogy of renewed growth, not a claim that every kind of fruit germinates simultaneously in one meteorological event.

[^j10-sun-upward]: **Three readings of upward movement.** Cai reads benefit passing upward; Lin reads the damage of depletion also reaching upward. The compilers object that both concentrate too narrowly on extracting wealth and instead include ministerial devotion and public labor as communication between below and above. Their argument belongs to the political ethics of the compilation. Preserving it does not require treating every upward transfer of resources as beneficial, nor every such transfer as inherently destructive.

[^j10-sun-readings]: **Corrections in Decrease.** Cai's 圭所以為損 in the base is read with the parallel as 卦所以為損, “this is why the hexagram is Decrease.” In Cheng's account of family and ritual, 父子主思 is corrected to 父子主恩, “affection governs father and son.” Conversely, the parallel's 元咎 in the canonical passage is not adopted over the base's coherent 无咎, “no blame.” A second transcription supplies useful evidence, not blanket authority.

[^j10-sun-vessels]: **Substance, ritual, and necessity.** The two 簋 are sacrificial vessels, not a claim that any two everyday bowls constitute a complete rite. Cheng interprets reduction through the balance of substance and ornament. The compilers instead begin from circumstances requiring economy and let sincerity compensate for insufficient material offerings. Their use of diminishing firmness to increase yieldingness concerns the sharing of an abundant inward resource; it does not recommend making sincerity weaker. Both interpretations are translated without making one the other's paraphrase.

[^j10-yi-wood]: **Wood or increase?** The canonical 木道乃行 is retained as “the Way of wood proceeds.” Cheng proposes 益 in place of 木, giving “the Way of increase proceeds.” Zhu Zhen's subsequent comparison of three Xun figures supports retaining wood and its relation to boats. This is a recorded historical emendation and disagreement, not a transcription correction made by this translation. Cheng's proposed reading is expressed within his own commentary only.

[^j10-yi-time]: **Benefit cannot simply be forced.** Zhu reads bestowal and generation through Qian and Kun, while the compilers link wind with Heaven's bestowal and thunder with Earth's generation. Their final insistence on the arrival of the appropriate seasonal energies applies the requirement of timing to human action as well as natural change. It is not a promise that effort is unnecessary once a favorable hexagram appears.

[^j10-guai-harmony]: **Decision without hatred.** 決 and 和 remain distinct: decisive removal is to retain harmony and compassion. He Kai explicitly denies that noble people should regard petty people merely as enemies or things. 小人 is the source's moral-political category; its appearance in a divination text does not establish that a particular modern opponent deserves that label. The retained argument is about the quality and justification of action, not force alone.

[^j10-guai-vigilance]: **Danger and awareness of danger.** 其危乃光 can include danger as an external condition and apprehension as an inward response. Cheng explains how sincere warning and vigilance avoid unforeseen trouble. “Announce within one's own domain” preserves the instruction to begin with one's own affairs before resorting to arms. The final yang growth is not yet complete: the top yin remains. The commentators do not treat numerical superiority as a reason to dispense with caution.

[^j10-guai-line-links]: **Compressed cross-references.** Xiang links the Commentary to Breakthrough's second-line night alarm, its fifth-line Image about a center not yet illuminated, and the top line's final misfortune. These are correspondences within the hexagram's inherited texts, not three new instructions. Wu's basket-and-mound comparisons contrast commencing a task with failing just before completion. A ren is a traditional measure of height; converting the metaphor into a precise modern measurement would add nothing to its point.

[^j10-gou-encounter]: **Who takes the initiative?** 趙汝楳 expressly distinguishes yieldingness meeting firmness from firmness meeting yieldingness. Lin allows the different orientations of Zhu's explanation and the Commentary, relating the latter to the warning that follows. The hen announcing the dawn is a traditional image of a reversal of authority. These directional and political assumptions are not silently removed from the translation.

[^j10-gou-marriage]: **An exclusionary historical analogy.** Cheng groups women, petty people, and 夷狄 within an argument about rising subordinate power. “Yi and Di peoples” retains the historical cultural labels without replacing them with a modern ethnic identification or presenting the hierarchy as a fact about human worth. The contrast with Influence depends on courtship initiative and ritual propriety in this tradition, not merely on the presence of sexual attraction.

[^j10-gou-good-meeting]: **Necessary encounters as well as dangerous ones.** The passage about Heaven and Earth praises encounters that generate life. Cheng extends this to rulers and ministers, sages and worthies, and effective affairs. Zhu's brief gloss identifies the fifth, while Cheng also includes the second in the meeting through centered correctness. The second is not formally a yang line in an odd position; Cheng's language here includes ethical centeredness rather than giving a universal positional definition.

[^j10-gou-two-sides]: **Why the praise does not cancel the warning.** Zhu emphasizes the first, almost imperceptible emergence of what may become harmful; Cheng emphasizes what cannot be achieved without meeting. Wu says the final praise embraces both. The compilers then distinguish proper meetings from concealed corruption intruding into them. The translation preserves this debate rather than treating Coming to Meet as either wholly auspicious or wholly an occasion for exclusion.

[^j10-cui-readings]: **Corrections and repunctuation in Gathering.** Cheng's 故所聚 in the base is read as the parallel's 故能聚, “thus gathering is possible.” The compilers' 推原能以聚 is read as 推原所以聚, tracing what makes gathering possible. Hu Bingwen's compressed contrast is punctuated as “Influence's disposition is communication; Constancy's, endurance; Gathering's, unity.” That last clarification changes punctuation, not characters. The voices remain separate despite their overlapping vocabulary.

[^j10-cui-temple]: **Temple, offering, and sincerity.** The king's coming to the ancestral temple is both a concrete rite and an image of gathering human hearts. Cheng's explanation also speaks of establishing the temple, rather than only entering a building already present. The great victim is a substantial sacrificial offering appropriate to gathered resources. It is not a claim that extravagance is always superior to simplicity: the comparison depends on the situation, as Decrease's two vessels show.

[^j10-cui-union]: **Gathering as a relation, not mere accumulation.** Hu distinguishes communication, endurance, and unity while finding principle in each. Wang Bi's shared dispositions and concordant energies describe why things gather. The compilers' conclusion joins ancestral belonging, shared kinds, and the sage's public role. These are not identical kinds of association. “Gathering” consequently remains broader than either stockpiling goods or forming a private party.

[^j10-sheng-root]: **The first line gives ascent a root.** The compilers object that a yielding fourth or fifth, or Kun above, cannot by itself distinguish Pushing Upward from other figures. Xun below provides the root, and its first yin supplies the beginning of growth. Their comparison with Without Falsity concerns the governing quality expanding through the whole figure. It does not instruct the diviner to replace the received hexagram with its reversed counterpart.

[^j10-sheng-yuan]: **元亨 and 大亨.** Both expressions can be translated “great success,” but Cheng distinguishes them and proposes that this Commentary mistakenly writes 大亨 for 元亨. The distinction was discussed under Great Possession in [Juan Nine](juan-09.md#ch14). The English therefore retains *yuan heng* and *da heng* where the disagreement is explicitly discussed, while translating the canonical phrase normally. Cheng's proposed correction is not silently inserted into that phrase.

[^j10-sheng-south]: **Direction and audience.** Cheng takes south as the direction a person faces and therefore as forward advancement. “Seeing the great person” can mean obtaining office through a ruler or advancing in the Way through a sage or worthy. The interpretation does not reduce every great person to a worldly patron. Nor does it require an actual modern journey south; its directional explanation belongs to the source's conventions.

[^j10-kun-passage]: **Where success is located.** 困而不失其所亨 allows a close connection between “one's proper ground” and “wherein one finds passage.” Cheng explicitly distinguishes outward prosperity from the flourishing of one's Way. The compilers further distinguish being joyful while in danger from danger itself offering a means toward joy. The English keeps the initially open canonical wording and then makes each interpretation explicit, rather than promising material success whenever oppression appears.

[^j10-kun-reading]: **A missing conditional connection.** The base reads 非剛中，剛遇困 in Cheng's commentary. The parallel has 非剛中則遇困: without firm centeredness, encountering oppression leads to loss of correctness. The English follows the latter. The correction restores the conditional link; it does not add another firm line or a new claim about a specific line's position.

[^j10-well-lifting]: **The well-sweep and its work.** Zheng Kangcheng explains Xun's wood as a 桔橰, a lifting device that lowers the vessel and raises water. The actual mechanism remains in the translation. Its role supports the later distinction between possessing a well and making its water available. “Nourishing without exhaustion” states the commentarial ideal of the well's continuing usefulness; it is not a hydrological guarantee concerning an actual well.

[^j10-well-rope]: **The unfinished act.** “The rope has not drawn clear of the well” continues the wording used in [Juan Seven](juan-07.md#eee-4620). The commentators' emphasis is that water not yet available for use has not accomplished the well's purpose. They also explain why the Tuan does not repeat two clauses found in the Judgment: constancy already includes both freedom from loss and gain and service to those coming and going. Their absence here is not an omission by the translator.

[^j10-ge-extinction]: **息: ending and renewed life.** Both principal commentaries distinguish extinguishing or cessation from generation, linking new life to the ending of a prior condition. The contrast with Opposition is correspondingly stronger than mere difference. The water-and-fire explanations are the commentators' accounts of the images. In particular, the claims about moving water and still lake water should not be substituted for a general physical law about extinguishing fires.

[^j10-ge-inner-dui]: **Dui in figures without an outer Dui trigram.** Zhu Zhen mentions Dui in Small Restraint and Great Restraint, although neither has Dui as one of its two main trigrams. Both contain Dui in their second through fourth lines. This offers a coherent nuclear-trigram reading of his wording; it is an interpretive identification, not a correction to the text. His larger point is the relation between the water imagery of Kan and Dui.

[^j10-ge-day]: **The elapsed day.** The base writes 巳日, while the parallel writes 已日. The English follows the commentators' explanation of a day passing before trust is secured, consistent with [Juan Seven](juan-07.md#eee-4621). It does not impose a calculation using a named si day of the calendrical cycle. Cheng also distinguishes initially explaining a change from the longer process by which doubtful people may come to trust it.

[^j10-ge-syntax]: **Small connecting words carry arguments.** The compilers repeatedly distinguish 以 from 而, and show that the order of a construction with 以 may vary. The English consequently uses such formulations as “through,” “expressed in,” and “joined to” according to the argument, rather than one inflexible equivalent. This is particularly important where clarity or compliance is the governing ground of action, not merely an additional quality beside it.

[^j10-ge-mandate]: **Changing the mandate.** 革命 here means replacement of a dynasty's mandate, illustrated by Tang and Wu. “Revolution” as the hexagram name therefore does not exclude political transformation, but neither does every occurrence designate a modern popular revolution. The legitimating account of following Heaven and answering the people is the compilation's traditional historical argument, not an independently tested rule licensing any claimed reformer.

[^j10-ge-mending]: **Repair versus recasting.** Zhu's metalworking analogy distinguishes patching worn vessels from casting them anew. 錮露 in the base and parallel is not silently replaced with a different phrase merely because the spelling looks unusual; it is read as the repair of leaks that the following sentence explicitly contrasts with recasting. The distinction is between incremental repair and thorough transformation, not between careful preparation and impulsive destruction.

[^j10-ding-cooking]: **亨 as cooking.** In the clauses about the cauldron's use, 亨 is read in the sense of 烹, cooking, rather than 亨, success or free passage. 大亨 there means cooking on an abundant scale, not the prognostication “great success.” 上帝 is rendered “the Lord on High,” preserving the ritual reference without importing a specifically Christian identity. Zhu contrasts sincerity expressed through a calf in this offering with ample ceremonial provision for worthy guests.

[^j10-ding-image]: **Image, vessel, and correctness.** Cheng treats the cauldron as a vessel embodying exemplary order, then reads the hexagram through it. His gloss of 方 as 正 concerns correctness and ordered design; it does not require every physical cauldron to be square. The source also gives priority to the image while using the implement to explain the figure. The translation retains this reciprocal exposition rather than pretending it supplies a simple archaeological chronology of invention.

[^j10-ding-ears]: **Hearing and sight in the figure.** Li supplies the image of the eye. Zhang Qingzi explains the ears through the fifth six as the cauldron's ears, connecting this passage to the line imagery translated in Juan Seven. 聰明 accordingly keeps its concrete sensory force, “perceptive ears and clear eyes,” while the commentaries develop its implications for intelligent government. It is not reduced merely to “cleverness.”

[^j10-ding-attribution]: **Names not expanded beyond the evidence.** The first collected gloss is attributed to 單氏渢, which is preserved in Chinese beside “Master Shan.” The next gives only 劉氏, “Master Liu.” Neither has been silently assigned a fuller identity. The selected parallel likewise gives these forms. The unusual personal graph and the abbreviated surname attribution should be retained for a future systematic study of the compilation's cited authors.

[^j10-zhen-fear]: **Apprehension and panic are different.** 虩虩 describes trembling, watchful apprehension; 啞啞 the ease of subsequent laughter and speech. The commentaries distinguish being startled outwardly from fear affecting the heart more deeply. The final gloss is crucial: vigilance can be a form of strength, and fear can prepare effective action. The translation therefore does not treat every reference to fear as incapacitating panic or every reference to composure as absence of concern.

[^j10-zhen-missing]: **A proposed omitted phrase.** Cheng argues that 不喪匕鬯, “does not lose the sacrificial spoon and fragrant wine,” has dropped out after the clause about those nearby. Zhu follows him. The canonical text is translated as transmitted in the selected base; the proposed words are not inserted without marking. Their full meaning and the argument for restoring them are already present in the translated commentaries. This is a historical textual conjecture, not a newly discovered omission established by the consulted witness.

[^j10-zhen-coming-forth]: **Succession or a ruler's absence?** Zhu reads 出 as the heir's coming forth to succeed and preside over sacrifice, while also reporting a proposal to read 鬯 instead. Cheng understands an existing ruler going out and his son maintaining the temples and state during the absence. Zhang Qingzi invokes “the Lord comes forth in Zhen” and Shun's composure as evidence for succession. “On coming forth” leaves the canonical wording open enough for these different explanations.

[^j10-gen-time]: **Stillness within action.** The commentators distinguish literal immobility from remaining in the principle appropriate to one's conduct. Action at the right time can therefore count as stopping in one's proper place. Cheng's distinction between 理 within things and 義 in dealing with them is preserved as “principle” and “rightness.” This is not a claim that any action becomes right merely because its agent feels inwardly calm.

[^j10-gen-back]: **Back or stopping?** The selected canonical text has 艮其止, whereas the Judgment in the earlier juan has 艮其背. Zhu explains the substitution as meaningful; Chao proposes restoring 背. Xiang reports the older graph 北 and a possible corruption into 止. The translation preserves the canonical difference and the conflicting explanations. “Like counterparts” means matching yin with yin and yang with yang across the trigrams, not an ordinary complementary yin-yang response. This accounts for the phrase “do not join.”

[^j10-gen-readings]: **Attested corrections in Keeping Still.** In Cheng's commentary, the base's 不得共所 is read with the parallel as 不得其所, “not attaining its proper place.” Xiang's citation 良其背 is read as 艮其背, “keeping the back still.” These differ from Chao's proposed change to the canonical 止: the former are corrections supported by the parallel, while the latter is the source's own reported conjecture. 背/止 has therefore not been uniformly normalized throughout.

[^j10-jian-gradual]: **The awkward opening.** 漸之進也 literally gives something like “the advancing of gradualness.” Zhu suspects that 之 is superfluous or represents another 漸. “Advancing by degrees” conveys the received phrase without choosing either conjectural alteration. The difference from 晉, Advance, and 升, Pushing Upward, lies in the manner and order of progress, not simply its speed. The marriage example gives ritual stages as its principal illustration.

[^j10-jian-position]: **Position and right conduct.** Liang distinguishes 進得位, advancing into a fitting place, from 進以正, advancing through correctness. Cheng's references to the fourth advancing and the third becoming uppermost are preserved as relational descriptions of the figure; the third is uppermost in the lower trigram, not the hexagram's sixth place. Both consulted transcriptions contain the compressed wording. It has not been reconstructed into a different line sequence merely to make one derivation more explicit.

[^j10-jian-order]: **Order changes the meaning.** Stillness within followed by outward deference gives gradual advance; inward deference ending in stillness gives Decay's failure to act. Wu expressly warns that the sequence cannot be reversed. The same two virtues, placed differently, do not produce the same interpretation. This reinforces the compilation's attention to inward and outward structure rather than a free association of favorable keywords.

[^j10-guimei-generation]: **The end and beginning of human life.** Zhu reads marriage as the completion of a woman's maidenhood and childbearing as a new human beginning. The introductory praise concerns the necessity of generational union; it does not make every arrangement represented by the figure auspicious. The compilers explicitly use this to balance the subsequent warnings. The base's 歸妹 is retained against the parallel's apparent 婦妹, and its 交感 against that witness's 交惑 in Cheng's opening explanation.

[^j10-guimei-delight]: **Pleasure, initiative, and ritual.** The compilers give 所歸妹 two related senses: the maiden desires to enter the marriage, and the one entering it is specifically a young girl. Their criticism concerns initiative, age, and the subordinating of ritual to desire within the historical marriage system. These social assumptions are translated, not replaced with a modern claim that all self-directed choice or affection is improper.

[^j10-guimei-positions]: **Improper position is not an automatic verdict.** The middle four lines are formally misplaced. Cheng's broader statement that all positions are improper also involves the relational interpretation of the outer lines, whose yin-yang places alone would not yield that conclusion. The compilers explicitly compare other figures with similar placement and show that their meanings differ. Positional rules contribute to a judgment; they do not independently generate every verdict.

[^j10-feng-clarity]: **A cause of abundance, not a synonym.** The compilers distinguish explaining what a name means from explaining what brings that condition about. Clarity expressed in action produces abundance; clarity is not simply another word for abundance. Their attention to 以 rather than 而 is part of this argument. The translation retains the causal force instead of presenting a list of independent, equally weighted virtues.

[^j10-feng-supplement]: **Supplementary editorial paragraph absent from the base.** Between Wu Yueshen's gloss and the passage about noon's decline, the parallel includes this additional judgment: “Esteeming greatness means that, when the king reaches this condition, what he esteems is great. If his aspirations are expansive, he may fail to attend carefully to small things and consider subtle matters. Wherever clarity fails to illuminate is already a sign of obscurity and an omen of decline. Thus being like the sun at noon means that, by remaining continually clear rather than darkened, one can remain continually centered rather than declining.” The passage is translated here only. It is not counted among the base's 610 aligned blocks.

[^j10-feng-waxing]: **Noon, fullness, and limits.** 食 is translated “is eclipsed” in the canonical phrase; Cheng's explanation also speaks more generally of diminution. The passage uses sun and moon to warn against complacent fullness. It should not be read as a precise astronomical assertion that every full moon is eclipsed. Likewise, the aspiration to preserve noon is interpreted morally through vigilance and renewal, not as an attempt to stop celestial motion. Kong's discussion of “lighter” and “weightier” comparisons is kept alongside his different ordering of honor and rank.

[^j10-feng-reading]: **亦 rather than 爾.** The base's final editorial sentence has 爾曰盛極當衰也, with disruptive punctuation. The parallel reads 亦曰盛極當衰也, “likewise says that flourishing at its utmost must decline.” The English follows the latter. The added paragraph translated in the preceding supplementary note is a separate matter: it is missing material in the base, not a replacement for this concluding sentence.

[^j10-lu-center]: **A mean fitted to sojourning.** Cheng explicitly says that the mean is not a single inflexible measure: sojourning has its own mean. Yieldingness must not become excessive, but the firmness prized elsewhere may be ill suited to this condition. The compilers again make stillness the ground to which clarity attaches. This continues their reading of Advance and Opposition rather than introducing a new, unrelated theory of intuition.

[^j10-lu-attribution]: **An unlabelled continuation.** The base places “a six in the fifth place…” in a separate paragraph without another speaker's name. The parallel presents it continuously after Wang Zongchuan's gloss. The translation keeps the base's separate aligned paragraph without inventing an attribution marker. It may be read as the continuation supported by the parallel, but no wording has been silently moved from one source block to another.

[^j10-xun-commands]: **Reiteration is not simply issuing twice.** Zhu rejects a numerical reading of 申命 as two issuances. It is earnest repetition and explanation sufficient to reach the audience. The compilers add inquiry into people's actual circumstances: penetration begins by learning what is hidden, not merely by making commands more insistent. 命 here is a command or injunction, distinct from Heaven's mandate or an individual's allotted life in other contexts.

[^j10-xun-firmness]: **Deference and penetration of yieldingness.** Cheng reads firm virtue deferring to the centered, correct Way. Zhao and He give greater emphasis to yang penetrating yin, which the compilers explicitly endorse. The English does not force every 巽 into either “compliance” or physical “entry.” “The lines that establish the figure” identifies its structural distinction; this is not necessarily the same role as the exemplary ruler or the line through which its work is accomplished.

[^j10-dui-wordless]: **The graph's missing speech component.** The contrast of 兌 with 說 recalls 咸 and 感 at the beginning of the juan. The commentarial point is joy that does not depend upon words, not the complete suppression of speech. These are traditional reflections on written forms and ethical meaning; they are not being presented as conclusions of modern historical linguistics.

[^j10-dui-leading]: **Willing allegiance and a retained variant.** The base's 足以 is rendered “suffices to”; the parallel instead has 是以, “thus.” Both produce intelligible arguments, so the base is retained rather than automatically corrected. The clauses about people forgetting toil and death belong to the source's account of legitimate leadership. Cheng distinguishes this from violating the Way to win transient praise. Their rhetoric is not an empirical guarantee of safety or a general endorsement of using pleasure to manipulate others.

[^j10-huan-structure]: **Root and effective connection.** The compilers distinguish the second's inward firmness as a foundation from the fourth's outward alignment as the means of restoring connection. Lin also reports a tension between the settled wording of the *Original Meaning* and Zhu's later recorded uncertainty. The English preserves that report without reconstructing an unrecorded final revision. These are different witnesses to interpretation, not reasons to suppress one of the voices.

[^j10-huan-center]: **Three readings of the center.** Zhu locates the center in the temple. Cheng relates it to gathering human hearts; He Kai explicitly rejects a merely spatial reading and stresses the ruler's inward centeredness. The compilers return to the fifth line while including sincerity in its significance. The short canonical sentence remains open to these interpretations. The translation does not make the word “center” carry exactly the same reference in every gloss.

[^j10-huan-boat]: **Wood as practical means.** Xun supplies wood and Kan the water to be crossed. The compilers retain this concrete equipment while extending it to preparedness for danger. Hu's “thirteen hexagrams” refers to the series of cultural inventions discussed through hexagram images in the Appended Statements, not thirteen additional figures outside the sixty-four. This passage also supports keeping 木, wood, in Increase's disputed wording.

[^j10-jie-measure]: **Apportionment, not separation into hostile halves.** 剛柔分 concerns the balanced distribution of firmness and yieldingness. Zhao reads mutual assistance, tension and relaxation, decrease and increase, each suited to its use. The base's clear 分 and 柔 are retained against the parallel's apparent 兮 and 乗 in these passages. No claim is made that agreement with the parallel would be preferable to coherent Chinese simply because it bears the Siku label.

[^j10-jie-bitter]: **Sweetness and bitterness.** The argument uses flavors to distinguish fitted restraint from harsh excess. Wu Yinghui relates them to the traditional correspondences of central earth and upward-blazing fire. These correspondences are preserved as his explanation, not converted into modern chemistry. “Must not be persevered in” matters: firmness in maintaining an excessive restriction is not automatically the steadfastness the text elsewhere praises.

[^j10-jie-readings]: **Attribution and an unresolved expression.** The base names 俞氏炎; the parallel has 俞氏琰, supporting the attribution to Yu Yan used here. Both transcriptions have 味之過正. “Excessive intensity of flavor” reads the phrase contextually as flavor exceeding its proper measure, in parallel with overworking the body and overthinking. No different Chinese reading has been established, and the translation does not claim an attested emendation to this expression.

[^j10-jie-joy]: **Why joy matters to limits.** Cheng describes delight being checked by difficulty; the compilers locate joy in restraint's ease and suitability, without oppressive constriction. Both connect this with passage rather than exhaustion. Their difference is retained. The proper position of an authority and its centered, correct virtue also remain distinct: possessing power to impose a limit is not equivalent to imposing the right limit.

[^j10-jie-institutions]: **Measure protects people and resources.** 制度 is rendered “institutions and measures,” retaining its public and regulatory scope. The point is not restraint for its own sake: commands and labor demands must fit their circumstances and times. Wu distinguishes limitation, setting bounds, from revolution, alteration, while treating both as aspects of natural and human ordering.

[^j10-zhongfu-open]: **An open center and solid centers.** The whole figure has two yin in its middle; each constituent trigram has a yang center. These are compatible descriptions at different scales. 虛 therefore signifies openness or freedom from encumbrance, while 實 signifies reliable substance and a governing ground. Neither means merely “empty of feeling” or “physically full.” The brooding-egg analogy provides a further image of an outer structure and inward life.

[^j10-zhongfu-laozi]: **A quotation retained as this book transmits it.** Both consulted Zhezhong transcriptions give the unusual sequence 髣兮髴…真精之中，其中有信. The [Wang Bi text of the Laozi, chapter 21](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/道德經_%28王弼本%29#二十一章), instead has 恍兮惚兮…其精甚真，其中有信. The main translation follows the quotation as presented here rather than silently replacing it with the separate Laozi witness. The surrounding earth, moon, and reproductive imagery likewise remains the compilers' inherited cosmological account, not an independent explanation in modern biological terms.

[^j10-zhongfu-wuwang]: **Complementary openness and substance.** The compilers contrast Without Falsity as Heaven's virtue with Inner Sincerity as Earth's, but make each include both openness and substance. Freedom from contrivance and accumulation of real virtue are complementary, not contradictory. Their argument should not be reduced to an opposition between doing nothing in one hexagram and acquiring things in the other.

[^j10-zhongfu-boat]: **Whose good fortune, and what kind of emptiness?** Wu says the good fortune belongs to the person whose sincerity reaches pigs and fish, not to the animals themselves. The boat is hollow as a vessel; Cheng also reads it as empty of a burden that could make it sink. Both belong to his analogy of inward openness. The translated claim about escaping sinking is his illustrative assurance, not modern boating advice or a prediction that a sincere traveler cannot drown.

[^j10-zhongfu-readings]: **Two corrections in Cheng's commentary.** The base's 通道至矣 is read with the parallel as 信道至矣, “the Way of trust is complete.” 乘木濟用 is read as 乘木濟川, “riding wood across a river.” These corrections restore the explicit themes of trust and river crossing without changing the archived Chinese. Elsewhere the base's 剛得中 remains preferable to the parallel's apparent 剛得申.

[^j10-xiaoguo-excess]: **Excess as a corrective measure.** The commentators repeatedly distinguish exceeding an ordinary measure from exceeding what the actual situation rightly requires. A slight departure may restore the mean when circumstances are already distorted. This is not a claim that excess is intrinsically virtuous. The examples of respect, mourning, and frugality concern the source's ethical and ritual practices, and the qualification “small” remains essential.

[^j10-xiaoguo-position]: **The third and fourth are not identically misplaced.** Zhu refers the combined phrase to both yang lines. Cheng differentiates them: the third lacks centeredness, while the fourth is formally misplaced. Thus the wording should not be used to infer that the third yang occupies an even position. The compilers then read centeredness through fitness for the time, distinguishing firm capacity for great responsibility from careful yieldingness in small matters.

[^j10-xiaoguo-bird]: **A disputed sentence and several kinds of descent.** Cheng suspects that “there is the image of a flying bird” is a gloss incorporated into the Commentary; it remains in the translated canonical text, with his objection alongside. His sound-and-flight analogy differs from Hu's resting place, Yu's wind direction, and Wu's yin-yang placement. None is silently made the sole literal explanation. Their shared concern is a limited, fitting descent rather than unrestrained ascent.

[^j10-xiaoguo-ridge]: **Ridgepole and feathered model.** The comparison joins Great Excess's supporting beams with Small Excess's feathers. 羽儀 carries the image of ceremonial adornment and exemplary bearing. The translation retains that image rather than reducing it simply to fame or leadership. The two figures require different corrections: firmness must not collapse under its burden, while light yieldingness must find restraint and a resting place.

[^j10-jiji-small]: **Small success or success in the small?** The canonical text as selected says “After Completion: success; it is the small that succeeds.” Zhu suspects a missing 小 after 濟, restoring the order “small success.” That conjecture is reported without rewriting the canonical passage. Cheng instead explains that great matters have already succeeded and small ones remain. Lu and the compilers distinguish external institutions from the greater inward work needed to preserve order. The differences continue the dispute translated in [Juan Eight](juan-08.md#eee-4635).

[^j10-jiji-inner]: **“Inner” retained in both clauses.** The compilers say that both After Completion and Before Completion take the inner trigram as primary, then describe the contrary tendency emerging in the outer. Both consulted transcriptions read 內 in the second clause. It has not been silently changed to 外 to harmonize it with Cai Yuan's subsequent statement that Before Completion's governing line lies in the upper trigram. A distinction between the named situation's initial configuration and the location of its auspicious governing line may explain the wording; this remains interpretation, not an attested correction.

[^j10-jiji-not-fatalism]: **An ending is not itself the cause of disorder.** Zhang and Yu explicitly make negligence at the end, not the mere existence of an ending, the cause of disorder. Cheng says that the sage changes course before exhaustion. These qualifications coexist with statements elsewhere about inevitable reversal. The text has not been flattened into either a deterministic prediction of collapse or an assurance that diligence can prevent every misfortune. The differing emphases remain attributed.

[^j10-weiji-fox]: **The little fox and the second line.** Cheng assigns this image to the second nine, although the first six's own oracle statement mentions a wet tail. His discussion addresses the hexagram Judgment from the second's situation; it is not a claim that the first line lacks those words. Zhu reads 汔 as “nearly,” while Cheng's emphasis on eagerness continues his alternative explanation in Juan Eight. The translated canonical phrase follows “almost across,” and the commentarial difference remains explicit.

[^j10-weiji-recluse]: **An acknowledged source.** Cheng attributes the final interpretation to a recluse of Chengdu. No personal name is supplied here, so none is invented. 男 refers to the yang lines in the cited *Miscellaneous Hexagrams* statement, not all men as a social class. The passage combines their lack of formal position with the favorable fact that all firm and yielding counterparts respond, leaving assistance possible despite incompletion.

[^j10-weiji-ending]: **Finishing without abandoning vigilance.** The juan ends by comparing negligence after accomplishment with failure to persevere before accomplishment. It does not conclude with a new oracle verdict. Guo's confidence in obtaining the right people belongs to his moral account of reciprocal assistance, while Wu's unceasing self-strengthening recalls Qian. This closes the second half of the Judgment Commentary; the compilation's separate Image Commentaries and later Wings still follow.
