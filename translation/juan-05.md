## 31. Xian 咸 — Influence

<!-- Source: https://www.eee-learning.com/book/4601 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:001 -->

![Xian: Gen below, Dui above](../assets/eee-learning/a033059a14d2-yi31b.png) **Xian 咸 — Influence** — Gen below; Dui above.[^j05-influence]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:002 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Concerning Xian, the *Sequence of the Hexagrams* says: “Only after Heaven and Earth exist do the myriad things exist; only after the myriad things exist do male and female exist; only after male and female exist do husband and wife exist; only after husband and wife exist do father and son exist; only after father and son exist do ruler and minister exist; only after ruler and minister exist do superior and subordinate exist; only after superior and subordinate exist do ritual and rightness have their place.” Heaven and Earth are the root of the myriad things; husband and wife are the beginning of human relationships. Thus the Upper Classic begins with Qian and Kun, and the Lower Classic with Influence, followed by Constancy. Heaven and Earth are two distinct things, so two hexagrams separately express their Ways. Male and female unite to become husband and wife, so in both Influence and Constancy the two constituent figures combine to express marriage. Xian means to affect and respond, with delight as its governing concern. Heng means enduring constancy, with correctness as its foundation. Yet delight has its own correctness, and correctness certainly contains delight. Deference joined with movement, and firm and yielding lines all corresponding: this is delight. Influence has Dui above and Gen below, the youngest daughter and youngest son. Nowhere is mutual attraction deeper than between the young; therefore the two youngest constitute Influence. Gen's character is substantial and steadfast; its stillness expresses sincerity and trustworthiness. The man's intention is steadfast as he humbles himself to seek union; the woman's heart delights and responds above. The man initiates the attraction: when he first moves her through sincerity, she responds with delight.[^j05-lower-classic]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:002 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:003 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:004 -->

**Qiu Fuguo:** In Influence, the union of the two youngest represents the beginning of marriage. It therefore discusses the feelings of mutual attraction and takes the man placing himself below the woman as its image: by this, the Way of marriage is accomplished. In Constancy, the mutual support of the two eldest represents marriage carried through to its completion. It therefore discusses the Way of household life and takes the man above and the woman below as its image: by the woman's place below the man, the order of married life is established. Decrease also contains the two youngest, but the man is not below the woman, so the meaning of mutual influence is slight. Increase also contains the two eldest, but the woman is above the man, contrary to the meaning of lasting constancy. Is this not why the Lower Classic begins with Influence and Constancy rather than Decrease and Increase?[^j05-marriage-order]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:005 -->

### Influence: success. It is beneficial to be steadfast in what is right. Taking a wife brings good fortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:005 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:006 -->

**Original Meaning.** Xian is mutual influence and response. Dui's yieldingness is above and Gen's firmness below, and they affect and respond to one another. Moreover, Gen's stillness makes the influence single-minded, while Dui's delight makes the response complete. Again, Gen, the youngest son, places himself below Dui, the youngest daughter. The man takes the initiative before the woman, fulfilling their proper relationship at the time of marriage.[^j05-xian-readings] Thus the hexagram is Influence, and its prognostication promises success and benefit in steadfast correctness; taking a wife brings good fortune. Influence naturally tends toward free communication. Without correctness, however, that success is lost, and everything undertaken brings misfortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:006 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:007 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Xian means influence. It is not called *gan*, “to influence,” because *xian* also means “all”: man and woman affect one another. No mutual influence is stronger than that between male and female, and among the young it is stronger still. Ruler and minister, superior and subordinate, and indeed all things, have their Ways of mutual influence. Where things influence one another, free communication becomes possible. If ruler and minister affect one another, their Way opens; if superior and subordinate affect one another, their intentions communicate. So too with father and son, husband and wife, relatives and friends: when feelings and intentions move one another, harmony and free passage follow. The same is true of affairs and things generally. Thus Influence contains a principle of success. “It is beneficial to be steadfast in what is right”: the benefit of mutual influence lies in correctness. Without correctness it enters into evil. Husband and wife drawn together through licentious enticement, ruler and minister through flattering compliance, superior and subordinate through crooked dealings—all are influence without correctness. “Taking a wife brings good fortune” speaks of the hexagram's capacities. Yieldingness above and firmness below, the two energies responding and joining, stillness accompanied by delight, the man placing himself below the woman: to take a wife in accordance with these meanings is correct and auspicious.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:007 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:008 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:008 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:009 -->

**Hu Bingwen:** Xian means influence. Why say *xian* rather than *gan*? Xian means “all”: influence without a calculating mind. Where there is no deliberate calculation of influence, nothing is beyond its reach. Influence necessarily communicates, but its benefit lies in correctness: this describes the Way of influence generally. “Taking a wife brings good fortune” speaks specifically of the manner in which one should marry.[^j05-uncontrived]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:009 -->

### First Six: Influence in the great toe.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:010 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:011 -->

**Original Meaning.** *Mu* is the great toe. Influence takes its images from the human body. Influence at the lowest point gives the image of the great toe. Its reach is still shallow; it wishes to advance but cannot yet do so. Therefore neither good fortune nor misfortune is stated. Although mutual influence governs this hexagram, all six lines favor stillness rather than movement.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:011 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:012 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The first six is lowest in the lower trigram and influences the fourth. Weak and at the beginning, its influence is not deep: how could it move another person? It is like movement in the great toe, insufficient to take a step. The great toe is the largest toe of the foot. Human influence differs in depth and weight. Recognize the time and circumstances, and one's conduct will not depart from what is fitting.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:012 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:013 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:013 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:014 -->

**A question in the Classified Conversations of Master Zhu:** “The inner trigram of Influence is Gen, stillness. Why do its lines all speak of movement?” The reply: “Although Gen is stillness, Influence means mutual affecting and response. All wish to move, and so all are spoken of in terms of movement. Yet although movement is discussed in the hexagram's structure, the slightest movement is already inauspicious.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:014 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:015 -->

**Cai Qing:** “Influence in the great toe” means, in effect, influencing through one's great toe. The other lines follow the same pattern.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:015 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:016 -->

**He also said:** The *Original Meaning* says, “Although mutual influence governs this hexagram, all six lines favor stillness rather than movement.” This is precisely the principle of receiving others with an open heart. The *Great Commentary* says, “Still and unmoving, it responds and thereby penetrates the circumstances of the world.” Master Cheng says, “Vastly open and wholly impartial, it responds fittingly as things arrive.” Master Zhou's “giving primacy to stillness,” Master Zhu's “an empty mirror and a level balance,” and earlier scholars' “influence without a calculating mind” all mean this.[^j05-still-response]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:016 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:017 -->

### Six in the Second Place: Influence in the calf. Misfortune. Remaining still brings good fortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:017 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:018 -->

**Original Meaning.** The calf is the fleshy part of the lower leg. When one wishes to walk, it moves first of itself: an image of restless impulsiveness unable to remain firm. The second line occupies this part of the body and, being yielding yin, cannot firmly preserve itself; hence the image. Yet it possesses centered and correct virtue and can remain in its place. Thus the prognostication is misfortune in movement, good fortune in stillness.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:018 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:019 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The second line is yin below and corresponds with the fifth; hence the warning about influence in the calf. The calf is the fleshy part of the lower leg. In walking, it moves first; the foot is then lifted, unlike the calf's spontaneous movement. If the second does not preserve the Way and await the superior's invitation, but moves like the calf, it loses itself in restless presumption: hence misfortune. To remain peacefully where one is, without moving, and await the superior's invitation accords with the Way of advancing and withdrawing and brings good fortune. The second represents someone centered and correct; the warning arises because it is in Influence and corresponds with the fifth. The further words “remaining still brings good fortune” mean that one should rest within one's proper station rather than initiate movement.[^j05-calf]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:019 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:020 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:020 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:021 -->

**Wang Bi:** The Way of Influence advances, leaving the toe and ascending to the calf. The calf is a restless, moving part of the body. To influence things through restlessness leads to misfortune. Since restlessness causes the misfortune, remaining still brings good fortune. The line does not ride upon firmness, so it can remain where it is and attain good fortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:021 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:022 -->

### Nine in the Third Place: Influence in the thigh. Holding fast to following. To go on brings humiliation.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:022 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:023 -->

**Original Meaning.** The thigh moves after the foot and cannot act independently. “Holding fast” means taking something as one's governing commitment. Both lower lines wish to move; the third likewise cannot preserve itself and follows them. To go on brings humiliation. Hence this image and prognostication.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:023 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:024 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Nine in the third place occupies a firm position with yang and has the capacity of yang firmness. It governs within and stands at the top of the lower trigram. It should establish itself in the correct Way and thereby influence things, yet instead it responds to the top six. Yang wishes to rise and delights in yin; the top occupies the utmost of influence and delight. Thus the third is moved and follows it. The thigh is below the torso and above the foot, unable to act freely, moving as the body moves; hence this image. The third cannot govern itself but follows things and moves, like the thigh. What it holds to is following external things. With the capacity of yang firmness, it is moved by what pleases it and follows along. To proceed in this way is shameful.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:024 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:025 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:025 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:026 -->

**Wang Zongchuan:** The third nine is highest in the lower trigram: this is the thigh. Although it belongs to Gen, yang occupies yang and has a correspondent above, so it cannot remain still. Hence “influence in the thigh.” The thigh moves in following the upper body. Excessively firm in capacity, the line cannot govern within; what it grasps and holds is following the upper body in movement. Thus it moves restlessly and loses correctness: “To go on brings humiliation.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:026 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:027 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** “Holding fast to following”: the *Original Meaning* understands this as following the two lower lines, while Cheng's Commentary understands following above. Yet “following” takes its meaning from proceeding one after another, as geese in flight. It is therefore more fitting to take the third as following the fourth. Following's first firm line follows the second yielding line, and its fifth firm line follows the yielding top: these provide evidence. The fourth is the heart's position. When the heart moves, the body follows; the third occupies the thigh's place, directly adjoining and supporting the fourth. Hence “influence in the thigh; holding fast to following.” Compare Keeping Still, where the third is treated as the heart's position and the second six likewise says, “It cannot rescue what it follows.” Although the heart certainly governs the body, letting the body immediately follow every movement of the heart is not the Way of regulating outward conduct to nourish the inward center. In human affairs, flattering submission and accommodating obedience by ministers and sons are examples. The third is neither centered nor correct in virtue, and so it acts thus.[^j05-thigh-following]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:027 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:028 -->

### Nine in the Fourth Place: Steadfast correctness brings good fortune; regret disappears. Restlessly going and coming, companions follow your thoughts.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:028 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:029 -->

**Original Meaning.** The fourth nine lies above the thigh and below the flesh of the back, and is also central among the three yang lines: the heart's image and the governing line of Influence. In influencing things, the heart should be correct and firm; only then does it accord with principle. Here yang occupies yin, losing correctness and unable to remain firm. The prognostication therefore supplies a warning: if one can be correct and firm, good fortune follows and regret disappears. But restless going and coming, unable to maintain correctness and firmness and burdened by private attachments in influencing others, draws only one's own companions after oneself; it cannot extend further.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:029 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:030 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Influence is human movement, so the images are all drawn from the body. The toe represents slight movement below, the calf movement that takes the initiative, the thigh following. The fourth nine takes no named bodily part but speaks directly of the Way of influence. It does not say “influence in the heart,” because influence itself is the heart's activity. The fourth lies centrally and in the upper trigram, at the heart's place; thus it governs influence and speaks of its Way. Correct steadfastness brings good fortune and removes regret. Influence without correctness brings regret. Moreover, the fourth belongs to the trigram of delight, occupies yin, and corresponds with the first: hence the admonition to correctness. The Way of influence communicates everywhere, but private attachments obstruct this communication and bring regret. The sage affects the hearts of all under Heaven as cold and heat, rain and sunshine do: reaching everywhere and meeting response everywhere. This is simply steadfast correctness. Such correctness means an open center without a self set apart. “Restlessly going and coming, companions follow your thoughts”: when one is correct and single-minded, influence reaches everywhere. But if one goes and comes restlessly, using a private mind to move things, one can move those reached by one's thoughts but not those beyond them. Thus only one's companions follow one's thinking. A privately attached mind, fixed on one corner or one affair—how could it become spacious enough to communicate everywhere? The *Appended Statements* says: “What need has the world of thinking and contriving? The world reaches one destination by different paths, arrives at one result through a hundred deliberations. What need has the world of thinking and contriving?” Through Influence, the Master discusses this Way of communication to its utmost. A private, calculating mind influences only narrowly. The world's principle is one. Paths differ, but their destination is the same; deliberations number a hundred, but their result is one. Things differ in ten thousand ways and affairs undergo ten thousand changes, yet when gathered under the one, none can depart from it. Make one's intention steadfastly correct, and influence communicates throughout the world. Hence “What need has the world of thinking and contriving?” How could private calculation reach everything? “The sun goes and the moon comes; the moon goes and the sun comes. Sun and moon impel one another, and light arises. Cold goes and heat comes; heat goes and cold comes. Cold and heat impel one another, and the year is completed. Going is contraction; coming is extension. Contraction and extension affect one another, and benefit arises.” Going and coming, contraction and extension, explain mutual influence and response. Contraction entails extension, and extension entails contraction: this is response. Sun and moon impel one another to produce light; cold and heat impel one another to complete the year. Effective activity is accomplished in this way; hence “contraction and extension affect one another, and benefit arises.” Influence is movement. Wherever there is influence there must be response. All movement is influence; influence necessarily receives response; response becomes influence anew, and this again receives response. Thus the process never ceases. “The inchworm contracts in order to extend. Dragons and snakes hibernate to preserve themselves. Refining understanding of rightness until one enters the numinous is how one brings it into use. Making one's activity beneficial and one's person secure is how one elevates virtue. Beyond this, none has yet known.” Having stated the principle of contraction and extension, the text explains it through living things. The inchworm contracts before extending: without contraction there is no extension, and extension is followed by contraction. Observe it, and one understands response. Dragons and snakes withdraw to preserve and rest themselves, and can afterward rise into vigorous activity. Without dormancy they could not emerge. Movement and rest affect one another: contraction and extension. The noble person immerses the mind in subtle principles of rightness, entering their numinous intricacy, in order to bring them into use. This inward concentration is accumulation; bringing it into use is expenditure. Accumulation and expenditure are contraction and extension. “Making one's activity beneficial and one's person secure is how one elevates virtue” continues the preceding account of bringing understanding into use. Beneficial action and security of person enlarge one's virtue and achievement. When action accords with principle, affairs are correct and the person secure. What the sage can accomplish is exhausted here. Hence “Beyond this, none has yet known.” The passage concludes: “To fathom the numinous and understand transformation is the fullness of virtue.” After saying “Beyond this, none has yet known,” it ends by speaking of thoroughly fathoming numinous subtlety and knowing the Way of transformation and generation: virtue at its fullest, beyond which nothing can be added.[^j05-heart-and-thought]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:030 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:031 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:031 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:032 -->

**Master Cheng:** Heaven and Earth's constancy consists in their heart extending to all things without a private heart. The sage's constancy consists in feelings answering every affair without private feeling. Thus, in the noble person's learning, nothing surpasses being vastly open and wholly impartial, responding fittingly as things arrive. Hence: “Steadfast correctness brings good fortune; regret disappears. Restlessly going and coming, companions follow your thoughts.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:032 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:033 -->

**Yang Shi:** The fourth nine lies below the flesh of the back and above the thigh: the heart's position. Yet the heart is not named, because it encompasses everything and cannot be confined to a position.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:033 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:034 -->

**A question in the Classified Conversations of Master Zhu:** “The Commentary's account of an open heart and steadfast single-mindedness at Influence's fourth nine seems entirely like reverent attentiveness.” The reply: “Indeed, I have said that reverent attentiveness is the heart's steadfast correctness.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:034 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:035 -->

**Another question:** “In ‘restlessly going and coming, companions follow your thoughts,’ is one side influencing and the other responding, with ‘restlessly’ adding a deliberate intention?” The reply: “Going and coming are indeed influence and response. ‘Restlessly’ means that while one thought wishes to move the other person, another wishes that person to respond. It is like setting right one's conduct while already wishing to calculate its profit, or illuminating the Way while already wishing to reckon its achievement. Or when a child is about to fall into a well, the heart is alarmed and wishes to save it, but also wants its parents to praise one's goodness. That is the fault of restless calculation.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:035 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:036 -->

**He also said:** “‘Restlessly going and coming, companions follow your thoughts’: the sages have never failed to teach people to think. It is only restless calculation that is forbidden, for that is already private. Influence and response have a natural principle of their own: why contrive them? But as for rightness and principle, one must certainly reflect on them.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:036 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:037 -->

**A question:** “Does ‘going and coming’ mean restless movement inside the heart, as though thoughts went to and fro within one's breast?” The reply: “No.” Another question: “Then is it restlessness in the interval between going and coming?” The reply: “That is not it either. It simply contrasts with ‘the sun goes and the moon comes.’ There, going and coming occur naturally. Here, ‘restlessly’ describes their distortion by added private intention. It is merely adding a hurried, pressing mind that cannot follow natural principle. Just as something goes, one already wants it to come; just as it comes, one already wants it to go. It is nothing but hurry.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:037 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:038 -->

**A question:** “Restless going and coming is like a hegemon influencing others from private motives and immediately wanting their response. Natural going and coming is like a true king: I influence without calculation, and others respond without calculation, universally and impartially, without private attachments.” The reply: “That is indeed so.” Another question: “Does the initiator influence privately while the others respond without private motives, but only in limited numbers?” The reply: “Their response is private too. Suppose I bestow favors privately. Those who receive my favors will soon regard me as their benefactor; those who receive none will not.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:038 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:039 -->

**Hu Bingwen:** Stillness without movement is the heart's underlying constitution; responding and thereby communicating is its activity. Restless going and coming has already lost that still constitution. How could it respond and penetrate all the world's circumstances? “Steadfast correctness brings good fortune; regret disappears” is influence without calculation. What contriving is needed? Restless going and coming is private.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:039 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:040 -->

**Lin Xiyuan:** Look back from restless going and coming to the fourth nine's steadfastness: it simply leaves going and coming free of calculation. One fulfills the Way of influencing others, without calculating whether they respond. This is correctness firmly maintained. Restless going and coming places the whole exchange upon one's mind and clings to it anxiously. “What need of thinking and contriving?” means that this is unnecessary.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:040 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:041 -->

**He also said:** Steadfastness means exercising one's influence without demanding another's response. Precisely because one does not demand a response, one's influence is not private. One influences those who respond and those who do not; no one is excluded from influence, and no one from response. Hence good fortune and the disappearance of regret. Restless going and coming means influencing others while insisting that they respond. This insistence makes one's influence private: one influences those who respond, but not those who do not. On their side, only those influenced respond; those not influenced do not. Thus “companions follow your thoughts.” Restless going and coming is contrivance. “Companions” are those whom one's thoughts reach; because one's thoughts reach them, they are called companions—as one might say a circle or faction.[^j05-companions]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:041 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:042 -->

### Nine in the Fifth Place: Influence in the flesh of the back. No regret.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:042 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:043 -->

**Original Meaning.** *Mei* is the flesh of the back, above and behind the heart. It cannot influence things, but has no private attachments. The fifth nine occupies just this position, hence the image. The warning to the diviner is that acting thus, although unable to influence things, can nevertheless avoid regret.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:043 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:044 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The fifth nine occupies the honored place and should influence the world through perfect sincerity. Yet it corresponds with the second and adjoins the top. If attached to the second and pleased by the top, it becomes partial, shallow, and narrow—not the Way of a ruler. How could it then move the world? *Mei* is the flesh of the back, behind the heart and unseen by it. If one can turn one's back on the private heart and influence more than what one happens to see and find pleasing, one attains the correct Way for a ruler to influence the world, without regret.[^j05-back]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:044 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:045 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:045 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:046 -->

**Kong Yingda:** Ma Rong says that mei is the back; Zheng Kangcheng says it is the flesh along the spine; Wang Su says it is on the back on either side of the spine. The explanations differ, but generally all locate it above the heart.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:046 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:047 -->

**Wang Zongchuan:** The top six stands at Influence's end and uses speech to seek favor and give pleasure. The fifth may associate with and delight in it because it is nearby. Mei is the flesh of the back, turned away from the heart. The warning is to turn away from the heart's private inclination, thereby avoiding the regret of excessive familiarity.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:047 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:048 -->

### Top Six: Influence in the jaw, cheeks, and tongue.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:048 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:049 -->

**Original Meaning.** The jaw, cheeks, and tongue all serve speech and lie at the body's top. The top six is yin at the end of delight and the extreme of Influence, influencing people through words without substance. Moreover, Dui represents mouth and tongue. Hence this image; misfortune and blame are evident without being stated.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:049 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:050 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The top is yielding yin, belongs to Dui, and governs delight. It also occupies the extreme of Influence, so its wish to affect things is extreme. Unable to influence through perfect sincerity, it expresses itself through mouth and tongue—the habitual behavior of petty people and women. How could this truly move anyone?[^j05-mouth] Instead of simply “mouth,” it says “jaw, cheeks, and tongue,” just as people today call faults of speech “lips and mouth” or “cheeks and tongue.” All these parts are instruments of speech.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:050 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:051 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:051 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:052 -->

**Wang Bi:** Jaw, cheeks, and tongue are the instruments of speech. To influence through these is to indulge in glib talk. Even restless going and coming falls short of breadth and illumination: how much more mere chatter! Its shallowness is clear.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:052 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:053 -->

**Guo Zhongxiao:** The *Changes* speaks of taking images close at hand from the body; only Influence and Keeping Still develop these images in such detail. Yet their conclusions differ markedly. Is this not because Influence reaches its extreme in delight and speech, while Keeping Still ends in stillness? Consider “keeping the jaw still; words have order,” and the difference becomes clear.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:053 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:054 -->

**General Discussion.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:054 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4601:055 -->

**Zheng Ruxie:** The hexagram speaks of mutual influence and response. Why do none of its six lines promise unqualified good fortune? The hexagram treats the whole; the lines distinguish its parts. Heaven and Earth respond, and all things are transformed and born; the sage moves human hearts, and peace and harmony prevail. This is Influence in its fullness. The six lines differ in what they influence: Influence in partial forms. From first to top, each takes the body as its image. Confined within a separate self, how could one influence everything?

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4601:055 -->

The first influences the great toe; the second, the calf; the third, the thigh; the fifth, the flesh of the back; the top, jaw, cheeks, and tongue. All concern particular parts. Their influence issues from the heart, so each is explicitly called influence. The fourth nine lies between upper and lower, at the heart's position; therefore it does not say “influence,” but describes the Way through which influence operates.

The fourth, lowest above, is the heart; the fifth, central above, the flesh of the back; the top, highest above, the mouth. This is the order of the upper three lines.

**Gong Huan:** Influence takes its images from the body, like Keeping Still. But Influence affects, while Keeping Still stops: affecting involves movement, stopping involves stillness. Thus Influence's lines do not equal Keeping Still's preponderance of good fortune over misfortune.

## 32. Heng 恒 — Constancy

<!-- Source: https://www.eee-learning.com/book/4604 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4604:001 -->

![Heng: Xun below, Zhen above](../assets/eee-learning/6cf5403128e9-yi32b.png) **Heng 恒 — Constancy** — Xun below; Zhen above.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4604:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4604:002 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Concerning Heng, the *Sequence of the Hexagrams* says: “The Way of husband and wife must endure; therefore Influence is followed by Constancy. Heng means enduring.” Influence is the Way of marriage, and husband and wife remain unchanged throughout life; thus Constancy follows Influence. In Influence the youngest son is below the youngest daughter: the man places himself below the woman, expressing their mutual attraction. In Constancy the eldest son is above the eldest daughter: the man above and woman below express the enduring Way of married household life. In speaking of attraction, the young are closest to one another; in speaking of the order of higher and lower, the mature should be scrupulously correct. Thus Dui and Gen make Influence, while Zhen and Xun make Constancy. The man above the woman, the man active outside and the woman compliant within: this is the customary order of human relationships, hence Constancy. Firmness above and yieldingness below, thunder and wind accompanying one another, deference joined with movement, firm and yielding corresponding—all express constancy.[^j05-marriage-order]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4604:002 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4604:003 -->

### Constancy: success. No blame. It is beneficial to be steadfast in what is right. It is beneficial to have somewhere to go.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4604:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4604:004 -->

**Original Meaning.** Heng means enduring constancy. In the hexagram, Zhen's firmness is above and Xun's yieldingness below; Zhen is thunder and Xun wind, and the two accompany one another; Xun is deferential compliance and Zhen movement, thus movement with deference; and among the six lines of the two trigrams, yin and yang correspond. These four features all express constant principles: hence Constancy. Its prognostication is that enduring in one's Way brings success without blame. Yet one must also preserve correctness to attain a Way genuinely capable of lasting. Then it is beneficial to have somewhere to go.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4604:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4604:005 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Constancy is lasting endurance. Its Way can bring free passage; only constancy that brings free passage is without blame. What persists without opening a way is not a Way fit to endure, and carries blame. The noble person's persistence in goodness is a Way fit to endure; the petty person's persistence in evil departs from it. Constancy brings success through steadfast correctness: hence the benefit in being correct. What is called constancy is a Way capable of enduring, not clinging to one corner without understanding change. Thus it is beneficial to go forward. Precisely because there is onward movement, constancy is possible: something fixed once and for all cannot endure. Moreover, where could an enduring Way go without benefit?[^j05-constancy]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4604:005 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4604:006 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4604:006 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4604:007 -->

**Classified Conversations of Master Zhu:** Heng is one continuous thing, running from beginning to end; it does not simply mean ordinary or usual. Its ancient form is 𢛢. It is said to depict a boat with its two ends touching the banks: this shows continuity from beginning to end.[^j05-heng-graph]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4604:007 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4604:008 -->

**Xu Ji:** Constancy has two meanings: constancy that does not change, and constancy that does not cease. “It is beneficial to be steadfast in what is right” is unchanging constancy; “it is beneficial to have somewhere to go” is unceasing constancy. Together they constitute the enduring Way. Lean toward either alone, and it is no longer the Way.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4604:008 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4604:009 -->

**Lin Xiyuan:** Precisely because it does not change, it does not cease.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4604:009 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4604:010 -->

### First Six: Demanding depth in constancy. Persistence brings misfortune. Nothing is beneficial.[^j05-depth]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4604:010 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4604:011 -->

**Original Meaning.** The first and fourth are proper correspondents: this is the usual principle. Yet the first is below and at the beginning; it cannot yet demand a deep response. The fourth belongs to Zhen and has yang's nature, rising rather than descending, and the second and third separate them. Its intention to respond to the first therefore differs from the usual pattern. Yielding and uncomprehending, the first cannot assess these circumstances. Moreover, yin below in Xun governs that trigram, whose nature is to enter deeply. It therefore invokes the usual principle to demand depth: the image of demanding depth in constancy. For a diviner acting thus, even correctness brings misfortune, and nothing is beneficial.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4604:011 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4604:012 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The first is below, and the fourth is its proper correspondent. A yielding, uncomprehending person can preserve a fixed rule but cannot assess circumstances. The fourth belongs to Zhen and is yang; firm and high, its intention is upward, not downward. The second and third also intervene, so its intention toward the first differs from the usual pattern. Yet the first expects a deep response: it knows constancy but not change. *Jun* means to deepen; “demanding depth in constancy” means pressing constancy too deeply. To preserve the usual rule without assessing circumstances, make deep demands upon those above, and hold firmly to this course leads to misfortune. Such inflexible attachment brings no benefit anywhere. People who demand too much on the strength of old acquaintance and thereby incur regret and blame exemplify this. Moreover, a mind bent on demanding much from above cannot remain content in its own place. Weakness and littleness unable to remain settled also lead to misfortune. In every hexagram, beginning and end are the places of shallowness and depth, slightness and fullness. To demand depth while still below is likewise to misunderstand the time.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4604:012 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4604:013 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4604:013 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4604:014 -->

**Lu Xisheng:** Constancy values enduring in one's Way, becoming gradually deeper each day. The first is constancy's beginning and should make gradual progress its constant practice. But with Xun's character and an impatient nature, it suddenly seeks deep penetration. This loses the meaning of enduring in one's Way and cannot serve as constancy. Hence persistence brings misfortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4604:014 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4604:015 -->

**Hu Yuan:** Everything under Heaven must develop gradually; only accumulated days and sustained duration bring achievement. Long study can establish one's work in the Way and bring one to sagehood and worthiness. Long governance can establish transformation through teaching and bring one toward Yao and Shun. All such accomplishments come through accumulated time, never suddenly. The first six occupies the beginning of the lower trigram, the start of an undertaking, yet demands a lasting Way and enduring results. It is like beginning study and immediately wanting to equal the Duke of Zhou and Confucius, or beginning to govern and wanting at once to transform people like Yao and Shun. Unable to accumulate long effort, it seeks the depth of a constant Way. Thus even in a correct course it encounters misfortune. “Nothing is beneficial”: proceeding thus must be fruitless. As Confucius said, “Desiring speed, one does not arrive.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4604:015 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4604:016 -->

**Wang Zongchuan:** The first governs Xun. At the beginning of Constancy, it makes deep penetration its constancy: hence “demanding depth.” It is like undertaking an affair and demanding completion before putting in a day's effort, or beginning study and demanding attainment of the Way before a day's work. Wanting an undertaking to succeed and study to reach the Way is certainly fitting. But expectations that are too deep and demands that are too hasty both end in failure. Hence misfortune, with nothing beneficial.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4604:016 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4604:017 -->

**Wang Shenzi:** Constancy means duration. How could anything lasting be accomplished in a single morning or evening? The first six is yielding in constitution but firm in intention. Its yielding constitution obscures its foresight; its firm intention makes it seek speed and fail to arrive. At Constancy's beginning, it is only starting to plan for endurance, yet suddenly demands depth. Hence “demanding depth in constancy.” If not violently impatient and unable to endure, it will become slipshod and unfit to endure. To cling to this as constancy is to invite misfortune. What benefit could there be?

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4604:017 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4604:018 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Lu, Hu, and the two Wangs all differ here from Cheng's Commentary and the *Original Meaning*. Their accounts are especially precise in relation to the hexagram and should be followed. In any affair, gradual progress permits endurance; without gradual progress, endurance is impossible. As Mencius says, “Those whose advance is headlong retreat swiftly.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4604:018 -->

### Nine in the Second Place: Regret disappears.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4604:019 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4604:020 -->

**Original Meaning.** Yang in a yin position should originally entail regret. But because it endures in the center, that regret disappears.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4604:020 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4604:021 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** In Constancy, occupying one's correct position is the enduring Way. A yang nine in a yin position does not follow the ordinary principle. Occupying what is not its proper constant place should bring regret. Yet the second nine, with centered virtue, responds to the fifth, which is also central. Center responds to center; both its abiding and its movement attain the mean. It can therefore remain long in the center. Enduring in the center does not lose correctness. Centeredness weighs more than positional correctness: centeredness is correct, whereas correctness need not be centered.[^j05-centered-correct] The second nine's firm-centered virtue responds to centeredness. Its superior virtue suffices to remove regret. Only those who can recognize the relative weight of circumstances can discuss the *Changes*.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4604:021 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4604:022 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4604:022 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4604:023 -->

**Cheng Jiong:** Great Strength's second nine, Release's first six, and this line all leave their reasons unstated: the lines themselves make them clear.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4604:023 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4604:024 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Constancy means what endures. What is centered endures. Only this line in the hexagram is firm and central. Great Strength's strength is warned against excess, and only the second of its four yang lines is central. Release favors the southwest and values remaining behind; only its first six is furthest behind. These points accord so clearly with their hexagrams that favorable prognostications can be attached directly and the wording kept brief.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4604:026 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The third is a yang line in a yang position, properly placed in its enduring station. Yet its intention follows the top six. Not only do yin and yang correspond: wind also follows thunder. It does not remain in its constant place and so represents an inconstant person. If virtue is inconstant, humiliation may follow. “May be offered” means that it comes from time to time. To hold firmly to inconstancy as one's constancy—is that not shameful?

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4604:028 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Yang in a yin position is not properly placed. If one occupies the wrong place, what benefit is there in constancy? ## 33. Dun 遯 — Retreat

<!-- Source: https://www.eee-learning.com/book/4605 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:001 -->

![Dun: Gen below, Qian above](../assets/eee-learning/8cf134aef711-yi33b.png) **Dun 遯 — Retreat** — Gen below; Qian above.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:002 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Concerning Dun, the *Sequence of the Hexagrams* says: “Constancy means duration. Things cannot remain forever in their place; therefore Constancy is followed by Retreat. Retreat means withdrawal.” Long remaining entails departure: the two require one another. Hence Retreat follows Constancy. Dun is withdrawing, avoiding, departing. The hexagram is a mountain beneath Heaven. Heaven is above, and yang's nature moves upward. A mountain rises high, yet despite its rising form, its character is stillness. It has the image of pressing upward but stops without advancing. Heaven advances upward and leaves it. The lower presses up while the upper departs: they separate in retreat. Two yin arise below; yin grows toward strength, while yang diminishes and withdraws. Petty people gradually gain ground, and the noble person withdraws to avoid them: hence Retreat.[^j05-dun-readings]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:002 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:003 -->

### Retreat: success. A little benefit in steadfast correctness.[^j05-small-benefit]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:004 -->

**Original Meaning.** Dun is withdrawal and avoidance. Two yin gradually grow, and yang should withdraw; hence Retreat, the hexagram of the sixth month. Although yang should retreat, the fifth nine is properly placed and has the second six responding below, as though something could still be accomplished. Yet the growing two yin below make retreat unavoidable. Thus the prognostication is that a noble person able to withdraw achieves success in the Way, although personally retiring. For petty people, the benefit lies in preserving correctness: they must not take advantage of gradual growth to encroach upon yang. “Small” means yielding yin and petty people. This prognostication resembles those of Obstruction's first two lines.[^j05-months]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:005 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Retreat is the time when yin grows and yang diminishes, and the noble person withdraws into concealment. The noble person retires to give scope to the Way. If the Way is not bent, this is success: hence Retreat's success. In practical affairs too, withdrawal can lead to success. When the petty person's Way grows, recognizing the beginnings and withdrawing is certainly good. But affairs differ, and advancing or diminishing with the time need not follow one fixed pattern. Yielding yin is growing but not yet very strong; the noble person may still remain a while and exert effort. Great rectification is not possible, but limited rectification remains beneficial.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:005 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:006 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:006 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:007 -->

**A question in Master Zhu's Discussions of the Changes:** “In Retreat's ‘small benefit in steadfast correctness,’ the *Original Meaning* takes ‘small’ to mean petty people. But the Classic has no other example of this use: in ‘a little benefit in having somewhere to go,’ ‘small steadfastness brings good fortune,’ and similar phrases, small merely contrasts with great.” The reply: “The Classic indeed has no such example. Inferred from the *Commentary on the Judgments*, however, it refers to petty people. For now, we should follow the Classic while preserving the Commentary's interpretation.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:007 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:008 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Cheng's Commentary and the *Original Meaning* explain “small benefit in steadfast correctness” differently. Judged by the *Changes*' customary usage, Cheng's account seems preferable. When three yin make Obstruction, the text says directly, “Not beneficial to the noble person's steadfastness.” Retreat has not yet reached Obstruction. One should handle the situation through modest withdrawal, without going so far as to provoke its full development. Hence the limited benefit in steadfast correctness.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:008 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:009 -->

### First Six: At the tail of retreat: danger. Do not undertake to go anywhere.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:009 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:010 -->

**Original Meaning.** Retreating behind the others gives the image of the tail and a dangerous course. The diviner must not go forward: remaining obscure and quietly awaiting events is the only way to avoid disaster.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:010 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:011 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Other hexagrams take the lowest place as the beginning. But Retreat is departure, and those in front advance first; thus the first becomes the tail. The tail is behind. To be behind in retreat is to fail to keep up, hence danger. Yielding and insignificant, the first is already behind and must not go: going brings danger. The insignificant can easily remain hidden. Since going is dangerous, it is better not to go and so avoid disaster.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:011 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:012 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:012 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:013 -->

**Lu Ji:** Yin's energy has already reached the second, leaving the first behind: hence the tail of retreat. In avoiding danger one should be ahead, but here one is behind; hence danger. Going would meet disaster, so one must not undertake to go anywhere.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:013 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:014 -->

**Kong Yingda:** “At the tail of retreat: danger” means being the very last to withdraw. Petty people grow within, and one should go outside to avoid them. Yet this line is deepest inside the hexagram and therefore last in retreat. Hence the tail and danger. Once danger has arrived, one's conduct should remain upright while one's speech is modest; one should undertake no further advance.[^j05-tail]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:014 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:015 -->

**A question in the Classified Conversations of Master Zhu:** “‘At the tail of retreat: danger. Do not undertake to go anywhere’ is explained as not going forward, but remaining obscure and quietly waiting. How should this be understood?” The reply: “Cheng's Commentary takes not going to mean not departing: retreat is already late, so departure would be dangerous, and not departing would avoid disaster. I do not think this is right. Being behind in retreat is the tail. Once one is already in danger, how could one still refuse to leave? Read as a prognostication, the distinction is particularly clear.”[^j05-tail]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:015 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:016 -->

**Wang Shenzi:** Retreat means departing; thus the first is behind. Those ahead see the beginnings and withdraw early. Yielding and indecisive, still and unable to act, the first withdraws late: the image of danger. Yet although behind, it occupies a humble station. Not advancing is itself retreat; further forward movement would only increase the danger.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:016 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:017 -->

**Yang Qixin:** In the hexagram as a whole, the two yin represent petty people. In the individual lines, all represent noble people withdrawing. They are all lines of Retreat and therefore develop the meaning of retreat.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:017 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:018 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** The *Changes* generally takes the first line as being ahead. Why does Retreat alone take it as behind? Because the interpretation changes with the hexagram. The first is earliest in sequence but innermost in position. Retreat means going far outward, so the outer trigram is favorable. Is not the innermost position necessarily behind? Someone asks: “Darkening of the Light's first nine is also within. Why is it considered to perceive the beginnings early?” There, the upper trigram is taken as the interior and the top six as the governing line. Thus the fourth six enters the left belly, and the fifth six encounters danger within. The first is consequently furthest away—the exact reverse of Retreat's arrangement.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:018 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:019 -->

### Six in the Second Place: Bind it with the hide of a yellow ox; no one can loosen it.[^j05-oxhide]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:019 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:020 -->

**Original Meaning.** Preserving oneself through centered compliance, beyond anyone's power to undo: this is resolve to retreat. The diviner should likewise hold firm.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:020 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:021 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The second and fifth are proper correspondents. Even at a time of separation and retreat, the second responds compliantly to the fifth with centered correctness, and the fifth draws close to the second with the same qualities. Their bond is naturally firm. Yellow is the central color; the ox is compliant; hide is strong and durable. The second and fifth associate through centered correctness and a compliant Way, as securely as though bound with oxhide. “No one can loosen it” means that the firmness of their bond is beyond description. Because this is a time of Retreat, it is expressed emphatically.[^j05-oxhide-reading]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:021 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:022 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:022 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:023 -->

**Wu Qi:** The second six occupies a minister's position and bears responsibility for the state. It ought not withdraw. That is why this line does not say “retreat.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:023 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:024 -->

**Gong Huan:** Five lines speak of retreat; only the second six does not. Responding upward to the fifth, it is firmly bound even at a time of retreat and cannot withdraw. Hence the image of binding with yellow oxhide. To say it has an inflexible intention to withdraw does not seem necessarily correct.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:024 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:025 -->

**Cai Qing:** How does hidden withdrawal manifest centered compliance? One gathers one's virtue inward without displaying it outwardly, avoids provocative words and heated arguments, and does not flaunt unbending integrity. One simply preserves oneself. This is centered compliance.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:025 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:026 -->

**Supplement — Kong Yingda:** Central and within, this is not someone withdrawing. If not the one withdrawing, it is the one from whom others withdraw. Everyone abandons it and leaves. How can it hold them firmly and make them stay? Only the Way of centered harmony and generous compliance can establish and settle them. Employ this Way, and none can overcome the bond, free themselves, and depart.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:026 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:027 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Cheng's Commentary and the *Original Meaning* differ here too. Wu and Gong develop Cheng's account: the second six properly corresponds to the fifth, like an intimate minister whose duty forbids departure—as in Jizi's words, “I do not consider fleeing away.” Cai develops the *Original Meaning*: withdrawal through centered compliance, like upright conduct joined with modest speech, also accords with being dignified without hatred. Kong offers a separate account: holding worthy people so that they do not depart. “Hold” is like the *Odes*' “holding me in friendly affection,” fitting the tone of the Classic's “bind it.” Accordingly, all these interpretations are retained.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:027 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:028 -->

### Nine in the Third Place: Entangled retreat. Illness and danger. Keeping male and female dependents brings good fortune.[^j05-dependents]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:028 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:029 -->

**Original Meaning.** Adjoining the two yin below gives the image of being bound when one should withdraw: a course of illness and danger. Yet keeping male and female dependents is auspicious. In the noble person's dealings with lesser people, only such household dependents may be maintained without requiring worthiness. Hence this prognostication.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:029 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:030 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Yang's intention delights in yin. The third is close to the second and attached to it. Retreat values speed and distance; with entangling attachments, how could one withdraw quickly and far? The obstruction to retreat is illness; slowness in retreat brings danger. Male and female dependents—petty people and women—cherish favors without understanding rightness. Treat them affectionately, and they are loyal to their superior. The private favor of affectionate attachment wins their hearts; applied to keeping dependents, it is auspicious. Yet this is not how a noble person should generally deal with petty people. The third and second are not proper correspondents; intimacy based on proximity is not the Way of dealing with noble people. Where attachment is correct, however, it cannot be called illness. The First Ruler of Shu's inability to abandon his officers and people is an example: dangerous, but without blame.[^j05-liu-bei]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:030 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:031 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:031 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:032 -->

**Kong Yingda:** The third nine has no correspondent above and adjoins the second. In a time of retreat, its intention is attached: hence entangled retreat. Retreat calls for distancing oneself from petty people. Attachment to yin produces illness and exhaustion, bringing danger. Drawing close to those nearby and remaining attached below may be applied to maintaining household dependents, but brings misfortune in great affairs. Hence the limited promise of good fortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:032 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:033 -->

**Hu Yuan:** Retreat requires going far away. The third nine is at the top of the inner trigram, close to the yielding second six, and cannot free itself for distant retreat: this is illness and danger. Although unable to retreat far, it can attain good fortune by managing the lesser people as household dependents. Such dependents are lowly: keep them distant when distance is appropriate and near when nearness is appropriate. This can bring good fortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:033 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:034 -->

**Su Jun:** “Keeping male and female dependents brings good fortune” shows how to deal with petty people, making clear that one must not become entangled. Petty people easily become familiar, as household dependents can easily beguile. The way to manage them is simply dignity without hatred: dignity blocks the wrong of presumptuous familiarity, while freedom from hatred softens their resentful temper. Treat them according to this rule, and one may escape exhaustion and attain good fortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:034 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:035 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Confucius says, “Women and petty people are difficult to maintain: draw them near and they become impertinent; keep them distant and they resent it.” Between excessive distance and excessive nearness, is there not precisely the meaning of dignity without hatred? Thus, at a time of Retreat, one still held by attachments and unable to depart may deal with petty people as with household dependents. Hu and Su explain this clearly.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:035 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:036 -->

### Nine in the Fourth Place: Retreat amid affection. For the noble person, good fortune; for the petty person, not so.[^j05-affectionate-retreat]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:036 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:037 -->

**Original Meaning.** It responds below to the first six, yet belongs to vigorous Qian: the image of having attachments but being able to sever them and withdraw. Only a noble person capable of self-mastery can do this; a petty person cannot. Thus the noble consulter meets good fortune, the petty consulter does not.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:037 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:038 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The fourth and first properly correspond: the first is someone loved. Although the noble person has affections, when rightness calls for retreat, departure is unhesitating. This is mastering oneself and returning to ritual, governing desire through the Way: hence good fortune. The petty person cannot act through rightness, but clings to loved things and private attachments, even to the point of personal disgrace. Thus for the petty person it is not good. The fourth belongs to Qian and can decide firmly, but since it occupies yin and has an attachment, the sage warns against the petty person's conduct, lest it lose correctness.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:038 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:039 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:039 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:040 -->

**Master Zhang:** Responding to yin, yet dignified without hatred: hence affectionate retreat. The petty person is blind to the beginnings of affairs. If anger does not create enmity, immersion in private affection causes trouble.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:040 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:041 -->

**Zhu Zhen:** Affection is what feeling loves. The noble person decides firmly through rightness, leaves the beloved, and departs: hence good fortune. “Not so” means inability to do this. The line responds to the first six, occupying yin and possessing an attachment. The warning about petty people strengthens the noble person's resolve.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:041 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:042 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Affection is the opposite of hatred. Affectionate retreat means absence of hatred: withdrawing calmly without resentful behavior. Mencius says, “Would I be like that little man, with his anger plainly displayed upon his face?” This is exactly the meaning. “For the petty person, not so” refers to the little man in Mencius's account.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:042 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:043 -->

**Further Editorial Judgment.** In “for the noble person, good fortune; for the petty person, not so,” suppose petty people are taken as the noble person's opponents. Then *pi* may be understood as Obstruction, the opposite of Peace. Graceful retreat preserves the Way's success while the person withdraws: certainly auspicious for the noble person, but how could it benefit the petty person? Since antiquity, when noble people withdraw, petty people have quickly fallen into ruin themselves. Retreat is not the noble person's misfortune but good fortune; causing that retreat is not the petty person's peace but obstruction. This is precisely the meaning of Splitting Apart's top line, “the petty person strips away the shelter.” Although the *Changes* does not plot on behalf of petty people, it never ceases to warn them. The *Original Meaning*'s reading of “small benefit in steadfast correctness” as a warning to petty people seems compatible with this.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:043 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:044 -->

### Nine in the Fifth Place: Admirable retreat. Steadfast correctness brings good fortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:044 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:045 -->

**Original Meaning.** Firm yang, centered and correct, responds below to the second six, likewise yielding, compliant, centered, and correct. This is admirable retreat. A diviner acting thus, with correctness, meets good fortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:045 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:046 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The fifth nine is centered and correct: admirable in retreat. Abiding in the centered and correct Way, stopping or moving as the time requires, is what makes it admirable. Hence correctness and good fortune. The fifth does have an attachment and correspondent, yet it and the second both maintain centered correctness. Their intentions, movements, and stillness are all centered and correct, without the fault of private attachment: hence admirable. The Judgment speaks generally of Retreat's time, so “moving with the time” and “a little benefit in steadfast correctness” retain the possibility of remedying the situation that calls for retreat. By the fifth line, retreat is approaching its extreme, and only centered correctness in retreat is discussed. Retreat is not ordinarily a ruler's business, so the ruler's position is not the focus. Yet a ruler too has things to avoid and keep distant; here also, the issue is centered correctness.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:046 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:047 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:047 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:048 -->

**Gong Huan:** “Admirable retreat; steadfast correctness brings good fortune” is what the *Commentary on the Judgments* calls success through retreat. The fifth is correctly placed and has a correspondent, moving together with the time.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:048 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:049 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Although the ruler's position is not the focus, an honored place can also represent a high minister. Withdrawing when one's work is accomplished is the minister's Way. Thus Yi Yin says, “A minister must not remain upon accomplished work for favor and profit.” Is this not admirable retreat? Its excellence goes beyond the fourth line's affectionate retreat.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:049 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:050 -->

### Top Nine: Spacious retreat. Nothing is unfavorable.[^j05-spacious]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:050 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:051 -->

**Original Meaning.** Firm yang at the hexagram's outermost point, without a binding correspondent below: distant retreat and an ample dwelling in it. Hence this image and prognostication. “Spacious” means ease, abundance, and contentment in oneself.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:051 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:052 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Spaciousness means fullness, breadth, and ease. Retreat is best when one departs freely and far, without entanglement or obstruction. The top nine belongs to Qian, firm and decisive, already outside the hexagram and without attachment below. Its withdrawal is distant and unburdened: abundant room and ease indeed. Retreat is a time of difficulty and constraint, yet skillful conduct makes it spacious. With retreat like this, what could fail to be beneficial?

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:052 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:053 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:053 -->

**Wang Bi:** At the outermost extreme, without correspondence inside, one departs freely and decisively. The heart has neither hesitation nor backward glances. Troubles cannot entangle it; corded arrows cannot reach it. Thus spacious retreat leaves nothing unfavorable.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:054 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4605:055 -->

**Jiang Bao:** The fourth's affection does not equal the fifth's excellence; the fifth's excellence does not equal the top's spaciousness. The top has neither correspondence nor attachment to the two yin, hence spaciousness. Spaciousness is the opposite of illness and exhaustion.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4605:055 -->

**General Discussion — Xiang Anshi:** The lower three lines are Gen and governed by stopping: hence not going forward, binding with hide, and entangled retreat. The upper three are Qian and governed by movement: hence affectionate, admirable, and spacious retreat.

## 34. Dazhuang 大壯 — Great Strength

<!-- Source: https://www.eee-learning.com/book/4606 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:001 -->

![Dazhuang: Qian below, Zhen above](../assets/eee-learning/0a06d2954e4a-yi34b.png) **Dazhuang 大壯 — Great Strength** — Qian below; Zhen above.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:002 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Concerning Dazhuang, the *Sequence of the Hexagrams* says: “Retreat means withdrawal. Things cannot withdraw forever; therefore Retreat is followed by Great Strength.” Retreat means separating and departing; strength means advancing into fullness. In Retreat, yin grows and yang withdraws; Great Strength is yang's flourishing. Decline necessarily gives way to flourishing, and decrease and growth require one another. Thus retreat must be followed by strength. The hexagram has Zhen above and Qian below: Qian is firm, Zhen moves. Moving with firmness expresses Great Strength. Firm yang is great, and yang's growth has already passed the center: the great is flourishing. Thunder's awe resounding above Heaven also expresses Great Strength.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:002 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:003 -->

### Great Strength: it is beneficial to be steadfast in what is right.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:004 -->

**Original Meaning.** “Great” means yang. Four yang grow strong: hence Great Strength, the hexagram of the second month. With yang flourishing, the diviner's good fortune and success need not be stated; benefit lies only in correctness firmly maintained.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:005 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The Way of Great Strength benefits from steadfast correctness. Great strength without correctness is merely forceful aggression, not the flourishing of the noble person's Way.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:005 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:006 -->

### First Nine: Strength in the toes. Going forward brings misfortune. This is certain.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:006 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:007 -->

**Original Meaning.** The toes are below and serve advancing movement. Firm yang below, at a time of strength, is forceful in advancing: hence the image. Being below yet pushing forcefully forward necessarily brings misfortune; hence this prognostication.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:007 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:008 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The first is firm yang, belongs to Qian, and lies below: forceful in advancing. Using strength from below is strength in the toes. The toes are low and move forward. A nine below uses strength without attaining the mean. At a time of strength, firmness could not act unchecked even above others; how much less below! Going therefore brings misfortune. “This is certain”: *fu* means trustworthiness, indicating that misfortune from forceful advance is assured.[^j05-fu-certainty]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:008 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:009 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:009 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:010 -->

**Wang Bi:** Strength below is called strength in the toes. To employ firm force from below and advance thus makes severe misfortune certain. Hence “going forward brings misfortune; this is certain.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:010 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:011 -->

**Wang Shenzi:** Although firm strength gives the hexagram its meaning, the individual lines all value using yieldingness: in movement through firmness, firmness must not be excessive. The toes are below and govern walking. The first belongs to Qian, occupies firmness, and uses firmness: strong in advancing without looking back. This would be excessive even above; how much more below! Misfortune is certain.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:011 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:012 -->

### Nine in the Second Place: Steadfast correctness brings good fortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:012 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:013 -->

**Original Meaning.** Yang in yin has already lost positional correctness. Yet its centered place still allows it to avoid losing true correctness. Therefore the diviner is warned to seek correctness through centeredness; only then can good fortune be attained.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:013 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:014 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Although the second is firm yang in a time of Great Strength, it occupies yieldingness and the center. Firmness and yieldingness attain the mean; strength is not excessive, and correctness brings good fortune. Someone asks: “Is not steadfastness a warning because a nine occupies the second place?” The reply: “The *Changes* takes what predominates as its meaning. Yang firmness in the vigorous trigram, at a time of Great Strength, occupying the middle Way—there is nothing incorrect in this. The fourth, however, is warned against incorrectness. Recognize the relative weight of time and meaning, and one can study the *Changes*.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:014 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:015 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:015 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:016 -->

**Wang Bi:** Occupying the center, with yang in yin, it treads humbly rather than arrogantly. Hence steadfast correctness brings good fortune.[^j05-zhuang-readings]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:016 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:017 -->

**Yi Fu:** Lines generally value proper position, but in Great Strength yang in yin is auspicious, lest yang firmness become excessively strong. Thus both the second and fourth say that steadfast correctness brings good fortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:017 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:018 -->

### Nine in the Third Place: The petty person uses strength; the noble person uses disregard. Persistence brings danger. A ram butts a fence and entangles its horns.[^j05-disregard]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:018 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:019 -->

**Original Meaning.** Excessively firm and not centered, at a time of strength: the petty person uses force, while the noble person uses disregard. *Wang* means nothing: treating what exists as though it did not. This is a noble person excessively courageous. Even if correct, such conduct is dangerous. The ram is strong and likes to butt; the fence is a hedge; entanglement means being trapped. This is the image accompanying the prognostication of danger in steadfastness.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:019 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:020 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The third nine occupies yang with firmness at a time of strength, and stands at Qian's end: strength at its extreme. In a petty person, such extreme strength becomes using force; in a noble person, using disregard. Petty people esteem physical force and use their strength and courage. Noble people are firm in intention and therefore employ disregard. Wang means absence, much as one says to make light of something: through extreme firmness, one treats affairs contemptuously and fears nothing. “Noble” and “petty” here refer to social standing, as in “a noble person with courage but without rightness makes disorder.” When firmness and yieldingness attain the mean, one neither breaks nor bends and acts fittingly throughout the world. Excessive firmness, however, lacks harmonious compliance, injures many, and wins no allies. To hold stubbornly to it is dangerous. All creatures use their strength: those with teeth bite, those with horns butt, those with hooves kick. Sheep have strength in their heads, and rams like to butt: hence this image. A ram butts the fence because it stands before it. Butting whatever it encounters, so fond of using strength, it must entangle its horns. Likewise, someone who prizes force and uses it against everything must end broken and trapped. Why does the third's extreme strength not receive a verdict of misfortune? Proceeding thus could indeed cause misfortune, but the text is speaking of danger before misfortune has arrived. Where misfortune is possible but not yet present, it says “danger.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:020 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:021 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:021 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:022 -->

**Jing Fang:** The strength is the same: the petty person uses it; the noble person possesses it but does not use it.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:022 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:023 -->

**Liu Mu:** Wang means not. The noble person esteems virtue and does not employ force. To hold fast to force would be dangerous.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:023 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:024 -->

**Hu Yuan:** The third nine is highest in the lower trigram, at Qian's utmost vigor. Yang in yang is a person of powerful strength. A petty person in this situation must rely on force to encroach upon others and will not stop even at strength's extreme. This is using strength. The noble person is different: strong without boasting, great without self-praise, not employing strength. The petty person, in a time of power, exceeds the mean whenever acting and advances without looking back. Like a stubborn ram, it charges even when a fence stands before it, until its own horns are caught. This leads to misfortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:024 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:025 -->

**Guo Yong:** Firmness reaches strength at the third. The petty person seeks to overcome others and therefore delights in strength and employs it. The noble person seeks to overcome private desires and therefore refrains from using strength outwardly. To treat force as correctness is dangerous. The ram's obstinate butting images the use of force; butting a fence and entangling its horns images the resulting danger. “The noble person uses wang” means the noble person does not employ force. Earlier scholars who understood wang as a net were mistaken.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:025 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:026 -->

**Xiang Anshi:** “The petty person uses strength; the noble person does not”: encouragement and warning are complete. The added “persistence brings danger; a ram butts a fence and entangles its horns” guards against thinking that using firmness in a firm position must be correct.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:026 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:027 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Jing Fang and the subsequent commentators explain “uses wang” differently from Cheng's Commentary and the *Original Meaning*. Compared with the Master's *Small Image*, their explanations are more fitting.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:027 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:028 -->

### Nine in the Fourth Place: Steadfast correctness brings good fortune; regret disappears. The fence opens; no entanglement. Strength in a great wagon's axle-support.[^j05-wagon]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:028 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:029 -->

**Original Meaning.** “Steadfast correctness brings good fortune; regret disappears” is the same prognostication as Influence's fourth nine. “The fence opens; no entanglement” continues the preceding line. Opening means breaking open. The third has the fourth before it, as though a fence remained. Before the fourth lie two yin, so the fence opens. Strength in the great wagon's axle-support likewise images the possibility of advancing. Yang occupies yin without pushing firmness to its extreme; hence this image.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:029 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:030 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** At the fourth, firm yang has grown strong beyond the center: great strength indeed. Yet its fourth position is not correct. When the noble person's Way is growing, how could incorrectness be allowed? Thus the warning: correctness brings good fortune and removes regret. At a time of the Way's growth, a small mistake can injure the prospect of successful advance and cause regret. In another hexagram, doubled firmness in a yielding position need not be unfavorable—Great Excess is an example. The fence limits and separates. Once it breaks open, strength is no longer trapped. A high, great wagon with a strong wheel-support plainly travels well: hence “strength in the great wagon's axle-support.” The support is a critical part of the wheel assembly. Wagons commonly fail when it breaks; a strong support makes the wagon strong. Strength there means strength for advancing. *Fu*, axle-support, and *fu*, spoke, are interchangeable here.[^j05-wagon]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:030 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:031 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:031 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:032 -->

**Wang Bi:** No one has ever preserved strength by abandoning modesty and overstepping ritual. Thus all the yang lines favor occupying yin positions.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:032 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:033 -->

**Zheng Ruxie:** At the end of four yang, strength easily becomes excessive. Only correctness and good fortune remove regret. The yang advance together; two yin cannot stop them. The open fence without entanglement means that their Way communicates freely; the strong wagon-support means vigorous movement.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:033 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:034 -->

**Classified Conversations of Master Zhu:** The second nine's “steadfast correctness brings good fortune” means preserving oneself without advancing. The fourth, however, has the image of possible advance. Yang in yin does not exhaust its firmness, and it meets two yin ahead: the image of an opening fence. Thus it advances, unlike the second, blocked by the firm third and fourth before it.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:034 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:035 -->

**Yu Yan:** A firm line in a yielding position does not carry strength to its extreme. Hence the warning through the prognostication: “Steadfast correctness brings good fortune; regret disappears.” The third faces the fourth's firmness, like a fence barring advance; it butts and becomes entangled. The fourth faces the fifth's yieldingness, like a fence already broken, requiring no butting; hence no entanglement. Mentioning the fence but not the ram continues the third line's wording.

### Six in the Fifth Place: Losing the sheep with ease. No regret.[^j05-lost-sheep]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:036 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:037 -->

**Original Meaning.** The hexagram's form resembles Dui and gives the image of a sheep: yielding outside, firm within. The fifth six alone is yielding and central, unable to butt. Although it loses its strength, it has nothing to regret. Hence this image and prognostication. Yi means ease: suddenly losing it without noticing. It can also be read as the character for a field boundary; this too is intelligible. The *History of the Han*, in its Treatise on Food and Money, writes “boundary” as yi.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:037 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:038 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Sheep move in flocks and like to butt, imaging the yang advancing together. Four yang are growing and advancing; the yielding fifth stands above them. Trying to control them by force would be difficult and cause regret. Treat them with harmony and ease, however, and the yang have no occasion to use their firmness: their strength is lost amid harmonious ease. This avoids regret. In terms of station the fifth is properly placed; in terms of virtue it is central. It can therefore employ harmony and ease, so that even strong yang find no use for their force.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:038 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:039 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:039 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:040 -->

**Classified Conversations of Master Zhu:** In “losing the sheep at yi,” it is better to read yi as a field boundary. The *History of the Han*'s Treatise on Food and Money writes that very word as yi. Later in the *Changes*, “losing the ox at yi” has the same meaning. What I have written in the *Original Meaning* simply preserves the earlier explanation for now.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:040 -->

**Hu Bingwen:** The Wanderer's top nine loses the ox with ease. The ox is compliant; a firm line at the extreme loses compliance without noticing. Here the sheep is lost with ease. The sheep is firm; the fifth six, yielding and central, loses firmness without noticing. Having lost its strength itself, this is the only line not to speak of strength.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:041 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:042 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** The Way of Strength values the mean. The second nine, while strength is growing, occupies the center with firmness: strength's correctness. At the fifth six, strength has already passed its prime, and yieldingness occupies the center. There is no further use for force; therefore losing the sheep brings no regret.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:042 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:043 -->

### Top Six: A ram butts a fence. It cannot retreat and cannot advance. Nothing is beneficial. Recognizing the difficulty brings good fortune.[^j05-ram-difficulty]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:043 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:044 -->

**Original Meaning.** At strength's end and movement's extreme, it butts the fence and cannot retreat. Yet its underlying constitution is yielding, so neither can it complete its advance. Given this image, the prognostication is evident. Fortunately it is not firm: if it handles the matter with awareness of difficulty, good fortune remains possible.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:044 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:045 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The ram is used only for its forcefulness, so even a yin line may bear the image. A six, yin, at Zhen's end and Strength's extreme is plainly excessive. Like a ram butting a fence, advancing obstructs the body and retreating catches the horns: neither is possible. Yielding in capacity, it cannot master itself to follow rightness—hence inability to retreat. Even if a yielding person is wholly intent on force, that force cannot last; resistance makes it shrink back—hence inability to advance. Acting thus brings no benefit anywhere. Yielding yin in Strength cannot hold firm: difficulty and constraint must make it lose its strength. In losing strength, it returns to the proper condition of yielding weakness. Thus difficulty brings good fortune. Using strength is unfavorable; recognizing difficulty and taking a yielding position is auspicious. Strength's end contains the possibility of change.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:045 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:046 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:046 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:047 -->

**Classified Conversations of Master Zhu:** The top six's image is especially apt. Strength has ended, movement is extreme, and there is nowhere to go, like a ram whose horns catch on a fence, unable to retreat or advance. Yet “difficulty brings good fortune” means that advance ultimately remains possible: only recognizing the difficulty makes it auspicious.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:047 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:048 -->

**Yi Fu:** The third faces the fourth, so it butts a fence. The fourth faces yin, so the fence opens. The top six has no obstruction ahead, yet also butts a fence because it is at the hexagram's limit. It cannot retreat, being above all the lines; it cannot advance, being too high to proceed. Yet recognizing difficulty brings good fortune: this is how the *Changes* completes both encouragement and warning.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:048 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:049 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Both the fifth and top are yin, at a time when yang's strength has already passed its height. Why does the fifth lose the sheep, while the top butts a fence as a ram? The *Changes* works through images. A sheep butts with its horns; the hexagram resembles Dui, giving a sheep-image, and the top six occupies the horns' position. Thus even a yin line butts the fence. Its yieldingness prevents the horns from becoming entangled; it merely cannot retreat or advance. “Recognizing difficulty brings good fortune” means understanding the difficulty and not treating it lightly. Advance when advance is possible; withdraw when it is not. This is what the *Miscellaneous Hexagrams* means by “Great Strength stops.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4606:049 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4606:050 -->

**General Discussion — Xiang Anshi:** Sometimes correctness means attaining the mean in practical principle; sometimes it means yin and yang occupying their proper positions. Balancing firmness with yieldingness and yieldingness with firmness, so that neither loses correctness, is correctness in principle. Firmness in a firm position and yieldingness in a yielding position is positional correctness. In Great Strength's time and meaning, benefit in steadfast correctness means preserving correctness in principle, not in line position. Thus the second nine, fourth nine, and fifth six are improperly placed yet favorable; the first nine, third nine, and top six are properly placed yet unfavorable. The second and fourth explicitly promise good fortune through steadfast correctness; the first and third explicitly warn of misfortune in advancing and danger in persistence. Lest this remain unclear, the sage explains in the *Small Image*: “The second nine's steadfast correctness brings good fortune because it is central”—good fortune through correctness rests on centeredness, not position. At the fifth six, “the position is not appropriate” likewise shows that freedom from regret rests on centeredness, not position. Such are the *Changes*' continual shifts in the meaning of the time.

## 35. Jin 晉 — Advance

<!-- Source: https://www.eee-learning.com/book/4607 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4607:001 -->

![Jin: Kun below, Li above](../assets/eee-learning/e96202f1c760-yi35b.png) **Jin 晉 — Advance** — Kun below; Li above.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4607:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4607:002 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Concerning Jin, the *Sequence of the Hexagrams* says: “Things cannot remain forever strong; therefore Great Strength is followed by Advance. Jin means advancing.” Things do not become strong only to stop forever; flourishing strength must advance. Thus Advance follows Great Strength. Li stands above Kun: light emerging above the earth. The sun rises from the earth, ascending and growing brighter: hence Advance, movement into abundant radiance. Anything gradually flourishing is advancing, so the Judgment says, “Jin means advance.” Some hexagrams state their virtues and others do not, as appropriate. Outside Qian and Kun, some certainly say “great success.” Those saying “benefit in steadfast correctness” indicate what is lacking but can bring accomplishment. There are differences, as in Revolution and Gradual Progress; these appear in their respective hexagrams. Advance's flourishing does not name virtues because it has no need to do so. Its light is already abundant, so success need not be stated again. It complies with great illumination, so no admonition to correctness is needed.[^j05-jin-virtues]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4607:002 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4607:003 -->

### Advance: the lord who brings peace is granted horses in abundance and received three times in a single day.[^j05-peace-lord]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4607:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4607:004 -->

**Original Meaning.** Jin means advance. The peace-bringing lord is one who secures his state. Abundant horses and three audiences in a day signify lavish gifts and manifest personal honor. The hexagram has Li above and Kun below: the image of the sun rising above the earth, and the virtue of compliance joined to great illumination. Moreover, it comes by transformation from Viewing: the yielding fourth six advances upward to the fifth. A diviner possessing these three qualities should likewise receive such favor.[^j05-jin-transformation]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4607:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4607:005 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Advance is a time of rising prosperity. Great illumination above and compliant attachment below image the feudal lords supporting their king: hence the peace-bringing lord. This is a lord whose governance brings security, sharing the virtue of the great illumination above and attaching himself compliantly to it. Thus he receives marks of favor and many horses. Carriages and horses are substantial gifts; “in abundance” means many. Not only are the gifts generous: he is personally honored, received three times within daylight hours. This expresses favor at its height. In a time of Advance, illumination above and compliance below bring ruler and minister into accord. From the superior's side, there is advance into radiant prosperity; from the minister's, promotion to distinction and receipt of shining favor.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4607:005 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4607:006 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4607:006 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4607:007 -->

**Guo Yong:** Advance receives its name on grounds somewhat like Great Possession. Fire above Heaven in Great Possession expresses the ruler's Way; light emerging above the earth in Advance expresses the minister's Way. A minister's advancement alone supplies the meaning of a whole hexagram. This is the minister's Way at its greatest: who but a peace-bringing lord could fulfill it?

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4607:007 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4607:008 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** The *Changes* has three hexagrams of progress: Advance, Pushing Upward, and Gradual Progress. All mean advancing, but differ. Advance is like the sun just rising and has the finest meaning. Pushing Upward is like a tree beginning to grow and comes next. Gradual Progress is like a tree already growing and gradually becoming tall and great; it comes after these. Their Judgments make the distinction clear.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4607:008 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4607:009 -->

### First Six: Advancing, then checked. Steadfast correctness brings good fortune. Not trusted: spacious patience leaves no blame.[^j05-patience]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4607:009 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4607:010 -->

**Original Meaning.** Yin below, with a correspondent neither centered nor correct, images wishing to advance and being checked. A diviner in this situation who preserves correctness meets good fortune. Even if others do not trust one, one should respond with breadth and patience; then there is no blame.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4607:010 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4607:011 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The first lies at Advance's bottom, the beginning of progress. “Advancing” means rising; “checked” means being restrained and pushed back. At the beginning, whether one's progress succeeds or not, only correctness brings good fortune. “Not trusted”: how could one at the bottom, only beginning to advance, immediately win deep trust above? If the superior does not yet trust one, one should preserve an inward steadiness, composed and spacious, not hurry to secure that trust. An urgent desire to be trusted either makes one fret and abandon one's principles or become indignant and injure rightness. Both carry blame. Breadth and patience avoid blame: the noble person's Way in advancement and withdrawal.

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4607:012 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4607:012 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4607:013 -->

**Wang Anshi:** The first six advances yieldingly: a noble person measuring advancement and withdrawal by ritual and rightness. Ordinary people, when not trusted, either hurry forward to seek accomplishment or hurry away in resentment that their superior does not recognize them. Confucius said, “I am waiting for the right buyer”: not trusted, yet patient in advancing. Mencius lingered in Qi: not trusted, yet patient in withdrawing.

Lest ‘steadfast correctness brings good fortune’ remain unclear, it makes the meaning plain again.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4607:014 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4607:015 -->

**Hu Bingwen:** At the beginning of advance, others often do not yet trust one. But obstruction lies with them; I must nevertheless remain correct. Distrust lies with others; I must nevertheless remain spacious and patient. Both correctness and patience are admonitions.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4607:015 -->

### Six in the Second Place: Advancing, yet sorrowful. Steadfast correctness brings good fortune. Receive this great blessing from the royal ancestress.[^j05-royal-ancestress]

**Original Meaning.** The second six is centered and correct, but has no responding support above: hence wishing to advance yet sorrowful. A diviner in this situation who preserves correctness meets good fortune and receives blessing from the royal ancestress. The royal ancestress refers to the fifth six. This is an auspicious prognostication for offering sacrifice to a deceased ancestress; anyone occupying an honored place through yin belongs to the same category.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4607:017 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4607:018 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The second six is below, without supporting correspondence above. Centered, correct, and gentle in virtue, it does not force its way forward. Thus advancement entails sorrow: progress is difficult. Yet preserving steadfast correctness should bring good fortune; hence the line's words. The royal ancestress is the grandmother, the highest honored yin, referring to the fifth six. The second preserves itself through centered correctness. The fifth six is a greatly illuminated ruler sharing its virtue, who must seek it and bestow favor and emolument. Thus it receives the great blessing from the royal ancestress. Jie means great.

**Collected Explanations.**

**Hu Bingwen:** Small Excess's second six says, “Encountering the ancestress.” Its ancestral mother is the royal ancestress here.

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4607:021 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** The second and fifth are corresponding positions. Yin responding to yang or yang to yin images ruler and minister; yin responding to yin images ancestress and granddaughter-in-law. Why “royal ancestress,” not simply “mother”? Ritual gives weight to the alternating ancestral generations, zhao and mu. Thus a grandson is joined in ancestral rites to his grandfather, and a grandson's wife to her husband's grandmother. The generations are matched; in the *Changes*, this matching images correspondence. Here “royal ancestress” is explicit. Small Excess says only “ancestress” because it follows the preceding mention of passing the grandfather.

**The compilers continue:** The fifth six governs the hexagram, and the second responds to it: hence receiving blessing.

### Six in the Third Place: The multitude gives its assent. Regret disappears.

**Original Meaning.** The third is neither centered nor correct and should have regret. Yet it and the two yin below all wish to advance upward, so the multitude trusts it, and regret disappears.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4607:024 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4607:025 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** A six in the third place lacks centered correctness and should entail regret and blame. Yet it stands atop the trigram of compliance, at compliance's utmost. All three yin comply upward; the third shares their intention and wins their assent. Thus regret disappears. With the intention to comply upward toward illumination, and the multitude assenting and following, what could be unfavorable? Someone asks: “Can joining the multitude without centered correctness be good?” The reply: “What the multitude assents to must be supremely fitting. Moreover, complying with great illumination above—how could that be bad? Hence regret disappears: the fault of lacking centered correctness is removed.” The ancients said, “Counsel that follows the multitude accords with Heaven's heart.”

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4607:026 -->

**Wu Yueshen:** The first is not trusted; the third receives the multitude's assent and is trusted. Trust below leads to trust above. Thus without the trust of one's friends, one cannot win acceptance above.

**Xiang Anshi:** Advance's Way is compliance joined to great illumination, yieldingness advancing upward: all center on compliance. **Editorial Judgment.** From the Judgment, the fourth nine—one yang near the ruler—would seem the peace-bringing lord's position. Why does the line's meaning contradict this? In a time of Advance, occupying a high station while losing the Way of quiet correctness and the discipline of modest withdrawal, greedy yet afraid of others—what could this be but the great rat? “Persistence brings danger” warns against making retention of emolument and position one's constant aim, without understanding advancement and withdrawal.

### Six in the Fifth Place: Regret disappears. Do not be troubled by loss or gain. Going forward brings good fortune; nothing is unfavorable.

**Original Meaning.** Yin in yang should bring regret. But great illumination is above and all below comply, so the diviner's regret disappears. **Cheng's Commentary.** Yieldingness in the honored place should bring regret, but great illumination with universal attachment below removes it. Do not insist on personally exercising discernment or fret over losses and gains. Proceeding thus brings good fortune with nothing unfavorable. The fifth six is a greatly illuminated ruler. The danger is not insufficient insight, but its excessive use: petty scrutiny that loses the Way of delegation. Where private judgment makes partial appointments, failure to examine causes blindness; where one fully employs the world's impartial capacities, why return to private scrutiny?[^j05-loss-gain]

**Collected Explanations.**

**Shi Jie:** Taking responsibility through the Way, one remains true to it whether gaining or losing, never allowing either to occupy the mind. The petty person worries about gaining and losing: that is the anxiety meant here.

At a time of Advance, fear of losing or not gaining easily arises; yielding capacity is also easily burdened by loss and gain. Great illumination above should be used for what ought to be done, not for private calculations of accomplishment and profit.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4607:040 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** The Judgment describes the favor shown to the peace-bringing lord; the Commentary explains this through yieldingness advancing upward. Clearly the sage identifies this line with that lord and makes it the hexagram's governing line. Every hexagram has a governing line, the one that accords with its Judgment. The fourth nine is high, yet its statement is so unfavorable that only the fifth six can fulfill the Judgment's meaning. Advancing like a great rat means worrying about gains and losses—the conduct of a base person. Not worrying about loss or gain means exhausting one's sincerity and loyalty—the noble person's intention.

### Top Nine: Advancing with the horns. Use this only to chastise one's own domain. Danger, yet good fortune and no blame. Persistence brings humiliation.[^j05-chastise-domain]

Firmness advancing to the limit departs greatly from the mean. It is suitable for nothing except chastising one's own domain; there, despite danger, it brings good fortune without blame. Campaigning in the four directions governs outwardly; chastising one's own domain governs inwardly. Thus this means inward self-government. In governing oneself, extreme firmness holds the Way more securely, and extreme advancement hastens the turn toward goodness. Applied inwardly, the top nine may be harsh, yet it brings good fortune without blame. Harshness is not peaceful harmony, but can accomplish something in self-government. “Persistence brings humiliation” completes the meaning: extreme firmness in advancing may accomplish self-correction, but is not centered, harmonious virtue and is therefore deficient against the standard of true correctness. Steadfast correctness does not lose the center.

**Wang Zongchuan:** At Advance's top nine, advancement has reached the horns and can go no further. Only turning back upon oneself, mastering oneself, and governing inwardly bring vigilant awareness of danger and good fortune. This avoids the blame of forceful advancement.

## 37. Jiaren 家人 — The Family

<!-- Source: https://www.eee-learning.com/book/4609 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4609:001 -->

![Jiaren: Li below, Xun above](../assets/eee-learning/8362e6288bb7-yi37b.png) **Jiaren 家人 — The Family** — Li below; Xun above.[^j05-family]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4609:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4609:002 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Concerning Jiaren, the *Sequence of the Hexagrams* says: “Yi means injury. Those injured outside must return to the family; therefore Darkening of the Light is followed by The Family.” Injury and distress outside lead back within. The Family concerns the Way inside the household: affection between father and son, rightness between husband and wife, and the order of honored and humble, elder and younger. Correct relationships and deepen affection and rightness: this is the family's Way. Xun outside and Li within show wind issuing from fire. Strong fire gives rise to wind; wind born from fire emerges from within. This images influence extending from the family outward. The second and fifth correctly position woman and man within and without. Illumination within and deference without constitute the Way of household life. What one possesses in oneself can be applied to the family; what one practices in the family can be applied to the state and ultimately bring order to the world. Governing the world is simply governing the family extended outward. Hence the image of issuing from within. The book of Master Wenzhong interprets this as “illuminating within and ordering without,” an explanation praised in ancient and recent times, but not the intention of the image. “Things become ordered in Xun” means that all things become clean and orderly in Xun's direction; it does not mean Xun itself signifies ordering. Likewise, “they contend in Qian” does not make contention Qian's meaning.[^j05-family-wind]

### Six in the Second Place: Pursue nothing independently. Attend to nourishment within. Steadfast correctness brings good fortune.[^j05-inner-provision]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4609:015 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4609:016 -->

**Original Meaning.** The second six is yielding, compliant, centered, and correct: the woman properly positioned within. Hence this image and prognostication.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4609:016 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4609:017 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Among family and flesh-and-blood relations, feelings generally overcome ritual and affection displaces rightness. Only someone firmly established can prevent private love from displacing correct principle. Thus The Family chiefly favors firmness, as in the first, third, and top lines. The second six has yielding capacity and occupies yieldingness: it cannot govern the household. Hence “pursue nothing independently”—nothing it undertakes in that role is suitable. Even heroic people can drown in affection and fail to preserve themselves; how much less can a weak person master attachment to wife and children! Yet a capacity like the second's is correct for the woman's Way: yielding compliance in centered correctness. Attending to nourishment within therefore attains correctness and good fortune. The woman dwells within and governs provisioning: hence nourishment within.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4609:017 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4609:018 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4609:018 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4609:019 -->

**Kong Yingda:** The second six occupies the center in its correct position and responds as yin to yang, fulfilling the woman's duty. Her constant Way is deferential compliance, not insisting on carrying anything through independently. Her principal responsibility is food within the household and offerings for sacrifice. This attains the woman's correctness, hence the line's statement.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4609:019 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4609:020 -->

**Wang Zongchuan:** “Pursue nothing independently” indicates not daring to act on one's sole authority. The woman's duty extends to serving sacrifices and providing food and drink, with no other business beyond these. The *Odes* says, “Neither wrongdoing nor self-willed initiative; let food and wine be her concern.” Gathering southernwood for sacrifice is not neglecting one's duty; gathering duckweed for sacrifice follows the proper standards. Extend this upward or downward through social ranks, and the responsibilities remain alike. This is steadfast correctness and good fortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4609:020 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4609:021 -->

**Yi Fu:** Yielding, compliant, and correctly placed, the second six responds to the fifth nine: the woman properly situated within. This line directly develops the meaning of benefit in the woman's steadfast correctness.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4609:021 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4609:022 -->

### Nine in the Third Place: The household stern and clamorous. Regret and severity, yet good fortune. Wife and children laughing without restraint: humiliation in the end.[^j05-severity]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4609:022 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4609:023 -->

**Original Meaning.** Firmness in a firm position, without centeredness, is excessive firmness: hence the image of stern severity. There may be regret and harshness, yet the outcome is auspicious. Unrestrained laughter is the opposite and leads to humiliation. Each diviner receives the outcome corresponding to their character, so both possibilities are stated.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4609:023 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4609:024 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The exact meaning of *haohao* is unclear. From its wording and sound, it resembles clamorous cries and suggests urgent constraint. The third nine stands at the inner trigram's top and governs within. Yang in firmness without centeredness is correctly placed but overly firm. Excessive firmness in household governance injures through harsh urgency, so the family becomes stern and clamorous. Over-strictness cannot avoid injury and must bring regret over severity. Affection predominates among kin, so excessive strictness is regretted. Yet even without the mean between lenience and force, an orderly, respectful household whose members are reverently cautious is still auspicious. Unrestrained laughter among wife and children, by contrast, leads eventually to humiliation. The hexagram does not itself present that image; it is stated as the opposite of sternness. Better excessive strictness than failure through indulgence. Such laughter means merriment without measure. Unregulated self-indulgence ultimately ruins a family and is shameful. Excessive caution injures human feeling, but established standards and correct relationships preserve affection and rightness. Unmeasured merriment undermines standards and disorders relationships: how could the family survive? Its extreme brings the misfortune of household ruin. Only humiliation is stated because humiliation must grow extreme before reaching misfortune; misfortune is not immediately pronounced.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4609:024 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4609:025 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4609:025 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4609:026 -->

**A question in the Classified Conversations of Master Zhu:** “The Commentary says the Way of household governance is to correct relationships and deepen affection and rightness. Yet correcting relationships seems to injure affection, while deepening affection seems to violate proper relationships. How is this resolved?” The reply: “One must deepen affection and rightness precisely where relationships are corrected, and deepen them without losing those relationships. Only that will do.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4609:026 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4609:027 -->

**Hu Bingwen:** Sternness makes rightness prevail over feeling; although regrettably severe, it is auspicious. Unrestrained laughter makes feeling prevail over rightness and ends in humiliation. Regret turns from misfortune toward good fortune; humiliation turns from good fortune toward misfortune. The third nine, firm in a firm position, seems able to be strict with the household. Yet adjoining two yielding lines, it also seems prone to excessive fondness for wife and children. Does it not stand between good and ill fortune? Hence both regret and humiliation are stated.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4609:027 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4609:028 -->

### Six in the Fourth Place: Enriching the household. Great good fortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4609:028 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4609:029 -->

**Original Meaning.** Yang governs rightness; yin, benefit. Yin occupying yin in an upper position can enrich the household.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4609:029 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4609:030 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The six has deferential compliance and occupies the fourth, its correct place. Correct placement means secure dwelling. Compliant in affairs and following the correct Way, it can preserve wealth. In household life, preserving wealth is great good fortune. Why does the fourth, a high station, mention only wealth? In a family, high station is household honor. Retaining wealth means preserving the family. What greater good fortune could there be?

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4609:030 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4609:031 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** In other hexagrams the fourth is the minister's Way; in The Family it is also the wife's Way. The husband governs the household's teaching, the wife its nourishment: what Laozi calls the teaching father and nourishing mother. From the second's provision within to the fourth's enrichment of the household, the inward responsibilities are fulfilled.[^j05-household-wealth]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4609:031 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4609:032 -->

### Nine in the Fifth Place: The king reaches his household. Do not worry. Good fortune.[^j05-reaching-family]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4609:032 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4609:033 -->

**Original Meaning.** Jia means to arrive, as in arriving at the great temple. “Having a household” is like “having a state.” The fifth nine is firm, vigorous, centered, and correct, responding below to the second six's yielding, compliant, centered correctness. A king who reaches his household in this manner need not worry: good fortune is certain. This is an auspicious prognostication for the betrothal and reception of a queen or consort; anyone possessing this virtue who receives it likewise meets good fortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4609:033 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4609:034 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The fifth nine is male and outside, firm and in a yang position, honored and centered-correct; its correspondent is compliant and correct within. This is household governance at its most correct and good. The fifth is the ruler's position, hence “king.” Jia means reaching: bringing the Way of having a family to its utmost. The king cultivates himself to order the family; when the family is correct, the world is ordered. No sage king has failed to begin with reverence in himself and correctness in his household. Once this Way reaches completion, the world is governed without anxious exertion: do not worry, and good fortune follows. The fifth reverently orders himself outside, the second orders the household within. Their shared virtue inside and outside is complete indeed.[^j05-family-supplement]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4609:034 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4609:035 -->

**Qiu Fuguo:** The third and fifth are firm yang and both govern the family. The third is firm but not central and errs through excessive strictness, incurring regret and severity. The fifth is firm and central, dignified yet loving, fully realizing household governance. All are transformed; anxiety is unnecessary and good fortune follows. Someone asks: “Household governance values strictness; the Judgment commends stern correctness, but the fifth stresses mutual love. Why?” The reply: “Strictness concerns proper distinctions and the family's rectification. Love concerns feeling and reaching the family. Jia implies moving and reaching others inwardly; hence mutual love.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4609:035 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4609:036 -->

**Gong Huan:** Jia is the same as ge, as in “reaching without words” and “luminously reaching the glorious ancestors”: moving and reaching inwardly. The fifth nine occupies the honored place with firm, centered correctness and governs the household. Its abundant virtue and supreme goodness reach the family members' hearts completely. A king's household is large and numerous, and its hearts difficult to unite. Where influence has not yet reached, do not worry; good fortune comes of itself. The first's safeguarding establishes rules and standards. The king's reaching the household transforms it through his own conduct.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4609:036 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4609:037 -->

**He Kai:** “Shun arrived before the Cultured Ancestor” and “the duke arrived at the great temple” show ge and jia used interchangeably. With personal example correct, one can influence the family so that father acts as father, son as son, elder and younger brothers each fulfill their roles, husband acts as husband, and wife as wife. Each finds the proper place and deepens mutual affection. A correct family settles the world, so good fortune comes without anxious contrivance.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4609:037 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4609:038 -->

**Master You:** The fifth is honored, hence the king. Jia means moving and reaching inwardly, as in “the king reaches the temple.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4609:038 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4609:039 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** The explanations of jia as inwardly moving and reaching have clear evidence and should be followed. He Kai's explanation is especially consonant with the *Image*.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4609:039 -->

### Top Nine: Sincerity and commanding dignity. Good fortune in the end.[^j05-family-trust]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4609:040 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4609:041 -->

**Original Meaning.** The top nine is firm and above, at the hexagram's end. Thus it speaks of the enduring Way of correcting the family. The diviner must possess sincere trustworthiness and serious dignity; then there is good fortune in the end.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4609:041 -->

Without perfect sincerity, the family cannot be governed. Trustworthiness must dwell within; then one can endure and the household members transform themselves. If goodness does not arise from perfect sincerity, one cannot preserve it oneself, much less cause others to do so. Thus sincerity is the foundation. In the affection surrounding wife and children, excessive tenderness removes strictness and excessive favor conceals rightness. Household trouble commonly begins with inadequate ritual and standards, allowing irreverence to arise. No household has escaped disorder when elders lose dignity and the young forget respectful compliance.[^j05-family-readings] Thus commanding dignity is necessary for final good fortune. Preserving the family to the end depends on these two alone—sincerity and dignity—so they are stated at the hexagram's conclusion.

No family has ever become orderly under such conditions. Hence the conclusion speaks of trustworthiness and dignity. These two preserve the family's final good fortune.

## 38. Kui 睽 — Opposition

<!-- Source: https://www.eee-learning.com/book/4610 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4610:001 -->

![Kui: Dui below, Li above](../assets/eee-learning/3c8b91972f8e-yi38b.png) **Kui 睽 — Opposition** — Dui below; Li above.[^j05-opposition]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4610:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4610:002 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Concerning Kui, the *Sequence of the Hexagrams* says: “When the family's Way reaches exhaustion, discord must arise; therefore The Family is followed by Opposition. Kui means discord.” Exhaustion of the family's Way necessarily brings estrangement and dispersal; thus Opposition follows The Family. Li is above and Dui below. Li's fire flames upward; Dui's marsh moistens downward. The two move contrary to one another: the meaning of Opposition. Moreover, the middle and youngest daughters live together but will marry into different households. Their intentions do not move together: this too expresses Opposition.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4610:002 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4610:003 -->

### Opposition: good fortune in small affairs.[^j05-small-affairs]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4610:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4610:004 -->

**Original Meaning.** Kui means discord and difference. Fire above and marsh below have contrary natures; the middle and youngest daughters intend different destinations. Hence Opposition. Yet the hexagram's virtues are delight within and illumination without. In terms of transformation, when it comes from Clinging, yieldingness advances to the third; when from Inner Sincerity, yieldingness advances to the fifth; when from The Family, both changes occur. In its structure, the fifth six attains the center and responds below to the second nine's firmness. Thus the prognostication does not permit great undertakings, but small affairs still have a path to good fortune.[^j05-kui-transformations]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4610:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4610:005 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Opposition is a time of estrangement and separation, not an auspicious course. Yet the hexagram's good capacities allow good fortune in small affairs even amid estrangement.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4610:005 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4610:006 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4610:006 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4610:007 -->

**Master Cheng:** Good fortune in small affairs means simply that even in a time of estrangement, one can still bring small affairs to a favorable outcome. Estrangement cannot be allowed to remain estrangement forever; there must be a Way to remedy it.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4610:007 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4610:008 -->

**Zhao Rumei:** Estrangement may simply arise from human feelings and circumstances. The sage has a means of responding to the time. Good fortune in small affairs means that skillful conduct within disagreement itself can still be auspicious. Is this not like limited rectification in Difficulty at the Beginning, or the time for action within described in the *Great Plan*?

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4610:008 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4610:009 -->

**He Kai:** Once estrangement has arisen, one cannot drive or pressure people with anger and resentment. Refraining from excess and bringing about change gradually is the sound way to reconcile opposition. Hence good fortune in small affairs. “Small affairs” is like saying to conduct affairs through yieldingness—not that great affairs are inauspicious and small ones auspicious.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4610:009 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4610:010 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Consider such line images as meeting an ill-disposed person, an encounter in a lane, and biting into tender flesh: Zhao and He's explanations of good fortune in small affairs are correct. Accommodating, working indirectly, and proceeding where things are easier all belong to this meaning.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4610:010 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4610:011 -->

### First Nine: Regret disappears. Lose a horse: do not pursue it; it returns of itself. Meet an ill-disposed person. No blame.[^j05-lost-horse]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4610:011 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4610:012 -->

**Original Meaning.** Without a proper correspondent above, there is regret. Yet in a time of Opposition, those of shared virtue respond to one another, and regret disappears. Hence the lost horse that returns of itself without pursuit. Nevertheless, one must also meet an ill-disposed person to avoid blame, as Confucius did with Yang Huo.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4610:012 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4610:013 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The nine is at the hexagram's beginning, the beginning of estrangement. Firmness moving below in such a time plainly entails regret. Regret can disappear because the fourth nine above is likewise firm yang, estranged and without companionship: those of the same kind naturally join. Both are yang, both occupy the bottom of their respective trigrams, and they occupy corresponding positions. Two yang do not ordinarily correspond, but in Opposition they unite. Their mutual support removes regret. Opposition's lines all involve relationships of correspondence. Union makes estrangement possible; where things are different from the outset, what estrangement is there? Only the first and fourth, though not proper correspondents, associate through shared virtue and therefore meet. A horse enables travel, and yang moves upward. Estranged and alone, one cannot proceed: this is losing the horse. Once the fourth joins, movement becomes possible: the horse is recovered without pursuit. An ill-disposed person is someone at odds with oneself; meeting means establishing communication. Even though people of shared virtue associate during Opposition, estranged petty people are very numerous. To abandon and reject them would be almost to set the whole world against the noble person. This loses breadth and tolerance and invites misfortune and blame. How could one then transform the ungood and bring them into harmony? Thus meeting the ill-disposed avoids blame. Ancient sage kings transformed the treacherous and violent into good people and turned enemies into subjects because they did not sever relations with them.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4610:013 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4610:014 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4610:014 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4610:015 -->

**Zheng Ruxie:** At Opposition's beginning and the bottom of the hexagram, one must wait calmly and receive others generously; then estrangement can be reconciled. Not pursuing the lost horse, which returns in time, is calm waiting. Estranged and without correspondence, one finds others contrary; rejecting them makes them still more contrary. Hence generous acceptance. No Way of reconciling opposition is better.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4610:015 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4610:016 -->

**Xiang Anshi:** The horse returns without pursuit: do not chase what departs. Meeting the ill-disposed without blame: do not reject those who come. This is the noble person's Way in Opposition when below and without correspondence. “Meet” has the same sense as “when pressed, one may see him”: it does not mean going out to seek him. Seeking him would contradict the warning against pursuit.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4610:016 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4610:017 -->

**Wang Shenzi:** At Opposition's beginning, estrangement is not yet deep, the lost horse has not gone far, and the ill-disposed person's alienation is not yet extreme. Pursuing the lost horse drives it further away; provoking the ill-disposed makes estrangement worse. Therefore let the horse return without pursuit, and meet the person to avoid blame. This is the proper course at the beginning. Otherwise, estrangement ends only in estrangement.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4610:017 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4610:018 -->

**He Kai:** Wait quietly, receive others modestly, and remain untroubled, as though not seeing the estrangement. Only by not fastening upon the estrangement can it be reconciled.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4610:018 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4610:019 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** This line expresses not erecting fixed divisions between agreement and difference. Not demanding agreement, one does not pursue the lost horse. Not establishing difference as a barrier, one meets the ill-disposed. Yet this is appropriate only at the beginning and below, when estrangement is not yet severe. Establish this disposition as the foundation, then adapt to one's circumstances. Here regret disappears because there is no proper correspondent: as Master Cheng says, union permits estrangement, but where things were different from the outset, what estrangement is there? The wording matches the fifth six's disappearance of regret, but the meaning differs.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4610:019 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4610:020 -->

### Nine in the Second Place: Encounter the lord in a lane. No blame.[^j05-lane]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4610:020 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4610:021 -->

**Original Meaning.** The second and fifth are proper yin-yang correspondents, yet in a time of Opposition they are at odds and do not unite. They must seek one another by indirect means and achieve an encounter before there is no blame. Hence this image and prognostication.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4610:021 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4610:022 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The second and fifth are proper correspondents, meant to associate. But in a time of estrangement, the principle of yin-yang correspondence weakens and the tendency of firm and yielding to oppose one another prevails. Recognizing this teaches the student of the *Changes* how to adapt. Thus, although proper correspondents, they must seek one another indirectly. The second, firm and centered below, responds to the fifth six as ruler. If their Ways unite, its intention can be realized and estrangement remedied. Yet their bond is insecure in such a time, and the second must seek an encounter through accommodating approaches, hoping for union. Hence encountering the lord in a lane. Only achieving union avoids blame: estrangement between ruler and minister is a great fault. A lane is a winding way; an encounter is a meeting. One should seek indirectly, hoping to meet and unite. Such accommodation means proceeding by good means, gently adjusting to bring about union—not bending oneself or compromising the Way.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4610:022 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4610:023 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4610:023 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4610:024 -->

**Zhang Qingzi:** In Opposition, only the second nine encounters the fifth six as lord. Hence encountering the lord in a lane. The Judgment's “attaining the center and responding to firmness” refers to this line.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4610:024 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4610:025 -->

**Jiang Tisheng:** The first and fourth meet through shared virtue; the second and fifth, proper correspondents, also “encounter.” The *Small Image* likewise says the third six “encounters firmness.” In a time of separation, seeking and joining one another may be simple in ceremony but is deeply earnest in feeling.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4610:025 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4610:026 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** In the *Spring and Autumn Annals*' usage, a meeting with full ceremony is called a formal assembly; without full ceremony, an encounter. Opposition repeatedly says “encounter”: this is good fortune in small affairs. Moreover, in ritual, ruler and minister or host and guest meet by crossing the courtyard and ascending the hall. A lane is a narrow path near the palace wall. Thus the ancients' running close along a wall expresses humility. Modest, discreet, and deferential in finding an entrance: this too is good fortune in small affairs.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4610:026 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4610:027 -->

### Six in the Third Place: See the wagon dragged back, its ox restrained, its occupant's hair shorn and nose cut off. No beginning, but an end.[^j05-shorn-nose]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4610:027 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4610:028 -->

**Original Meaning.** The third six and top nine properly correspond, but the third stands between two yang: dragged from behind by the second and restrained ahead by the fourth. At a time of Opposition, the top nine's jealous suspicion is also deep; hence the further injury of cropped hair and a severed nose. Yet perversity cannot overcome correctness, and union must ultimately be achieved. Hence this image and prognostication.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4610:028 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4610:029 -->

**Base-edition gloss:** *Kun* 髡, pronounced like 昆, is the punishment of shaving the hair.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4610:029 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4610:030 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Yielding yin can scarcely stand independently in ordinary times; how much less amid estrangement! The third lies between two firm lines, unable to find a secure place, and plainly suffers encroachment. Its proper correspondent is above, and it wishes to advance and unite intentions with it; but the fourth blocks ahead and the second pulls from behind. Wagon and ox are means of travel. The dragged wagon is restraint from behind; the checked ox is obstruction ahead. Those behind pull it back; what stands ahead is what the advancing person strives against. Thus it suffers further injury above, inflicted by the fourth. “Its occupant's hair shorn and nose cut off”: tian means shaving the head, yi cutting off the nose. The third follows its proper correspondent, but the fourth blocks it. Although yielding yin, the third occupies firmness and intends to proceed; forcing its way against the obstruction, it suffers injury. Shorn hair followed by a severed nose means repeated injury. It does not unite with the second or fourth: at a time of estrangement, union with them has no basis. This accords with occupying firmness and preserving correctness. As for its proper correspondent, estrangement's extreme contains the principle of eventual union. Initially oppressed by the two yang, it has no beginning; afterward it must unite, and so has an end. The character for restraining combines “control” and “hand,” signifying holding back.[^j05-kui-punctuation]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4610:030 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4610:031 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4610:031 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4610:032 -->

**Hu Yuan:** Tian should be written er 而. The old forms resemble one another; later copying introduced the error. Under Han law, cutting the temple hair of an offender was called er. Likewise, in the *Rites of Zhou*, the carpenter's er figures on musical-instrument supports signify shorn temple hair.[^j05-shorn-nose]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4610:032 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4610:033 -->

### Nine in the Fourth Place: Estranged and alone. Encounter the good man; exchange sincere trust. Danger, but no blame.[^j05-good-man]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4610:033 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4610:034 -->

**Original Meaning.** Estranged and alone means lacking a correspondent. Encountering the good man means finding the first nine. Exchanging trust means that shared virtue brings mutual confidence. Yet this is Opposition's time, so awareness of danger is necessary to avoid blame. The same applies to the diviner.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4610:034 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4610:035 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The fourth nine, in a time of Opposition, occupies an insecure place, without correspondence and between two yin: estranged and alone. Firm in virtue, isolated without allies in such a time, it must seek and unite with those of kindred disposition. Hence encountering the good man. “Man” designates yang; yuan means good. At Opposition's beginning, the first nine joins someone of shared virtue and removes estrangement's regret, conducting itself excellently. Thus it is called the good man, as one might say a worthy gentleman. The fourth has passed the center, its estrangement already severe, and does not equal the first's goodness. Both are yang at the bottom of their respective trigrams, in corresponding positions. Each lacks responding support, so shared virtue naturally draws them together. Such a meeting requires perfect sincerity: exchanging trust means each possesses sincere trustworthiness. If the two yang above and below unite through perfect sincerity, in what time could they not act, what danger could they not overcome? Thus there is no blame even amid danger. Estranged, alone between two yin, and incorrectly placed, the fourth faces danger and blame. Encountering the good man and exchanging trust frees it from blame.

**Collected Explanations.**

Though the fifth has yielding capacity, the second helps it through yang firmness and enters deeply. Then one can proceed with blessings: what blame remains? King Cheng of Zhou, despite his youth, established flourishing royal government; Liu Shan, despite his obscurity and weakness, had the prospect of restoration. This came from entrusting themselves to sage and worthy assistance, and from the depth with which the Duke of Zhou and Kongming entered their hearts.

## 39. Jian 蹇 — Limping

<!-- Source: https://www.eee-learning.com/book/4611 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4611:001 -->

![Jian: Gen below, Kan above](../assets/eee-learning/277a9af2af33-yi39b.png) **Jian 蹇 — Limping** — Gen below; Kan above.[^j05-limping]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4611:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4611:002 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Concerning Jian, the *Sequence of the Hexagrams* says: “Opposition means discord. Discord must bring difficulty; therefore Opposition is followed by Limping. Jian means difficulty.” Estrangement necessarily brings impediments and difficulty, so Limping follows Opposition. Jian means danger and obstruction, hence difficult progress. Kan is above and Gen below: Kan is danger, Gen stopping. Danger ahead brings a halt, preventing advance. With a dangerous pit ahead and a steep obstruction behind, there is Limping.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4611:002 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4611:003 -->

### Limping: the southwest is beneficial; the northeast is not. It is beneficial to see the great person. Steadfast correctness brings good fortune.[^j05-directions]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4611:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4611:004 -->

**Original Meaning.** Jian is difficulty: feet unable to advance, difficulty in walking. Gen below and Kan above mean seeing danger and stopping, hence Limping. The southwest is level and easy; the northeast is dangerous and obstructed, and is also Gen's direction. Already amid difficulty, one should not run into danger. Moreover, the hexagram comes from Small Excess: yang advancing occupies the fifth and attains the center; retreating enters Gen and does not advance. Thus the prognostication favors the southwest, not the northeast.[^j05-jian-transformation] In a time of difficulty, only seeing the great person allows the trouble to be overcome; only preserving correctness brings good fortune. The fifth nine, firm, vigorous, centered, and correct, images the great person. From the second upward, all five lines occupy their correct positions, further expressing steadfast correctness. Hence the benefit in seeing the great person and good fortune through correctness. Seeing danger values the ability to stop, but one must not remain stopped forever. Amid danger, advance can be beneficial, but correctness must not be lost.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4611:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4611:005 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Southwest is Kun's direction. Kun is earth, compliant and easy in character. Northeast is Gen's direction. Gen is mountain, stopping and hazardous. Amid difficulty, it is beneficial to dwell compliantly in easy ground, not to remain in danger. Compliance and ease relieve difficulty; remaining in danger intensifies it. A time of difficulty requires a sage or worthy person capable of relieving the world's troubles, hence the benefit in seeing the great person. Relief must follow a greatly correct Way and preserve it firmly: thus steadfast correctness brings good fortune. Whoever faces difficulty must preserve correctness. Even if the trouble remains unresolved, not losing correct virtue is auspicious. Failing to hold firm and falling into perversity, even if it wins temporary escape, is bad conduct. Those who understand rightness and the mandate of life do not act thus.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4611:005 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4611:006 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4611:006 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4611:007 -->

**Wang Bi:** The southwest is earth; the northeast, mountain. Go to level ground and difficulty is resolved; go into mountains and the way is exhausted.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4611:007 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4611:008 -->

**Fan Zhongyan:** Limping resembles Difficulty at the Beginning. But there, movement within danger allows difficulty to be addressed. Here, stopping within danger means one must not yet confront it.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4611:008 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4611:009 -->

**Gong Huan:** Limping takes its name from seeing danger and being able to stop. Apart from the second and fifth, which correspond to relieve difficulty, the lines all favor stopping rather than going. Yet affairs cannot remain stopped forever. Thus the southwest and seeing the great person are beneficial for overcoming trouble. None of the lines pronounces misfortune or blame.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4611:009 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4611:010 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Earlier scholars explain southwest and northeast through Kun and Gen, making southwest level earth and northeast hazardous mountain. But the wording suggests that southwest means the western and southern directions, northeast the eastern and northern—not the two diagonal points. This meaning begins in Kun and is developed in the Judgments of Limping and Release. Compare their general meanings: Kun favors following rather than leading; Limping favors coming back rather than going forward; Release may allow going forward, yet ultimately finds security in returning. Thus west and south should signify retreating and remaining behind, east and north advancing and taking the lead. Kun can gain companions when behind, but benefits from losing them when ahead. Limping calls for withdrawal behind, not advancement into the lead. These are the two hexagrams' meanings. Once difficulty is released, forward movement may become possible; therefore Release does not forbid the northeast. Yet its favorable southwest means that security ultimately lies in returning to govern oneself. In King Wen's trigram arrangement, yang occupies east and north, yin west and south. Yang leads and advances; yin follows and withdraws. This is the broad division, seemingly not a contrast of dangerous and easy terrain.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4611:010 -->

A firm, centered, correct ruler amid great difficulty needs a minister equally firm, centered, and correct to relieve the world's trouble. The second's centered correctness certainly helps, but yielding assistance cannot accomplish that whole task. Since antiquity, sage kings relieving the world's difficulty have always had sage or worthy ministers: Tang and King Wu had Yi Yin and Lü Shang. Even ordinary rulers have overcome great difficulty through firm, discerning ministers: Liu Shan with Kongming, Tang Suzong with Guo Ziyi, and Dezong with Li Sheng. Even a worthy, discerning ruler cannot overcome difficulty without such ministers. Thus a six in the fifth place with a nine in the second often achieves success through assistance, as in Unformed Understanding and Peace. A nine in the fifth with a six in the second often falls short in achievement, as in Difficulty at the Beginning and Obstruction. A minister surpassing the ruler supplies what the ruler cannot do; one falling short merely assists, and so cannot accomplish great works.

**Hu Bingwen:** The other lines all call going difficult. Yet the sage fears that if everyone refuses to go, there will be no escape from difficulty. If ruler and minister in the fifth and second also refuse to go, who should go? Thus the second says difficulty upon difficulty, and the fifth great difficulty.[^j05-jian-readings]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4611:046 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4611:047 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Only the second and fifth lack language of going and coming. Ruler and minister jointly relieving difficulty cannot disclaim responsibility and have no rightful ground for avoidance. It resembles Retreat, where the other lines withdraw but the second, corresponding with the fifth, remains firmly resolved not to leave. Hu's explanation is correct. No correspondence in the *Changes* is weightier than that of second and fifth. The second's “king's minister” points to the fifth; the fifth's “companions come” points to the second. A subordinate who divines the fifth should consider the state's difficulties and deepen selfless service. A superior who divines the second should recognize the minister's loyalty and steadfastness and broaden the assistance of arriving companions. This is just like Master Zhu's explanation of Qian's second and fifth taking the respective roles of host and guest. Extend it to Unformed Understanding, The Army, and the other hexagrams, and the same applies throughout.[^j05-guest-host]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4611:047 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4611:048 -->

### Top Six: Going brings difficulty; coming brings greatness. Good fortune. It is beneficial to see the great person.[^j05-coming-greatness]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4611:048 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4611:049 -->

**Original Meaning.** Already at the hexagram's limit, one has nowhere further to go and would only increase difficulty. Coming to join the fifth nine and relieving difficulty together brings great achievement. The great person is the fifth nine. The diviner is instructed to act accordingly.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4611:049 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4611:050 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Yielding yin at difficulty's extreme ventures into extreme danger by going: hence difficulty. Instead of going, it comes, following the fifth and seeking the third, gaining firm yang's assistance: hence greatness. Difficulty is confinement and constraint. Greatness means breadth and ease. Coming brings spaciousness and relieves difficulty. At difficulty's extreme, escape becomes possible, but the top six's yielding capacity cannot itself escape; firm assistance can merely ease its trouble. At such an extreme, relief is auspicious. Without firm, centered, correct yang, how could one emerge from difficulty? Seeing the great person means that, at this extreme, meeting someone of great virtue can bring relief. The great person is the fifth: their adjacency supplies this meaning. Firm, centered, correct, and in the ruler's place, it is the great person. Why does the fifth not state an achievement in relieving difficulty, while the top benefits from seeing it? In the fifth's own case it remains inside Kan's danger without firm assistance, so no such achievement is expressed. At the top, difficulty has reached its limit, and encountering great virtue can relieve it: hence benefit. Lines take different meanings, as Difficulty at the Beginning's first nine has correct intention yet is called a raider from the second six's perspective. Why does only the top speak of good fortune? The other lines attain correctness and each has something good, but none has escaped difficulty, so good fortune cannot yet be pronounced. Only the top, at difficulty's extreme, gains spacious relief and is called auspicious.[^j05-jian-final]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4611:050 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4611:051 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4611:051 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4611:052 -->

**Classified Conversations of Master Zhu:** The other lines do not speak of good fortune because they have not left difficulty behind. At the top six, “going brings difficulty; coming brings greatness; good fortune” means that difficulty's extreme contains a possibility of relief.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4611:052 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4611:053 -->

**Xiang Anshi:** The top six originally has nowhere to go; “going” merely means failing to come back. The first six originally has nowhere to come; “coming” merely means refraining from going.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4611:053 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4611:054 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Although a hexagram's top and fifth adjoin, they do not ordinarily imply following one another. The top stands above the fifth and thus images someone outside the affair, unlike the second, third, or fourth. Sometimes it expresses honoring the worthy, but then a fifth six must encounter a top nine, as in Great Possession, Great Restraint, Nourishment, and The Cauldron. Following also makes its fifth nine's encounter with the top six an act of humbling oneself before the worthy, because that hexagram means firmness coming below yieldingness. In general, however, a top six meeting a fifth nine is rarely auspicious and often brings misfortune or humiliation: entanglement with yin burdens firm, centered correctness, as in Great Excess, Influence, Resoluteness, and Joy. Only when the hexagram explicitly favors seeing the great person, and identifies that person with the fifth nine, may the top six return and follow the adjoining fifth. The Judgments of Contention and Xun identify the fifth nine as the great person, but their top nines meet firmness with firmness and do not follow. Pushing Upward's Judgment also speaks of seeing the great person, but it has no fifth nine and therefore uses the distinct wording “use this to see.” Only Limping and Gathering Together identify the fifth nine as the great person encountered by a top six. Yieldingness meeting firmness permits following. Thus Gathering Together's top cries and sighs, seeking union with the fifth without blame; Limping's top comes to join the fifth and attains good fortune. Limping's top surpasses Gathering Together's because gathering at its extreme disperses, while difficulty at its extreme is released. Apart from Qian's second and fifth, this alone among the line statements says, “It is beneficial to see the great person.”[^j05-final-comparison]

## Translator's Endnotes

The base is the repository's unchanged [易學網 transcription of Juan Five](../source/juan-05.md). “Parallel text” below means the [*Siku Quanshu* transcription, fixed Wikisource revision 759589](https://zh.wikisource.org/w/index.php?title=御纂周易折中_%28四庫全書本%29/卷05&oldid=759589), consulted selectively for doubtful passages. Comparison of two transcriptions is not collation with a printed or manuscript facsimile. Corrections, retained variants, and interpretive decisions are distinguished below; the parallel text is not assumed error-free. The added Yang Wenhuan gloss is explicitly supplementary. The original compilation's Editorial Judgments are not translator's notes.

[^j05-influence]: **咸, influence and response.** The title is retained as *Influence*, but the commentaries repeatedly pair 感, affecting or stirring, with 應, responding. The relationship is reciprocal, not simply one person exerting power over another. “Attraction,” “influence,” and “response” therefore vary with context. The body's successive parts make partial, localized responses visible; the heart is discussed as their governing source. No generic modern psychology has been substituted for that bodily and cosmological account.

[^j05-lower-classic]: **The beginning of the Lower Classic.** Juan Five begins with Hexagram 31, after Clinging closes the Upper Classic. Cheng's opening quotation lays out the traditional account of human relationships beginning in the union of male and female. His ensuing contrast is between the two separate hexagrams Qian and Kun, and the paired trigrams within each of Influence and Constancy. The statement about deference, movement, and universal line correspondence refers to Constancy; it is not an unnoticed change in Influence's trigram structure.

[^j05-marriage-order]: **Courtship and household hierarchy.** The source distinguishes 男下女, the man placing himself below the woman in courtship, from 男尊女卑, the hierarchical ordering of married life. Gen and Dui are the youngest son and daughter; Zhen and Xun the eldest. The argument depends on these traditional family attributes of the trigrams. The historical hierarchy and gendered prescriptions are translated as the commentators' own, rather than replaced by a modern model of partnership or presented as the translator's advice.

[^j05-xian-readings]: **Corrections in Influence.** The base's 男先子女 in the *Original Meaning* is read 男先於女, supported by the parallel text: “the man takes the initiative before the woman,” not a reference to children. In Cheng's long fourth-line quotation, base 以祟德也 is read 以崇德也, likewise supported: elevating virtue. These are character corrections, not changes to the interpretation. Ordinary variant forms such as 說 for 悅 in the sense of delight are translated without modernizing the Chinese source.

[^j05-uncontrived]: **“Without a calculating mind.”** Hu Bingwen's 無心之感 exploits the graphic difference between 咸 and 感, the latter containing 心, heart-mind. His point is influence unburdened by private calculation, not unconsciousness or an absence of feeling. The following commentaries explain this ethical wordplay. It is not presented here as a proven account of the characters' historical formation.

[^j05-still-response]: **Stillness is not refusal to respond.** The recurrent opposition is between receptivity and self-interested initiation, not between practical action and total inactivity. 寂然不動 and 感而遂通 name the heart's still constitution and responsive activity. 鑑空衡平 images a mirror free to receive appearances and a balance remaining level. “Master Zhou” is Zhou Dunyi; his primacy of stillness is cited alongside the Cheng and Zhu formulations, not treated as an instruction to suppress every thought.

[^j05-calf]: **The moving calf.** The anatomical description serves the ethical image of movement beginning prematurely, before an appropriate summons or occasion. It is translated as the source's account, not endorsed as a modern account of gait. “Remaining still” can mean staying in one's station and preserving one's principles. It is not necessarily remaining physically motionless.

[^j05-thigh-following]: **Whom does the thigh follow?** Zhu Xi relates the third to the two lower lines; Cheng relates it to the top; the compilers favor the adjoining fourth as heart. The reference to Keeping Still is their comparison, not a uniform rule that the heart always belongs to the same line. Their closing description of the third as 不中正 is retained as a judgment of its virtue. The third is formally a yang line in an odd position; equating every use of 正 with positional parity would make the discussion needlessly contradictory.

[^j05-heart-and-thought]: **Heart-mind and deliberation.** 心 includes thought, intention, and feeling; “heart” is used consistently rather than severing these into modern faculties. The long quotation and exposition are from the *Appended Statements* and concern reciprocal contraction and extension. 信 there is read in the sense of 伸, extending, not “trust.” 何思何慮 does not ban reflection: Zhu explicitly says that rightness and principle must be considered. The rejected activity is anxious, private calculation designed to compel a return. “The numinous” renders 神 without importing a separate supernatural being into each occurrence.

[^j05-companions]: **Response limited by one's own circle.** 朋 here is not an unconditional commendation of friendship. The commentators read “companions follow your thoughts” as the narrowing of influence to those already reached by one's private intentions. Lin's claim that non-calculating influence leaves no one outside its reach belongs to his normative account of impartial responsiveness. It should not be silently recast as a guaranteed technique for obtaining universal agreement.

[^j05-back]: **脢 and the heart's back.** The precise anatomical location varies even among the authorities quoted. All place it on the back near or above the heart. Zhu reads relative insensitivity without private attachment; Cheng reads turning away from private inclination to achieve broader influence. These are distinct explanations of “no regret,” not interchangeable descriptions of one anatomy.

[^j05-mouth]: **Words without substance.** The top line's fault is not speech itself but persuasive display lacking sincere substance. Cheng's comparison to petty people and women is his historical generalization and has not been softened or adopted as the translator's judgment. 郭忠孝's concluding contrast with Keeping Still refers to its fifth line, 艮其輔，言有序: regulating the jaw so that speech has order.

[^j05-constancy]: **Endurance without rigidity.** 恒 combines lastingness with regularity. Cheng expressly rejects 守一隅而不知變, holding to one corner without understanding change. Xu Ji's distinction between 不易, not changing, and 不已, not ceasing, is essential: constancy preserves the proper Way through continued, responsive activity. The English therefore distinguishes constancy from the stubborn persistence criticized in particular lines.

[^j05-heng-graph]: **The ancient graph.** The base prints 𢛢 and preserves Zhu's explanation of a boat spanning two banks. The parallel transcription has an unrendered or missing graph at this point. The base's character is retained. Zhu's explanation is translated as a reported graphic interpretation, not offered as an independently established paleographic derivation. “Not simply ordinary or usual” distinguishes the strong continuity of 恒 from the weaker everyday sense of 尋常.

[^j05-depth]: **浚恒: two accounts of demanding too much.** Zhu and Cheng focus on demanding deep, constant attachment from a correspondent who cannot supply it under present conditions. The later commentators and compilers instead emphasize seeking mature results before allowing time for gradual effort. Neither is reduced to a generic warning against commitment. In this line, persistence can itself be the problem; rendering every 貞 as an unconditional instruction to persevere would reverse the warning.

[^j05-centered-correct]: **Centeredness and correctness.** 中 frequently concerns the second and fifth positions, the centers of the two trigrams. Formal positional correctness places yang in odd positions and yin in even ones. Ethical correctness is not identical with that arrangement. Cheng here explicitly values centeredness over positional correctness. Great Strength later develops the distinction at length: a technically misplaced line may act appropriately, while a correctly placed one may use excessive force.

[^j05-heng-readings]: **Corrections in Constancy.** At the fourth line, the base's 胡氏媛 is read 胡氏瑗, Hu Yuan, with the parallel text. At the fifth, 故恒久其德剛為貞也 is read with the parallel's 則 rather than 剛: lasting in this virtue “therefore constitutes steadfast correctness,” not an unexplained attribution of firmness to yielding obedience. These corrections do not remove the gendered argument that follows.

[^j05-hunting]: **Effort without a fitting method.** The fourth line does not condemn long effort; it condemns persisting in the wrong place or by the wrong method. The compilers' reference to Han Yu fishing with Master Hou illustrates wasted effort when one's means do not fit the intended end. No precise poem title is supplied by the base, and none has been conjecturally inserted into the translation.

[^j05-heng-woman]: **婦人 and 夫子.** Here 夫子 means a man or husband, not “the Master” as a title for Confucius. The commentators apply the same image differently according to the consulter's expected social role. Their opposition between a woman's constant compliance and a man's obligation to judge independently belongs to the historical text. Zhu's further distinction between image and prognostication is preserved: the line presents a configuration; its application depends on the consulter, rather than an abstract line dispensing fortune automatically.

[^j05-shaking]: **An unresolved illustration.** Both consulted transcriptions read 如振衣，如振書, literally shaking a garment and shaking a book. The second example is unusual, but neither witness supplies a corrected reading, so it has been retained rather than silently replaced with a plausible object. Cheng's following 抖擻運動 makes the general meaning clear: rapid, repeated agitation. This lexical uncertainty does not obscure the contrast between settled constancy and continual disturbance.

[^j05-dun-readings]: **Corrections and a retained distinction in Retreat.** The opening base 有卜陵之象 is read 有上陵之象 with the parallel text: pressing or rising upward. In the further fourth-line Editorial Judgment, base 履敗 is read 覆敗, ruin or defeat, as in the parallel. In the second-line commentary, however, the odd explanatory move from “cannot loosen” to “cannot fully describe” is shared by both witnesses and has not been normalized away; see the separate note on 說.

[^j05-small-benefit]: **小利貞.** Zhu's formal commentary makes 小 the small or petty people, while Cheng understands limited rectification still possible before the situation becomes complete Obstruction. The recorded conversation acknowledges that the Classic itself provides no clear precedent for Zhu's particular construction. The main oracle follows “a little benefit,” while both commentarial readings remain. Retreat's limited opportunity should not be conflated with the more categorical warning in Obstruction.

[^j05-months]: **Months in the growth-and-decline sequence.** Retreat is assigned to the sixth month and Great Strength to the second in the traditional sequence correlating hexagrams with the waxing and waning of yin and yang. These are conventional calendrical correspondences, not claims that a consultation must concern June or February in the modern civil calendar. The translation preserves the source's month numbers without converting them into Gregorian dates.

[^j05-tail]: **Going out, going forward, and remaining hidden.** Retreat reverses the usual assumption that the first line is first in progress: the innermost place is last to get away. The injunction 勿用有攸往 is accordingly disputed. Cheng can read it as avoiding a dangerous departure once late; Zhu's recorded conversation objects that danger may make departure necessary. Wang Shenzi distinguishes refusing forward involvement from refusing retreat. The translation retains these tensions rather than turning the line into a universally applicable instruction to remain in place.

[^j05-oxhide]: **What is bound?** The Chinese 執之 leaves the object open. Zhu makes the unbreakable bond a resolve to retreat. Cheng, Wu Qi, and Gong Huan make it a binding relationship that prevents rightful departure. Kong Yingda offers a third account: a person within the situation can retain those otherwise departing by centered harmony and generous compliance. The main statement therefore says “bind it,” without inserting one commentator's chosen object. Yellow, ox, and hide are then interpreted through centeredness, compliance, and firmness respectively.

[^j05-oxhide-reading]: **說: loosening or speaking.** In the oracle 說 is normally understood as 脫, loosening or release. Cheng's quoted explanation instead says the bond is so firm that it is 不可勝言, beyond full description. Both transcriptions preserve this wording. The translation reports that explanatory shift rather than pretending that Cheng and Zhu offer the same grammatical reading.

[^j05-dependents]: **臣妾.** In this domestic context, “male and female dependents” preserves a historical category of subordinate household persons, which can include servile status; it is not a modern employment relationship. Cheng also associates it with 小人女子, petty people and women, in his account of affection and obligation. The several interpretations concern managing unavoidable ties, not a translator's recommendation to acquire or dominate dependents.

[^j05-liu-bei]: **The First Ruler of Shu.** 蜀先主 refers to Liu Bei. Cheng invokes his unwillingness to abandon officers and people as a counterexample to attachment arising merely from private fondness: a tie grounded in rightness can be dangerous without being blameworthy. The example is retained in its limited function here; it does not establish that every costly attachment is ethically required.

[^j05-affectionate-retreat]: **好遯 and 否.** 好 may name what one loves, yielding the reading “withdrawal despite affection.” The compilers instead emphasize the opposite of hatred: an unresentful, composed departure. Their further discussion reads 否 not merely as “not so” but as the name or condition of Obstruction for those who drive the worthy away. The oracle's “retreat amid affection” leaves room for the first two readings; the third is explicit in the Editorial Judgment. These are not three interchangeable English synonyms.

[^j05-spacious]: **肥 is not literal bodily fatness here.** Both major commentaries explain 肥 through breadth, fullness, ease, and freedom from entanglement. “Spacious retreat” conveys that amplitude without replacing the image by a purely modern ideal of leisure. Wang Bi's corded hunting arrows extend the figure of escaping capture. Jiang Bao is 姜寶 as printed in the source, not Gan Bao 干寶, who appears later in the juan.

[^j05-fu-certainty]: **有孚 as certainty.** In Great Strength's first line, the commentators explicitly gloss 孚 as the certainty of the warning: forceful advance from below is sure to bring misfortune. Elsewhere it can mean sincerity or trustworthiness. “This is certain” here preserves that contextual difference; it is not a promise that the consulter possesses a protective virtue overriding the adverse prognostication.

[^j05-zhuang-readings]: **Two corrections in Great Strength.** In Wang Bi's second-line gloss, base 貞占 is read 貞吉, supported by the parallel: correctness and good fortune, not “correct divination.” In Wang Shenzi's first-line explanation, base 況下下乎 is read 況在下乎: “how much more below.” No alteration is made to the source file. The fifth-line claim that its station is 正 belongs to Cheng's discussion of rank and governing function; it should not be confused with a claim that yin in the fifth is formally correct.

[^j05-disregard]: **用罔: the major dispute.** Zhu and Cheng understand 罔 as treating something as nothing: the powerful noble or high-ranking person disregards obstacles and becomes recklessly courageous. Jing Fang and the following commentators read it negatively: the noble person does not use strength. The compilers prefer this second reading. The main oracle retains the first to remain consistent with the base's leading commentary, while the full alternative is translated. Cheng explicitly explains 君子 and 小人 here partly through rank, preventing the supposedly “noble” figure's recklessness from becoming an unnoticed contradiction.

[^j05-wagon]: **輹 and 輻.** “Axle-support” gives 輹 a mechanical function distinct from the wheel's spokes, 輻. Cheng equates the terms in this passage; the translation preserves his equation but does not make it the sole technical identification. See the discussions of Small Restraint and Great Restraint in [Juan Two](juan-02.md#eee-4554) and [Juan Four](juan-04.md#eee-4580). The significant contrast here is between destructive butting against a barrier and supported movement after the barrier opens.

[^j05-lost-sheep]: **易: ease or a field boundary.** The *Original Meaning* leads with losing the sheep unawares or with ease, while permitting 埸, a field boundary. The recorded conversation says the boundary reading is preferable and admits that the written commentary still retains the earlier explanation. Both are preserved. The claim about the *History of the Han*'s character usage is Zhu's cited evidence, not an independent modern emendation by the translator. The oracle follows the base's leading reading and is not silently rewritten in light of the later conversation.

[^j05-ram-difficulty]: **艱則吉.** This can describe hardship itself bringing a return to yieldingness, or recognizing difficulty and responding cautiously. The commentaries articulate both. The main wording follows the compilers' final explanation, 知其難而不敢輕易以處之. Good fortune is conditional on a changed way of dealing with the predicament, not a claim that being immobilized is inherently beneficial. The top's horn-image is supplied by the hexagram's shape even though the line is yin.

[^j05-jin-virtues]: **A difficult introductory passage.** Cheng's 卦有有德者，有无德者 concerns whether the hexagram's statement explicitly names auspicious qualities, not whether a hexagram literally possesses no virtue. The following references to 元亨 and 利貞 guide the translation. The base and parallel both preserve a compressed and awkward passage, including 无用有也. “It has no need to do so” is an interpretive construal, not a recovered variant. The point is that Advance's radiance and compliance already convey success and correctness without requiring their explicit repetition.

[^j05-peace-lord]: **康侯.** The commentators explain this as 安國之侯 or 治安之侯, a lord whose governance secures his state. The translation therefore uses “the lord who brings peace,” not a personal name. 錫 marks a grant or bestowal; 蕃庶 indicates abundance. 晝日三接 describes repeated, conspicuous reception within one day. The line retains the concrete institutions of rank, royal gifts, and audience rather than converting them into modern career terminology.

[^j05-jin-transformation]: **Advance from Viewing.** The line comparison exchanges Viewing's fourth yin and fifth yang, giving Advance's fourth yang and fifth yin. Zhu foregrounds the yielding fourth advancing into the fifth. This is a traditional explanatory transformation of hexagram structure, not by itself a direction to change a particular line in a consultation. The three grounds of the prognostication are the solar image, the paired trigram virtues, and the rising yielding line.

[^j05-patience]: **裕.** Breadth, amplitude, and unhurried composure are all involved. “Spacious patience” is not resignation to indefinite mistreatment, but the refusal to sacrifice principle either through anxious self-promotion or indignant withdrawal. Wang Anshi's references to Confucius awaiting a buyer and Mencius lingering in Qi illustrate patience on the two sides of advancing and leaving; the imagery is retained as the source presents it.

[^j05-royal-ancestress]: **王母 and ancestral generations.** Cheng explicitly reads 祖母, grandmother, while Zhu relates the prognostication to sacrifice for a deceased female ancestor. The term here is not identified with the Queen Mother of the West. The compilers explain yin responding to yin through the ritual pairing of a grandson's wife with her husband's grandmother in the alternating zhao–mu generations. This is why “royal ancestress” rather than an unqualified “queen mother” is used. Their interpretation differs from reading the fifth solely as a living ruler.

[^j05-rat]: **鼫鼠.** “Great rat” retains a conventional non-specific rendering. The text's immediate concern is the combination of acquisitiveness, fear, and insecure possession of a high place, not zoological identification. No particular modern species is asserted. The image is not diluted to the abstract phrase “an anxious official,” since its animal character matters to the contrast with the peace-bringing lord.

[^j05-jin-readings]: **A correction and a syntactic decision.** In Zhu's discussion of the top line, base 面言其合下 is read 而言其合下 with the parallel text; 合下 means the condition present from the outset, not something physically below. In Cheng's fourth-line commentary, the words about sharing virtue with the superior and complying upward are construed as referring to the three yin below. The transmitted wording is awkward and does not explicitly supply that subject; the English makes it explicit from context. This is a syntactic clarification, not an attested insertion into the Chinese.

[^j05-loss-gain]: **Letting go of anxious calculation.** 勿恤 does not abolish responsibility for outcomes. Zhu rejects calculating achievement and profit privately; Cheng contrasts proper delegation with intrusive personal scrutiny. The compilers then apply the fifth to the peace-bringing lord rather than insist that it must describe the sovereign. That change of referent is explicit, and no uniform “ruler” has been imposed on all explanations of the fifth position.

[^j05-chastise-domain]: **伐邑: outward image, inward application.** Zhu allows chastisement of a private domain; Cheng makes it inward self-government. The compilers give concrete intermediate examples: disciplining one's own household and preventing improper use of official connections or authority. The severe military verb is retained, but the commentaries do not present this as license for indiscriminate outward aggression. Whether 貞吝 criticizes persistence in forceful advancement or the imperfect quality of severe self-correction remains a dispute within the source.

[^j05-darkened-light]: **夷 as injury.** The familiar title “Darkening of the Light” is retained, while the exposition regularly says that illumination is injured. This keeps visible the source's distinction between clear understanding concealed under hostile conditions and actual benightedness. It is principally the top line that lacks illumination; the other lines do not become morally dark merely because they must obscure themselves outwardly.

[^j05-drooping-wings]: **Injured wings or folded wings?** Zhu and Cheng initially read a bird's impaired flight; Xiang Anshi and others read deliberate lowering or folding of the wings before injury becomes manifest. Both versions are fully translated. The three days without food underscore urgency and hardship in withdrawal; they are not made into a fasting prescription. The host's criticism expresses the difficulty of explaining timely departure to those who have not recognized the danger.

[^j05-early-withdrawal]: **Cheng's historical examples.** Xue Fang and Yang Xiong contrast timely avoidance with failure to leave; Master Mu's departure from Chu illustrates recognizing danger before others do; Yuan Hong illustrates withdrawal before persecution becomes apparent. The anecdotes are translated in the form Cheng supplies, without adding dates or claiming independent verification of every detail. “Masters Shen and Bai” separates the names that the base's punctuation awkwardly joins to the preceding mention of Chu.

[^j05-left-thigh]: **Injury and rescue.** Cheng's discussion of the left thigh, the right side, and bracing a crossbow explains his bodily image of an injury that impairs movement without yet being decisive. It is the source's functional anatomy, not medical guidance. The strong horse supplies an effective means of rescue; it has not been replaced by a modern abstraction such as “resources.”

[^j05-rescue-scope]: **Whose rescue?** Zhu and Cheng primarily explain rapid escape from injury. The compilers broaden the second line's responsibility: as the governing line of illumination, it should urgently seek to remedy darkness. Their comparison with [Abundance in Chinese Juan Seven](../source/juan-07.md#eee-4627) distinguishes a still-remediable injury from the broken right arm. This editorial intervention is substantial and is not merged into the earlier accounts of self-preservation.

[^j05-southern-hunt]: **南狩 and 不可疾貞.** The south supplies the direction of light; the hunt becomes removal of harmful forces. 疾 means haste here. Zhu warns broadly against precipitous action; Cheng especially warns against abruptly reforming ingrained habits after removing the chief offender. “Do not press rectification too quickly” preserves that temporal distinction. The *Announcement on Drunkenness* is invoked by Cheng to distinguish removing leading wrongdoers from patient reform of wider customs.

[^j05-mingyi-readings]: **Corrections in Darkening of the Light.** The third-line *Original Meaning* reads 成湯赴於夏臺 in the base; the parallel has 起, giving Tang's rising or emergence from Xia Terrace. The fourth-line *Original Meaning* has 按君 in the base and 闇君 in the parallel: “benighted ruler.” In Cheng's third-line passage, 克至暗之相 is read with the parallel's 象, “image.” These corrections are adopted in English while the Chinese base remains unchanged.

[^j05-left-belly]: **A genuine divergence over the fourth line.** Zhu begins by admitting that its meaning is unclear, then understands hidden withdrawal. Cheng instead makes the fourth an insinuating minister and explicitly identifies the fifth as the dark ruler. Elsewhere he assigns that role to the top. The translation preserves this local disagreement and shift of referent; it does not silently repair it into Zhu's arrangement. Yang Shi and Hu Bingwen associate the fourth with Weizi's departure. “Obtain the heart” therefore ranges from grasping the appropriate course to capturing another person's confidence.

[^j05-jizi]: **Concealment without inward surrender.** Jizi is the Shang royal kinsman and minister named by the source; Zhou here is the last Shang ruler, not the Zhou dynasty. Cheng recounts feigned madness and servile status as means of preserving inward correctness under a dangerous ruler. The phrase 內難 concerns peril within close political and familial relations, not merely an inward psychological conflict. Jizi's imposed obscurity must remain distinct from the top line's actual loss of illumination.

[^j05-family]: **家人 and the historical household.** The title means the persons of a household, and the commentary includes ancestry, marriage, children, dependents, property, and governance. “Family” is retained as readable English, but a modern nuclear household is not an adequate equivalent. The text's gendered allocation of duties is translated without alteration. Its prescriptions are the historical authors' arguments, not an endorsement of those arrangements by the translator.

[^j05-family-wind]: **Wind issuing from fire.** The image supports influence moving from inner cultivation through household life to the wider polity. Cheng rejects treating 巽 itself as “ordering” merely because the trigram sequence says 齊乎巽. His comparison with 戰乎乾 makes the grammatical point: something happening at a trigram's place does not automatically become that trigram's inherent meaning. The disputed explanation attributed to Master Wenzhong is accordingly retained and distinguished from Cheng's own.

[^j05-family-safeguards]: **閑.** The word here means enclosing, guarding, or setting bounds, not leisure or idleness. The commentators emphasize establishing standards before disorder develops. Their account of household regulation includes hierarchical assumptions that the translation preserves, but it has not been expanded into instructions for punishment. “Regret disappears” also differs from claiming that a household can never occasion regret.

[^j05-inner-provision]: **无攸遂 and 中饋.** The first expression is explained as not independently insisting on carrying an undertaking through. The second concerns provisioning within the household, including food for ritual offerings. The translation therefore avoids both “achieve nothing” and an exclusively secular “cook the meals.” Cheng's application to an unfit household governor and his subsequent application to the woman's role are separate steps in his argument, not an accidental contradiction removed by paraphrase.

[^j05-severity]: **嗃嗃 and 嘻嘻.** Cheng explicitly says that the exact lexical value of 嗃嗃 is uncertain; he hears clamorous cries and severe constraint. 嘻嘻 is contrasting unmeasured merriment. The translation preserves both sounds' social implications without treating ordinary laughter as inherently culpable. Zhu distinguishes two possible prognostications according to the consulter's conduct; Cheng treats the lax alternative as a hypothetical warning. The notes do not erase the source's preference for excessive strictness over indulgence.

[^j05-household-wealth]: **Teaching and nourishing.** The compilers take the fourth, normally read as a minister's place, as another expression of the wife's role. Their 教父 and 食母 borrow Laozi's paternal and maternal language to distinguish teaching from nourishment. These labels are translated as part of their interpretation, not used to impose one unchanging assignment of persons to line positions throughout the book. The progression from the second's provisions to the fourth's household wealth is explicit.

[^j05-reaching-family]: **假: arriving and reaching the heart.** Zhu and Cheng begin with 至, arrival or attainment; the collected explanations develop 感格, inwardly affecting or reaching others. “The king reaches his household” keeps the connection between arrival and effective moral influence. It does not mean that he merely returns to a building. The compilers favor the latter group of explanations, especially He Kai's account of personal example restoring proper relationships.

[^j05-family-supplement]: **Supplementary gloss absent from the base.** After Cheng's fifth-line commentary, the parallel text has a Collected Explanations heading and this gloss by Yang Wenhuan: 閑有家閑之於其始，假有家則假之於其終也. Translation: “Safeguarding the household safeguards it at the beginning; reaching the household reaches it at the end.” The base proceeds directly to Qiu Fuguo and omits both this gloss and the heading. Yang's words are supplied here as a clearly marked supplement, not inserted into the source-aligned main translation or counted as an additional base block.

[^j05-family-trust]: **Sincerity and authority.** 孚 and 威 are kept distinct: sincere trustworthiness and a dignity that commands respect. Neither is reduced to the other. He Kai's concluding gloss locates household government in the governor's own conduct, linking the line to the *Great Image*'s substantive speech and constant action. This is the source's own qualification of an account otherwise strongly concerned with hierarchy and discipline.

[^j05-family-readings]: **忘 rather than 忌.** In Cheng's top-line commentary, the base reads 少忌恭順; the parallel reads 少忘恭順. The latter is adopted: the young “forget respectful compliance.” This restores the balanced opposition with elders losing dignity and avoids an unsupported reading in which the young are being told to fear compliance.

[^j05-opposition]: **Difference, estrangement, and reunion.** 睽 is retained as *Opposition*, but the exposition also requires “estrangement,” “discord,” and “difference.” The middle and youngest daughters' distinct marital destinations explain 志不同歸; it does not say that they simply dislike one another. The chapter continually distinguishes recognizing difference from aggravating separation through suspicion or coercion.

[^j05-small-affairs]: **小事吉.** Zhu initially limits the scope of suitable undertakings; He Kai explicitly argues instead for a yielding, measured manner of action. The compilers support that second explanation through the lane, the ill-disposed person, and tender flesh. “Good fortune in small affairs” retains the compact oracle while the commentary shows why “small” cannot automatically be treated as a numerical or financial limit on a consultation.

[^j05-kui-transformations]: **Three explanatory origins.** The comparisons exchange Clinging's second and third lines, Inner Sincerity's fourth and fifth, or both pairs in The Family, producing Opposition. They explain yieldingness advancing into the third or fifth. These structural accounts are distinct from selecting changing lines in divination. The source gives multiple origins rather than one exclusive historical derivation.

[^j05-lost-horse]: **Meeting without chasing.** The horse is the means of movement and, in Cheng's reading, the restored connection with the fourth line. 惡人 is kept as “ill-disposed person” rather than weakened to mere difference, but Cheng himself explains it as someone at odds with oneself. Xiang Anshi distinguishes receiving an encounter from actively seeking it. Confucius and Yang Huo provide the source's example of guarded contact without surrender of principle; “no blame” is not blanket approval of the other person's conduct.

[^j05-lane]: **An indirect approach is not moral compromise.** Cheng expressly excludes 枉己屈道, bending oneself and compromising the Way. The compilers contrast formal 會 with less ceremonious 遇 and explain the lane near the palace wall as modest, discreet access. Neither the concrete lane nor the institutional setting has been replaced by modern “networking.” The ethical limit belongs in the reading as much as the practical flexibility.

[^j05-shorn-nose]: **天, 髡, and 劓.** The oracle's 天 is not translated “Heaven” here because the commentaries read it as shaving the head. Hu Yuan proposes 而 as an older form connected with shorn temple hair. This is Hu's transmitted textual proposal, not a correction independently established by the translator. 劓 is cutting off the nose; the punitive imagery is retained. The separate pronunciation gloss on 髡 belongs to the online base edition and is labeled as such, rather than attributed to Zhu or the imperial compilers. “No beginning, but an end” signifies an initially frustrated relation ultimately completed, not the absence of all causation.

[^j05-kui-punctuation]: **A changed sentence division.** In Cheng's third-line explanation, the base punctuates 三不合於二，與四睽之時. The translation instead groups 二與四 together: the third does not unite with the second or fourth. The unpunctuated parallel supports that grouping, which accords with the preceding two obstructions. This is repunctuation, not a substituted character. Zhu attributes the additional suspicion to the top, while Cheng locates the bodily injury in conflict with the fourth; their explanations remain different.

[^j05-good-man]: **元夫.** Cheng reads 元 as goodness and identifies the figure with the first nine's excellent conduct; Kong Yingda emphasizes its initial position. “Good man” follows the former without implying that the same expression must mean “husband” everywhere. The first and fourth are corresponding positions occupied by two yang, not an ordinary yin-yang pair. Their meeting through shared virtue is precisely what the commentators find distinctive in a time of Opposition.

[^j05-impartial-meeting]: **Connection without faction.** The compilers do not treat every instance of solitude as a defect. A high minister without a private faction can be acting appropriately. Their example of Zhuge Liang and Fa Zheng concerns association through public duty despite differing inclinations. It qualifies the account of meeting the good man: mutual trust need not become personal partisanship.

[^j05-biting-skin]: **宗 and 膚.** 宗 is here explained through the corresponding second line: an associate, companion, or person relied upon. “Companion” avoids falsely requiring literal kinship. 膚 retains the image of tender flesh or skin, familiar from Biting Through. Zhu and the compilers emphasize ease of reunion; Cheng emphasizes penetration sufficient to bring two people together. The image is not a direction to bite another person. Gong Huan's contrast with Fellowship makes the governing circumstance decisive: a private pairing can be too narrow there but restorative here.

[^j05-kui-image]: **The Biting Through comparison.** Changing Opposition's second line alone produces Biting Through; this structural comparison is directly verifiable from the figures. The further report of its image from the second to the top is a separate, compressed image-reading, not a claim that five positions are literally a six-line hexagram. The source's alternative has been preserved without supplying a speculative reconstruction.

[^j05-ghost-wagon]: **Suspicion creates the apparition.** The commentaries explicitly explain the mud-covered pig and wagon of ghosts as the top line's hostile misperception of its proper partner. The rain marks suspicion dissolving and yin and yang reuniting. The translation retains the strange images but does not turn them into an independent claim of supernatural observation. “Marriage partner” preserves 婚媾 and its relation to the corresponding line; it is not reduced to an unspecified business alliance.

[^j05-kui-readings]: **Corrections and a preferred base reading.** In Feng Dangke's General Discussion, base 對卦 is read 外卦 with the parallel: the outer trigram, contrasted with the inner. In Cheng's top-line commentary, base 改云 is read 故云, “hence it says.” The parallel itself has apparent slips elsewhere, including 至識 where the base's 至誠 fits the fourth-line argument about perfect sincerity. The English follows the intelligible base there rather than treating the parallel as uniformly authoritative.

[^j05-limping]: **蹇.** The title *Limping* continues the terminology established in Juan One and keeps the bodily sense that Zhu explicitly gives: feet unable to advance. “Difficulty” and “impediment” are used through the exposition where English requires them. This distinguishes 蹇 from 否, already translated *Obstruction*, and from 屯, difficulty at the beginning. The title is a traditional image of impeded movement, not a diagnosis of the consulter.

[^j05-directions]: **Southwest and northeast: two systems within the text.** Zhu and Cheng read the two diagonal directions through Kun and Gen, associating them with easy and hazardous terrain. The compilers instead separate them into west and south versus east and north, then interpret them through withdrawal and advance. Their objection to the earlier explanation is explicit. The translation retains the oracle's conventional directional wording and lets the commentary distinguish these accounts; it does not quietly combine them into a single map or travel recommendation.

[^j05-jian-transformation]: **From Small Excess.** Moving Small Excess's fourth yang into the fifth, exchanging it with the fifth yin, produces Limping. Zhu contrasts yang's advancement into the center with retreat into Gen's stopping. His account is explanatory symbolism, not a blanket command to move forward whenever the fifth appears. The following clauses insist both on the ability to stop and on not remaining stopped forever.

[^j05-going-coming]: **往 and 來.** Going commonly means advancing outward or upward; coming commonly means returning inward or downward. Here Zhu's recorded conversation prefers Cheng's broader contrast between advance and refraining from advance, since the lowest line has no lower place to reach. Yet other passages in the same compilation specify particular lines as destinations. Those local explanations remain visible. Translating every 來 as a literal downward move would falsely settle a question the source continues to debate.

[^j05-minister-duty]: **匪躬之故.** The phrase denies acting for one's own person or private interest. It does not mean that the minister's bodily condition caused no part of the difficulty. Zhu refuses to make success or failure the measure of this line's duty. Han Yu's comparison with Decay distinguishes rightly refusing service from neglecting a responsibility already one's own. This is the text's account of role-bound obligation, not a general demand that readers disregard personal welfare in every situation.

[^j05-jian-second-capacity]: **A limit within Cheng's praise.** Cheng praises loyalty while judging the yielding minister insufficient to resolve the whole crisis. The following 小可濟 is shared by the two consulted transcriptions and is rendered conditionally: were even limited relief attainable, the sage would commend it. This difficult compression has not been silently expanded into a claim that success is guaranteed. Zhu's account, by contrast, keeps the prognostication focused on wholehearted effort rather than capacity sufficient for a favorable result.

[^j05-jian-return]: **Return to companions or to one's station.** Zhu and Cheng read the third as returning to the two yin below; Kong Yingda and Wu Yueshen read it as resting in its appropriate position. The compilers favor the former because the third governs the inner trigram. The oracle's concise “coming is a return” preserves 反 without preempting that disagreement.

[^j05-jian-connection]: **Whom does the fourth join?** Zhu joins it to the third; Cheng extends the connection to those below; Xun Shuang instead reads returning to support the fifth. The compilers strongly prefer Xun, citing both the usual fourth–fifth relationship and this hexagram's favorable encounter with the great person. This reverses the direction assumed by a simple rule that “coming” must always mean joining a lower line.

[^j05-great-difficulty]: **The difficulty of the whole.** The fifth's difficulty is “great” because it bears the ruler's responsibility for the entire situation, not merely one minister's assignment. Cheng then questions whether the second's assistance is sufficient, while Zhu promises help to a consulter possessing the fifth's virtue. The historical ruler–minister examples are retained as Cheng's argument about complementary capacities; the translation does not supply modern rankings or an independent historical verdict on those figures.

[^j05-jian-readings]: **Two consequential corrections.** In the fifth-line *Original Meaning*, the base's 古者有是德 is read 占者有是德 with the parallel: “a diviner possessing this virtue,” not “the ancients possessing this virtue.” In Hu Bingwen's following gloss, 復不住 is read 復不往, again supported by the parallel: if ruler and minister also refuse to go, who will go? This restores the explicit contrast with the other lines' warnings against advance.

[^j05-guest-host]: **The consulter need not occupy the line's apparent social rank.** The compilers explicitly allow a subordinate receiving the fifth to consider the ruler's difficulty, and a superior receiving the second to recognize the minister's loyalty. Their comparison with Qian's second and fifth invokes the reciprocal host–guest relation discussed in [Juan One](juan-01.md#eee-4542). The image's person and the person consulting the oracle need not be identical. This is a practical interpretive rule in the source, not a translator's modernization.

[^j05-coming-greatness]: **碩.** Zhu reads great achievement from joining the fifth. Cheng emphasizes enlargement, ease, and relief from confinement, while also mentioning both the fifth and third as sources of assistance. “Coming brings greatness” preserves the shared term without prematurely choosing between achievement and spacious relief. Good fortune first appears explicitly among the line statements here, at difficulty's extreme; that is a textual observation, not a promise that every prolonged hardship resolves itself without action.

[^j05-jian-final]: **Different perspectives, not one fixed prediction.** Cheng explains why the fifth can be insufficient to solve its own difficulty yet beneficial when encountered from the top. He expressly compares the differing uses of Difficulty at the Beginning's first line. His broad statement that the other lines attain correctness should not be pressed into an exact parity count: the first six is formally misplaced, whereas the second through top are correctly placed, as Zhu specifies in the opening. No character correction is established for Cheng's generalization, and it is retained.

[^j05-final-comparison]: **The final comparison of fifth and top.** The compilers distinguish a fifth six honoring a top nine from a fifth nine associating with a top six. Their exceptions depend on the particular hexagram, especially Following, Limping, and Gathering Together. The analysis should therefore not become a universal prohibition on same-polarity relations or a universal rule that the top follows the fifth. The distinction between 利見大人 and 用見大人 is also retained. The concluding claim concerns the exact wording of line statements, excluding general Judgments: outside Qian's second and fifth, Limping's top alone says 利見大人.
