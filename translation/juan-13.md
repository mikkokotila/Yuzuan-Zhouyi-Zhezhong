**Original Meaning.** “Appended statements” originally meant the words composed by King Wen and the Duke of Zhou and attached beneath the hexagrams and lines: the text now constituting the Classic. This part is Confucius's commentary on those appended statements. Because it discusses the general structure and interpretive conventions of the whole Classic, there is no particular passage of the Classic to which it can be attached. It therefore stands separately, divided into an upper and a lower part.

**Hu Yigui:** The name “Great Commentary” derives from the Grand Historian's citation of “the world reaches the same destination by different roads, and arrives at one end through a hundred deliberations” as the Great Commentary on the Changes. ## Chapter 1 · Heaven, Earth, and Ease and Simplicity

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:009 -->

### Heaven is exalted and Earth lowly: Qian and Kun are established. Low and high are arrayed: noble and humble have their positions. Movement and stillness have their constancy: the firm and yielding are distinguished. Tendencies gather by kind; things divide into groups: good fortune and misfortune arise. In Heaven, images take shape; on Earth, bodily forms take shape: change and transformation become visible.[^j13-opening]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:009 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:010 -->

**Original Meaning.** Heaven and Earth are the concrete realities of yin and yang as form and qi; Qian and Kun are the names of the Changes' hexagrams of pure yin and pure yang. Low and high are the upper and lower positions of Heaven, Earth, and the myriad things; noble and humble are the upper and lower positions of the Changes' hexagrams and lines. Movement is yang's constancy, stillness yin's; firm and yielding are the names for yin and yang in the hexagrams and lines. “Tendencies” means the directions in which affairs turn: the good and evil among affairs and things each divide according to kind. Good fortune and misfortune are the terms by which the hexagrams and lines pronounce a divinatory judgment. Images are such things as the sun, moon, and stars; bodily forms are such things as mountains, rivers, animals, and plants. Change and transformation are yin changing into yang and yang transforming into yin in the Changes' yarrow stalks, hexagrams, and lines. This says that the sages, in making the Changes, took the concrete reality of yin and yang as the basis for the exemplary images of hexagrams and lines. This is what Zhuang Zhou meant by “the Changes speaks of yin and yang.”[^j13-bian-hua]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:010 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:011 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:011 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:012 -->

**Han Kangbo:** When tendencies have kinds and things have groups, there are likeness and difference, gathering and separation. Following what they share brings good fortune; going against the direction they take brings misfortune. Thus good fortune and misfortune arise. Images refer to the sun, moon, and stars; forms refer to mountains, rivers, plants, and trees. The suspended images revolve, bringing darkness and light; mountains and lakes communicate their qi, and clouds move and rain is bestowed. Thus change and transformation become visible.[^j13-han-name]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:012 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:013 -->

**Su Shi:** Heaven and Earth are one thing; yin and yang are one qi. They become now images, now forms, differing in where they are. Thus the word “in” makes their unity clear. An image is the finest flowering of form, manifest above; a form is the embodied substance of an image, remaining below. People see above and below and simply take them for two. How could they know that they have never ceased to be one? Viewed in this light, what the world calls change and transformation always issues from one and becomes two through its different locations. Beyond two, the multiplicity cannot be counted. Thus “in Heaven, images take shape; on Earth, bodily forms take shape” is the beginning of change and transformation.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:013 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:014 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu — Question:** “Does the opening passage of Chapter One mean that the sages made the Changes by following the spontaneity of creative transformation?” **Answer:** “At the beginning, the sages followed the spontaneity of Heaven's principle and set it down in a book. But these are the words of someone speaking afterward: seeing the concrete reality of Heaven and Earth, he recognizes that the book of the Changes is like this.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:014 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:015 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu, further:** In “Heaven is exalted and Earth lowly,” the first half of each clause speaks of the principles before our eyes; the second speaks of the book of the Changes and how its makers matched Heaven and Earth. Look now at Heaven and Earth before us: these are Qian and Kun; low and high are noble and humble. There is no objection to explaining the latter half as the Changes before the lines were drawn. Yet the sage speaks from a time when the Changes already existed.[^j13-before-lines]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:015 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:016 -->

**Cai Qing:** Here the Master speaks after the Changes has come into being and looks back to the time before it existed, showing that there was a Changes before the drawing of its lines. The Changes has Qian and Kun, firm and yielding, good fortune and misfortune, change and transformation. Yet none of these names or distinctions was invented by the sages out of nothing. All were modeled upon what already exists within the six directions.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:016 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:017 -->

**Cai Qing, further:** “Established” implies that the exalted and lowly each rest in their proper share; “have their positions,” that low and high are arrayed in sequence; “distinguished,” that the division is clear and without confusion.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:017 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:018 -->

**Cai Qing, further:** With Heaven and Earth, Heaven is exalted and Earth lowly: their low and high are manifest and unchanging. With the myriad things—mountains, rivers, hills, and valleys—their low and high are likewise plainly visible.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:018 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:019 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** This passage describes the origin of the making of the Changes. It embraces Qian, Kun, and all six children. “Heaven is exalted and Earth lowly” means that Heaven and Earth have their positions; “low and high are arrayed” also includes mountains, lakes, and the rest. Heaven moves and Earth is still; mountains are still and water moves: these certainly have their constancy. Yet even things possessing essential qi without bodily substance have their times of gathering and dispersal, arising and resting, and their measures of flowing and stopping, obscurity and brightness. Thus thunder, wind, water, and fire are also included. Gathering by kind and dividing into groups comprehensively describe all the foregoing. In Heaven there are directions: spring, autumn, winter, and summer correspond to south, north, east, and west, and their life-giving and killing qi gathers by kind. On Earth there are things: high and low, dry and wet distinguish floating from sinking and rising from descending; their clear and turbid kinds divide into groups. So far the passage describes the underlying constitution of creative transformation. As for Heaven's images and Earth's forms: yin and yang rooted in one another constitute reciprocal change; yin and yang operating by turns constitute successive change. These last three clauses proceed from constitution to activity, opening the way for what follows.[^j13-season-order]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:019 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:020 -->

### Therefore the firm and yielding rub against one another, and the eight trigrams stir one another into motion.[^j13-combining]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:020 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:021 -->

**Original Meaning.** This describes the transformations of the Changes' figures. At the beginning of the sixty-four hexagrams there are only two lines, firm and yielding. Their mutual interaction makes four; the four interact to make eight; the eight stir one another into motion to make sixty-four.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:021 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:022 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:022 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:023 -->

**Han Kangbo:** Rubbing and pressing against one another describes the mutual influence of yin and yang. Pushing and stirring one another describes the successive movement of their transforming activity.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:023 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:024 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu:** “Rubbing” is those two things striking and rubbing against one another; “stirring” is their turning round and pushing the combinations forth. Rubbing belongs to what happens before the eight trigrams; stirring belongs to what happens after the eight trigrams, when the sixty-four hexagrams are made. Once those eight trigrams exist, they turn round and stir forth the sixty-four hexagrams.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:024 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:025 -->

**Wu Cheng:** At the beginning of drawing the figures, one firm and one yielding line interact with the firmness and yieldingness of the second line to make the four images. Then the two firm and two yielding components interact with the firmness and yieldingness of the third line to make the eight trigrams. Once the eight trigrams are complete, each of the eight upper trigrams is placed in turn above a single lower trigram. One trigram thus makes eight hexagrams, and eight trigrams make sixty-four.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:025 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:026 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Although this passage speaks directly of drawing the figures, this principle already exists between Heaven and Earth. Rubbing means one interacting with one: Heaven with Earth, water with fire, mountain with lake, thunder with wind. Stirring means one interacting with eight: Heaven interacts with Earth, yet also with water, fire, mountain, lake, thunder, and wind; Earth interacts with Heaven, yet likewise with all six others. Because Heaven and Earth's principle is like this, the sages drew the figures to embody its images.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:026 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:027 -->

### They are stirred by thunder and lightning, moistened by wind and rain. Sun and moon move in their courses: now cold, now heat.[^j13-thunder]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:027 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:028 -->

**Original Meaning.** This is change and transformation taking the form of images.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:028 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:029 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:029 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:030 -->

**Kong Yingda:** This further explains the earlier “change and transformation become visible,” and the firm and yielding rubbing against one another and the eight trigrams stirring one another. Once the eight trigrams set one another in motion, each has its work to perform. Zhen's thunder and Li's lightning stir things; Xun's wind and Kan's rain moisten them. Li's sun and Kan's moon move in their courses: one period is cold and another hot. Qian, Kun, Gen, and Dui are not named because Qian and Kun are fully discussed before and after this passage, while the thunder, lightning, wind, and rain mentioned also emerge from mountains and lakes.[^j13-kong-placement]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:030 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:031 -->

**Zhang Jun:** Thunder and lightning stir, and those possessing qi arise; wind and rain moisten, and those possessing form grow.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:031 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:032 -->

**Qiu Fuguo:** The foregoing speaks of Qian and Kun, noble and humble, firm and yielding, good fortune and misfortune, change and transformation. This is yin and yang standing in relation to one another: the underlying constitution of reciprocal change. Here, rubbing and stirring, arousing and moistening, and movement in courses describe yin and yang in circulation: the activity of successive change. What follows speaks of Qian and Kun's virtues and conduct, and concludes with the human embodiment of Qian and Kun.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:032 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:033 -->

**Wu Cheng:** The chapter opens by naming only Qian and Kun: the parents are named to embrace the six children. Here the six children are discussed first and afterward gathered together under Qian and Kun. Zhen is thunder, Li lightning; ting here means lightning. The Guliang Commentary on the Spring and Autumn Annals says, “What is zhen? Thunder. What is dian? Ting.” Xun is wind and Kan rain. In Sovereign Fuxi's trigram diagram, the left begins with Zhen and continues with Li: “stirred by thunder and lightning.” The right begins with Xun and continues with Kan: “moistened by wind and rain.” Wind is followed by rain, so both are spoken of as moistening. Li is the sun and Kan the moon. Gen's mountain, in the sternly congealing northwest, is cold; Dui's lake, in the warm southeast, is heat. On the left, Li followed by Dui is the sun moving to produce heat; on the right, Kan followed by Gen is the moon moving to produce cold. Master Shao says, “The sun makes heat; the moon makes cold.” The Documents says, “In the courses of sun and moon there are winter and summer.”[^j13-fuxi-diagram]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:033 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:034 -->

### Qian's Way brings the male to completion; Kun's Way brings the female to completion.[^j13-male-female]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:034 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:035 -->

**Original Meaning.** This is change and transformation taking bodily form. These two passages again make clear how the Changes appears in concrete reality, illuminating and being illuminated by what precedes them.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:035 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:036 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:036 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:037 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu:** Heaven and Earth, father and mother—plainly there is one principle. If Qian's Way forms the male and Kun's Way forms the female, then every male in the world is Qian's qi and every female Kun's qi. From here one can penetrate all the way up and all the way down: a single qi runs through the whole.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:037 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:038 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu, further:** “Qian's Way brings the male to completion; Kun's Way brings the female to completion” speaks of human beings and other things alike. Among animals there are males and females; among plants there are also male and female, as with male hemp and the male and female kinds of bamboo. None can depart from yin and yang, firmness and yieldingness.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:038 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:039 -->

**Wu Cheng:** Qian's formation of the male is the father's Way; Kun's formation of the female is the mother's Way. The left begins with Zhen, passes through Li and Dui, and ends with Qian. The right begins with Xun, passes through Kan and Gen, and ends with Kun. Thus these two sentences about Qian forming the male and Kun forming the female gather the preceding discussion together at its close.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:039 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:040 -->

**He Kai:** From “Heaven is exalted and Earth lowly” to “change and transformation become visible,” the text begins with Qian and Kun and extends to the utmost of transformation. From “the firm and yielding rub against one another” to “Kun's Way brings the female to completion,” it begins with transformation and traces its source back to Qian and Kun.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:040 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:041 -->

### Qian governs the great beginning; Kun acts to complete things.[^j13-governing]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:041 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:042 -->

**Original Meaning.** Zhi, “knows,” means governs. Qian governs the beginning of things, and Kun acts to complete them. Following the discussion of male and female, this explains Qian and Kun's principle. Everything belonging to yin and yang is like this. In general, yang precedes and yin follows; yang bestows and yin receives. Yang is light and clear, not yet formed; yin is heavy and turbid, with visible traces.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:042 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:043 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:043 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:044 -->

**Hu Yuan:** Qian is said to know, Kun to act, because Qian's generation of things begins without form, before there is any making or doing. Kun receives Heaven's qi; once things have taken form, affairs can be undertaken. Thus Qian is said to know and Kun to act.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:044 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:045 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu:** Zhi should be glossed as “to be in charge,” not understood as the knowing of knowledge and perception. The great beginning does not yet have form: there is only governing it. Completing things belongs to the time of unfolding activity, so there is doing.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:045 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:046 -->

**Chai Zhongxing:** When the one qi moves, awareness naturally appears; it is Qian that initiates the impulse of life. Once the one qi is stirred into response, it joins wondrously and condenses, and its form becomes manifest. This is the meaning of acting to complete, and it is Kun that does so.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:046 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:047 -->

**Wu Cheng:** The foregoing discussed the eight trigrams and gathered them under Qian and Kun. This now follows the two sentences on forming the male and female and speaks exclusively of Qian and Kun. Qian's male is the father because it initiates things: initiating means beginning their qi. Kun's female is the mother because it completes things: completing means completing their embodied substance. Governing is presiding without deliberate intention; acting is doing with visible traces.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:047 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:048 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** From “stirred by thunder and lightning” through these two sentences should be treated as one passage. The six children share the offices of generating and completing; Qian and Kun preside over their achievement. The following passage proceeds from the work of transformation back to its source in ease and simplicity, and forms a passage of its own.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:048 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:049 -->

### Qian governs through ease; Kun accomplishes through simplicity.[^j13-ease-simplicity]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:049 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:050 -->

**Original Meaning.** Qian is vigorous and moves: with what it governs, it can at once begin things, meeting no difficulty. Thus through ease it governs the great beginning. Kun is compliant and still: whatever it can accomplish follows yang and is not initiated on its own. Thus through simplicity it can complete things.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:050 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:051 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:051 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:052 -->

**Yu Fan:** Qian suspends its images, manifest and bright. Kun is yin; when yang moves, it opens. “Without practice, nothing is unfavorable”: Earth's Way is radiant.[^j13-yu-kun]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:052 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:053 -->

**Han Kangbo:** Heaven and Earth's Way begins things well without contrived action and completes them well without toil. Thus it is called ease and simplicity.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:053 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:054 -->

**Yang Wanli:** This praises Qian and Kun's work: though all-embracing and without bounds, their virtue is utterly essential and free from complication.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:054 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:055 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu — Question:** “What are ease and simplicity?” **Answer:** “Its vigorous movement is why there is ease. Ease means knowing obstructions and difficulties. When people have private intentions, things become difficult. Simplicity is simply following compliantly. If one introduces even a little something else beyond that, how can there be simplicity? People nowadays are all private intention; that is why they cannot be simple and easy.”[^j13-knowing-obstacles]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:055 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:056 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu — Question:** “In ‘Qian governs through ease; Kun accomplishes through simplicity,’ may we say, from the learner's standpoint, that complete openness and impartiality are ease, and responding compliantly as things come is simplicity?” **Answer:** “Yes. Qian's ease belongs to knowing; Kun's simplicity belongs to conduct.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:056 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:057 -->

**Wu Cheng:** Ease and simplicity refer to Qian and Kun's principle. Beginning things is what Qian governs. Yet Qian's nature is vigorous: its governance presides over things without tiring the heart. Thus it is easy, not difficult. Completing things is what Kun does. Yet Kun's nature is compliant: its doing follows yang without inventing affairs. Thus it is simple, not complicated. Here Qian and Kun both refer to Heaven and Earth; the Changes' two hexagrams of Qian and Kun are their images.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:057 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:058 -->

**Zhang Zhenyuan:** Qian's governing the great beginning seems extraordinarily difficult; Kun's completing things seems extraordinarily laborious. Yet Qian governs through ease, and Kun accomplishes through simplicity. This is what is meant by Heaven and Earth completing transformation without deliberate intention.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:058 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:059 -->

**Wu Yueshen:** Qian has vigor as its constitution and movement as its activity; hence ease. Kun has compliance as its constitution and stillness as its activity; hence simplicity. Movement and stillness here distinguish yin and yang. Yet Qian governs the great beginning and entrusts the work to Kun: thus it begins in movement and ends in stillness. Kun follows yang and acts to complete things: thus it begins in stillness and ends in movement. Again, Qian's governing and Kun's accomplishing are both the movement of activity; Qian's ease and Kun's simplicity are both the stillness of underlying constitution. Again, in the four virtues Kun receives from Qian: origination and success are both movement, benefit and steadfastness both stillness. One must not assign movement exclusively to Qian and stillness exclusively to Kun.[^j13-not-rigid]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:059 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:060 -->

### Ease makes one easy to know; simplicity makes one easy to follow. Being easy to know brings affinity; being easy to follow brings achievement. With affinity, one can endure; with achievement, one can grow great. What can endure is the worthy person's virtue; what can grow great is the worthy person's work.[^j13-enduring-great]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:060 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:061 -->

**Original Meaning.** When a person's conduct is like Qian's ease, the heart is open and clear, and others can readily know it. When it is like Kun's simplicity, affairs are essential and concise, and others can readily follow. Being easy to know brings many who share one's heart, and so there is affinity. Being easy to follow brings many who unite their strength with one's own, and so there is achievement. Affinity gives unity within, and so one can endure; achievement extends to what is outside, and so one can grow great. Virtue is what one realizes in oneself; work is what one accomplishes in affairs. The preceding passage distinguished the virtues of Qian and Kun. This says that when people model Qian and Kun's Way to this extent, they may become worthy.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:061 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:062 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:062 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:063 -->

**Fan Changsheng:** Because it is easy to know, things draw near and attach themselves to it. Because it is easy to follow, things take it as their model and achieve results.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:063 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:064 -->

**Kong Yingda:** At the first beginning there is no form and nothing is yet made or done; therefore the text speaks only of knowing. Once things have taken form, affairs can be undertaken; therefore it speaks of acting. Ease means unlabored simplicity, with nothing contrived. Through this there is governance, hence “Qian governs through ease.” Simplicity means economy, without the need for burdensome exertion. Through this there is accomplishment, hence “Kun accomplishes through simplicity.” If things are made difficult, one cannot govern them; if affairs are made burdensome, one cannot accomplish them. “Being easy to know brings affinity”: when one's nature and intentions are readily known and the heart contains no treacherous difficulties, there is harmony and closeness. “Being easy to follow brings achievement”: when affairs are readily followed and free from burdensome toil, their accomplishment is easy. “With affinity, one can endure”: once things are harmonious and close, they do not injure and destroy one another, and so can last. “With achievement, one can grow great”: successful work can gradually accumulate and expand. “What can endure is the worthy person's virtue”: enabling things to endure is that virtue. “What can grow great is the worthy person's work”: once the achievement is great, it is the work of a worthy person.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:064 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:065 -->

**Su Shi:** Simplicity and ease mean oneness. Because there is oneness, there is trustworthiness; because there is trustworthiness, things know it. It is easy, and following it is not difficult.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:065 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:066 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu:** Through “Qian governs through ease; Kun accomplishes through simplicity,” the text speaks of Qian and Kun's virtues. From “ease makes one easy to know” onward, it speaks of people embodying both virtues. Qian governs through ease because its vigor never ceases: it concerns itself only with generating things, without all those difficulties and obstructions. Thus it can govern the great beginning through ease. Kun compliantly receives Heaven, concerning itself only with completing things, without all those troublesome undertakings. Thus through simplicity it can complete them. In general, yang bestows and yin receives. Qian generates things as a jar pours out water: its Way is utterly easy. Kun merely receives Heaven to complete things, with no separate undertaking: its principle is utterly simple. In human beings, there is no difficulty or obstruction, and one is naturally straightforward; hence others find one easy to know. One follows principle without troublesome interference; hence others find one easy to follow. Easy to know, one gains the affinity of people who share one's heart; easy to follow, one gains their combined effort and achieves results. **Editorial Judgment.** Heaven and Earth, low and high, movement and stillness, tendencies and things, images and forms are the concrete reality of creative transformation. Qian and Kun, noble and humble, firm and yielding, good fortune and misfortune, change and transformation are the fixed names within the Changes' figures. Those names arise from transformation's concrete reality. Thus, once the underlying constitution of transformation is established, the figures' principles are complete. Constitution established, activity must proceed. Hence, with firm and yielding, one rubs against one; with the eight trigrams, one stirs with eight. This is how the dispositions of creative transformation interact without becoming separate; the order of drawing the figures images this. Thunder and lightning are Zhen and Li, wind and rain Xun and Kan. Heat that delights things is Dui; cold that brings them to rest is Gen. Forming the male and presiding over the great beginning is Qian; forming the female and presiding over the completion of things is Kun. This is how the workings of creative transformation change without exhaustion; the positions in the diagram image this. Yet the principle of transformation returns wholly to ease and simplicity. The heart is one and undivided: hence ease. Affairs follow their course without contrived action: hence simplicity. Heaven and Earth's abundant virtue and great work are only ease and simplicity. The worthy person's advancing virtue and cultivating work, and the sage's exalting virtue and widening work, are likewise only ease and simplicity. This is why setting out the hexagrams and appending statements accords with the principle of nature and the mandate. Scholars speak of four meanings of the Changes: the unchanging, reciprocal change, successive change, and ease and simplicity. Thus the passage beginning “Heaven is exalted and Earth lowly” concerns the unchanging; the two clauses on firm and yielding rubbing together concern reciprocal change; from thunder and lightning through Kun's completion of things concerns successive change; from Qian's governing through ease onward concerns ease and simplicity. The origin of the Changes' Way is exhausted here. Hence this is the first chapter of the Appended Statements.[^j13-four-senses]

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:226 -->

### One yin, one yang: this is called the Way.[^j13-one-yin-yang]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:226 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:227 -->

**Original Meaning.** Yin and yang operating by turns are qi; their principle is what is called the Way.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:227 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:228 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:228 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:229 -->

**Master Shao:** The Way has neither sound nor form and cannot be seen. Thus it borrows its name from the way that is a road. Whenever people travel, they must follow a way. One yin, one yang is Heaven and Earth's Way. Through it things are born; through it they are completed.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:229 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:230 -->

**Master Cheng:** Apart from yin and yang there is no Way. What makes yin and yang be yin and yang is the Way. Yin and yang are qi. Qi belongs to what is below form; the Way belongs to what is above form.[^j13-above-below]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:230 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:231 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu:** Principle is one. What has form is called a concrete thing; what has no form is called the Way. Yet without concrete things the Way does not take form, and without the Way concrete things do not stand. Yin and yang, too, are concrete things; what makes them yin and yang is the Way. Thus one yin, one yang come and go without cease, and the sage points to this to make clear the Way in its entirety.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:231 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:232 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** “One yin, one yang” embraces both standing in opposition and operating by turns. Standing in opposition includes Heaven and Earth, sun and moon, and the like: what the previous chapter calls firm and yielding. Operating by turns includes the coming and going of cold and heat, and the like: what the previous chapter calls change and transformation.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:232 -->

### What continues it is goodness; what completes it is nature.[^j13-goodness-nature]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:233 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:234 -->

Completion speaks of its being fully present; nature means what things receive. Things, once born, have their natures, each fully possessing this Way: the affair of yin. The writings of Master Zhou and Master Cheng explain this fully.

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:236 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:237 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:239 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:241 -->

**Xiang Anshi:** All that the Way generates is good: this is origination, from which the myriad things issue together. <!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:244 -->

**Xiong Liangfu:** Heaven's Way circulates, bringing forth and nourishing the myriad things: the continuation of goodness. Origination is foremost among goodness; goodness is precisely origination. <!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:245 -->

**Pan Shizao:** Goodness is nature's source; nature is goodness made real. Both goodness and nature are Heaven's principle. Though firmness and yieldingness, good and evil, centeredness and partiality differ in their midst, Heaven's mandate in its original condition does not differ.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:245 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:246 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** The sage's use of “continues” is exceptionally precise; this word must not be passed over. It resembles a child's continuing the parental line or continuing a parent's intention. Human beings are Heaven and Earth's children. Heaven and Earth's principle is entrusted to human beings in its entirety, and they receive it, just as the Classic of Filial Piety says that the body, hair, and skin are received from one's parents. Yet to say “entrust” speaks from Heaven and Earth's side; to say “receive” speaks from the human side. Only “continue” reveals the succession joining Heaven and humanity, embracing both entrusting and receiving. What Heaven entrusts and humanity receives is principle, none of which is not good. How, then, could what constitutes human nature be other than good? Mencius's teaching of the goodness of nature is rooted here. Yet once this principle is embodied in human beings and other things, though its source is wholly good, its subsequent streams divide and differ. The humane, the wise, and the common people mentioned below are all confined by the partiality of what they receive and cannot preserve entire the fullness entrusted to them. Thus Cheng and Zhu's account of the endowment of qi is also rooted here. One could not hear the Master speak of nature and Heaven's Way. Yet this statement in the Appended Statements speaks of nature and Heaven's Way to the utmost. If later discussions of nature take their measure from the Master, the wrangling of the various schools can be brought to rest.[^j13-inheritance]

## Chapter 6 · Breadth and Greatness

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:296 -->

### The Changes—how broad and great! As to the distant, it is unbounded; as to the near, still and correct; as to what lies between Heaven and Earth, complete.[^j13-far-near]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:296 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:297 -->

**Original Meaning.** Unbounded means inexhaustible. Still and correct means that principle is present in the things themselves. Complete means that there is nothing it does not possess.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:297 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:298 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Distant and near are spoken of horizontally; between Heaven and Earth, vertically. Principle extends to where nothing lies outside it: hence distant. Nature is fully present in one's own person: hence near. The mandate reaches from Heaven to humanity, penetrating above and below: hence between Heaven and Earth. Unbounded is what was called weaving together in all fullness; still and correct, what was called resembling; complete, what was called framing and encompassing.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:298 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:299 -->

### Qian in stillness is undivided; in movement, direct. Thus it generates greatly. Kun in stillness gathers inward; in movement, opens outward. Thus it generates broadly.[^j13-great-broad]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:299 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:300 -->

**Original Meaning.** Qian and Kun each have movement and stillness, as their four virtues reveal. Stillness is constitution, movement activity; in stillness they are distinct, in movement they interact. Qian is one and solid, so the text speaks of its substance and calls it great. Kun is two and open, so it speaks of its capacity and calls it broad. Although Heaven's form encloses Earth from outside, its qi constantly moves within Earth. This is why the Changes is broad and great.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:300 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:301 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:301 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:302 -->

**Kong Yingda:** When qi has not issued into movement, it is still and concentrated in one; hence “in stillness, undivided.” When it circulates, the four seasons do not err, cold and heat do not deviate, and firmness attains correctness; hence “in movement, direct.” Because its movement and stillness are like this, it can generate greatly. Closure, storage, gathering, and contraction make Kun's stillness an inward gathering. In movement it opens and generates the myriad things; hence “in movement, opens outward.” Because it is like this, it can generate things broadly.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:302 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:303 -->

**Master Cheng:** Qian is yang: without movement it is not firm. In stillness it is undivided, in movement direct. Without concentration in one, it cannot proceed directly. Kun is yin: without stillness it is not yielding. In stillness it gathers inward, in movement it opens outward. Without inward gathering, it cannot spread abroad.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:303 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:304 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu:** Heaven is one undivided whole. Though it surrounds Earth from outside, its qi springs forth from within Earth. Though Earth is a single mass within Heaven, its interior is open and can admit Heaven's qi as it springs upward. Generating greatly means being a whole that embraces everything; generating broadly means having the breadth to receive that qi of Heaven. Undividedness and directness are just one thing proceeding straight ahead. Gathering and opening are two: when it gathers, it gathers; when it opens, it opens. These are the forms of the odd and the even.[^j13-odd-even]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:304 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:305 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu, further:** Qian is undivided when still and direct when moving, and so generates greatly. Kun gathers when still and opens when moving, and so generates broadly. This describes yin and yang's constitution and nature, and the drawn lines faintly resemble it. Qian's line is odd: one sees its undivided stillness and direct movement. Kun's line is even: one sees its gathering stillness and opening movement.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:305 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:306 -->

**Hu Bingwen:** Qian is vigorous, and therefore bestows as one. Kun is compliant, and therefore receives as two. Undivided stillness is the preservation of the one; direct movement is its extension. Gathering stillness is the joining of the two; opening movement is their separation. The extension of the one is how it moves within Kun's two. Thus the text speaks of substance and calls it great. The separation of the two is how they receive Qian's one. Thus it speaks of capacity and calls it broad.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:306 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:307 -->

**Lin Xiyuan:** This traces why the Changes is broad and great. Qian and Kun are the parents of the myriad things. Each has its nature and qi, and each has movement and stillness. Qian's nature and qi are concentrated in one when still, without turning elsewhere. Because of this undividedness, their movement proceeds directly without bending or yielding. Because it proceeds directly, the issuing forth of its nature and qi reaches every part of the four quarters and the eight outer reaches. Its compass is utterly great; hence it generates greatly. Kun's nature and qi gather and join in stillness without leaking away. Because of this inward gathering, their movement opens without closing anything out. Because it opens without exclusion, wherever Qian's qi reaches, Kun can receive it. Its capacity is utterly broad; hence it generates broadly. Qian and Kun are Heaven and Earth. Generating greatly and broadly both refer to them. The breadth and greatness of the book of the Changes model this; the breadth and greatness discussed in this particular passage must not be taken as the book itself.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:307 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:308 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** This passage continues “broad and great” from the preceding one and traces Heaven and Earth's breadth and greatness entirely to ease and simplicity. Thus the following passage says that the book of the Changes “matches Heaven and Earth in breadth and greatness” and concludes by returning to ease and simplicity. Undivided in stillness and direct in movement means not the slightest private crookedness: nothing describes ease more fully. Gathering in stillness and opening in movement means not the slightest contrived action: nothing describes simplicity more fully. Ease appears in directness, meaning openness without treacherous difficulty; its root comes from undividedness. Simplicity appears in opening, meaning free passage without obstruction; its root comes from inward gathering.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:308 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:309 -->

### Its breadth and greatness match Heaven and Earth; its change and free passage match the four seasons; its meanings of yin and yang match sun and moon; the goodness of its ease and simplicity matches consummate virtue.[^j13-matching]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:309 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:310 -->

**Original Meaning.** The breadth and greatness of the Changes, its change and free passage, its account of yin and yang, and its virtues of ease and simplicity correspond in this way to Heaven's Way and human affairs.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:310 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:311 -->

**Original Meaning — Chapter Summary.** This is the sixth chapter.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:311 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:312 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:312 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:313 -->

**Kong Yingda:** The opening chapter made ease the worthy person's virtue and simplicity the worthy person's work. Here both are called consummate virtue because, when set in contrast, virtue and work are distinct, but when spoken of comprehensively, work derives from virtue and both are called virtue.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:313 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:314 -->

**Wu Cheng:** Within the book's breadth and greatness there are change and free passage and the meanings of yin and yang, just as Heaven and Earth have their four seasons, sun, and moon. The four seasons, sun, and moon are Heaven and Earth themselves, just as the six children of the Changes are Qian and Kun themselves. Its breadth, greatness, change, free passage, yin, and yang are all governed by the goodness of ease and simplicity. Heaven and Earth's consummate virtue is likewise nothing but this goodness. Thus the goodness of the book's ease and simplicity matches Heaven and Earth's consummate virtue.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4704:314 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4704:315 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** These last three chapters elaborate the passage “change and transformation are the images of advance and retreat.” They begin by saying that the Changes can weave together Heaven and Earth's Way in all its fullness: the principles of the hidden and manifest, death and life, spirits and ghosts are precisely the workings of advance and retreat, day and night. Next they say that the Changes resembles Heaven and Earth: the nature of humaneness and rightness is precisely the Way of the Three Ultimates. They also say that the Changes can frame and encompass Heaven and Earth's transformations. Assisting Heaven and Earth's transforming nourishment and also knowing it, one threads together the Way of the Three Ultimates and the workings of advance and retreat, day and night. Fathoming principle and fully realizing nature so as to reach the mandate completes the work of the numinous and transformation: this is the Changes' inner meaning. They then unfold these points one by one. Heaven and Earth's Way is one yin, one yang. Heaven and Earth's nature is one humaneness, one wisdom. Heaven and Earth's transformation is one manifestation, one concealment, stirring the myriad things. Change without fixed form is unceasing generation: its manifestation in Qian and Kun and its taking shape in divination and action are all this. The numinous without fixed location is yin and yang beyond fathoming. Finally, they gather it all together in the fullest praise. In the Changes' fathoming of principle, the distant is unbounded; in its full realization of nature, the near is still and correct; in its reaching the mandate, all between Heaven and Earth is complete. They further trace its root to ease and simplicity. Undivided stillness and direct movement are ease; inward-gathering stillness and outward-opening movement are simplicity. The principle of ease and simplicity is fully present in the Way of the Three Ultimates and operates through advance and retreat, day and night. Thus, taken as a whole, the Changes matches Heaven and Earth in breadth and greatness. Distinguished into its aspects: change and transformation are the images of advance and retreat, and change and free passage match the four seasons; firmness and yieldingness are the images of day and night, and the meanings of yin and yang match sun and moon; the movement of the six lines is the Way of the Three Ultimates, and the goodness of ease and simplicity matches consummate virtue.[^j13-final-structure]

## Translator’s Endnotes

The base is the repository’s unchanged 易學網 text. Doubtful readings were compared selectively with [the Siku Quanshu transcription, fixed revision 528601](<https://zh.wikisource.org/w/index.php?title=御纂周易折中_(四庫全書本)/卷13&oldid=528601>). Corrections, repunctuation, supplementary material, and unresolved readings are distinguished below. This is not a systematic facsimile collation. Added descriptive chapter headings are navigational, not translations of source headings.

[^j13-scope]: This juan contains Chapters One–Six of the upper Appended Statements, not the whole upper part. The remaining upper chapters follow in Juan Fourteen. The source’s opening and closing juan titles and its six chapter summaries are preserved but distinguished from the canonical text. The attribution of the Wings to Confucius is the commentators’ traditional account, reproduced as their account rather than independently established authorship. “Original Meaning” is Zhu Xi; “Editorial Judgment” is the imperial compilers. Quotations from the Conversations retain their separate voice.

[^j13-key-terms]: 氣 is retained as qi: the vital-material activity through which these commentators describe bodies, weather, life, and awareness, not simply modern physical energy. 理 is “principle”; 道 is “the Way”; 造化 is “creative transformation,” without implying creation from nothing. 心 is normally “heart,” including thought, intention, and feeling. 體 and 用 are rendered according to context as underlying constitution and activity, or embodiment and use; they should not automatically be read as a Western opposition between inert substance and an external function.

[^j13-great-commentary]: The Grand Historian is Sima Qian. Hu Yigui offers an explanation of the title 大傳 based on a lineage of textual transmission. The translation preserves this as Hu’s historical explanation, not as a settled account of how the title first arose. His quotation is from the lower Appended Statements.

[^j13-opening]: The opening alternates between the world and the book: Heaven and Earth / Qian and Kun, elevation / ranked positions, movement and stillness / firm and yielding lines, kinds and groups / divinatory judgments, celestial images and terrestrial forms / transformations. 方 can mean direction or tendency; the main translation follows Zhu Xi’s 事情所向, while the compilers explicitly develop the spatial and seasonal sense. The hierarchy of 尊卑 and 貴賤 belongs to the text and is not replaced by an egalitarian paraphrase.

[^j13-bian-hua]: 變化 is generally “change and transformation.” Here Zhu distinguishes 變, the advance from yielding yin toward firm yang, from 化, the reverse process. His listing of “pure yin and pure yang” after Qian and Kun is not a one-to-one identification in that order: Qian is pure yang, Kun pure yin. 蓍策 are the yarrow stalks or stalk-counts used in divination. The Zhuangzi quotation uses 道 as a verb, “to speak of,” rather than naming the Way.

[^j13-han-name]: The source attributes this and subsequent glosses to 韓氏伯, Han Bo, conventionally known as Han Kangbo. The English uses Han Kangbo throughout. His explanations belong to the received Wang Bi–Han Kangbo commentarial tradition but must not be attributed to Wang Bi himself.

[^j13-before-lines]: Zhu’s speech is repunctuated after 處如此: it concerns the places where the book matches Heaven and Earth, then turns to the example before the reader. No Chinese words are removed. “The Changes before the lines were drawn” names the order that the diagrams embody, not a claimed lost manuscript.

[^j13-season-order]: The base lists 春秋冬夏 and 南北東西 in that order, and the parallel Siku transcription has the same sequence. Read positionally, those lists do not yield the familiar season–direction correspondences. Their ordering is therefore preserved, not silently rearranged; the sentence may be enumerating the sets rather than pairing each item consecutively. “Six children” denotes the six trigrams besides Qian and Kun. The mutual-rooting and alternation of yin and yang distinguish 交易 from 變易 here.

[^j13-combining]: 摩 and 盪 retain their physical images of rubbing and stirring. Zhu and Wu read them as successive stages of constructing figures: two line types, four two-line combinations, eight trigrams, and sixty-four hexagrams. The compilers insist that this construction embodies relationships already present in the world. Wu’s 貞 and 悔 here designate lower and upper trigrams; they do not mean moral steadfastness and regret in this passage.

[^j13-thunder]: 雷霆 ordinarily evokes thunder and the thunderbolt. “Thunder and lightning” follows the specific reading of Kong Yingda and Wu Cheng, who explicitly identify 霆 with 電 and distribute the terms to Zhen and Li. The pronoun 之 is left broad: the text moves between the figures’ transformations and the things they image. The English does not turn these traditional correspondences into a claim about modern meteorology.

[^j13-kong-placement]: Kong’s compact sentence is read as 乾坤上下備言，雷電風雨亦出山澤也: Qian and Kun are fully discussed before and after this passage, while thunder, lightning, wind, and rain also emerge from mountains and lakes. This repunctuation, rather than the base’s stop after 備, explains why none of those four trigrams is absent from the argument.

[^j13-fuxi-diagram]: Wu uses the Fuxi arrangement: his sequence and directions should not be silently changed to those of the later King Wen arrangement. “Left” and “right” describe the diagram as he reads it, not a map with modern north-at-the-top orientation. The passage concerns the six child-trigrams distributed between Qian and Kun. Its assignments of cold and heat are preserved as Wu’s cosmological interpretation.

[^j13-male-female]: 男 and 女 are male and female, not merely abstract “active” and “receptive” qualities. Zhu deliberately extends the claim from human beings to animals and plants. His bamboo example is reproduced as a historical classification, not endorsed as modern botanical taxonomy; no biological mechanism has been supplied by the translator.

[^j13-governing]: 知 usually means knowing, but Zhu expressly glosses it 主 and 管, “govern” or “be in charge,” here. The main text follows that gloss while Chai’s discussion retains actual awareness, 知覺. The distinction is important: the compilation does not supply a single unchallenged theory of cognition in nature. Later 知 in this juan often means wisdom and is then translated accordingly.

[^j13-ease-simplicity]: 易 in 易簡 is yì, “ease,” not the book-title sense of change. 簡 is simplicity or freedom from needless complication. Neither is laziness: the commentators derive ease from vigor and simplicity from compliance, then apply both to human effort. The following argument also plays on 易知, “easy to know,” so the governing sense of 知 must not be forced into every occurrence.

[^j13-yu-kun]: The compact base phrase 坤陰陽動辟 is read as 坤陰，陽動闢: “Kun is yin; when yang moves, it opens.” The parallel transcription has the same sequence. This is a disclosed punctuation and syntactic interpretation, not a recovered missing character. The concluding quotations refer to Kun’s second-line statement and Small Image.

[^j13-knowing-obstacles]: The surprising reading 易是知阻難之謂, “ease means knowing obstructions and difficulties,” occurs in the base, the parallel Siku transcription, and the separately transmitted Conversations of Master Zhu, juan 74 (https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/朱子語類/074). It is retained rather than conjecturally changed to 無, “without.” Nearby discussions in that separate transmission connect Qian’s ease and Kun’s simplicity with knowing danger and obstruction; nonetheless the precise force of this compressed definition remains open.

[^j13-not-rigid]: Wu explicitly rejects a rigid identification of yang with movement and yin with stillness in every context. His argument depends on changing the level of analysis: sequence of action, constitution and activity, and the four virtues. These are the commentator’s distinctions, not contradictory sentences to be smoothed into a single binary scheme.

[^j13-enduring-great]: The translation follows Zhu’s shift from cosmological Qian and Kun to human practice. 德 is what is realized within oneself; 業 is the work accomplished in affairs. 有親 can encompass affection, affinity, and shared intention, not only literal kinship. Zhao Guangda’s final gloss differs from Zhu’s explanation of accomplishment through cooperative help, and that disagreement has not been harmonized.

[^j13-no-interference]: 無事 in the Mencius quotation does not mean doing nothing regardless of circumstances. Lin takes it as following the course that does not require forcing or complicating things. This is why the translation uses “no interference.” The quoted example is Yu’s guiding of water, an undertaking rather than a withdrawal from responsibility.

[^j13-human-place]: The Chinese does not repeat 天地 after 其中. Zhu explicitly supplies Heaven and Earth as the reference and takes 成位 as fulfilling the human place between them. The ensuing 參 means joining them as a third, not being identical to Heaven or Earth. The main translation leaves “their midst” concise and lets the commentary supply its account.

[^j13-chapter-summaries]: The six paragraphs beginning “This is Chapter…” belong to Zhu Xi’s Original Meaning, not to the canonical Appended Statements. They are printed in bold in the base transcription, much like canonical passages; the translation identifies them separately. Their placement is preserved, including where a summary precedes collected explanations of the chapter’s last passage.

[^j13-four-senses]: The compilers distinguish 不易, 交易, 變易, and 易簡: the unchanging, reciprocal or relational change, successive transformation, and ease with simplicity. These four are their analytical categories here. They should not be conflated with the more familiar threefold enumeration used in some other accounts of 易.

[^j13-divination]: Zhu’s insistence on an original divinatory purpose is retained alongside the work’s moral and cosmological interpretations. The translation does not replace divining with purely psychological reflection. Conversely, faithfully translating the commentators’ confidence in divination is not an independent demonstration of its predictive reliability.

[^j13-kong-observation]: The word 案 occurs inside the paragraph attributed to Kong Yingda. It is translated “A further observation” within that same attribution, not promoted to an independently labeled imperial Editorial Judgment. The base supplies no separate 【案】 heading here.

[^j13-four-judgments]: The primary sentence lists 吉凶 beside 失得 in nonparallel order. The translation preserves “good fortune and misfortune / loss and gain,” while Zhu’s and Yu Fan’s explanations explicitly pair good fortune with gain and misfortune with loss. 得失 also carries the sense of getting conduct right or wrong; it is not confined to acquiring or losing possessions. 悔 and 吝 are translated “regret” and “humiliation,” but the commentaries also explore the dispositions that lead into or out of these judgments.

[^j13-lin-shades]: 吝 ranges here from an oracle’s humiliation or shame to contracted, grudging conduct. Zhu’s last comparison is between pride and the meanness of withholding or shrinking, so “meanness” is used in that final phrase rather than mechanically repeating “humiliation.” His yin–yang assignment is the historical commentator’s analysis of dispositions.

[^j13-cycle-reading]: The base has 吝自言而趨凶, literally “humiliation proceeds from words toward misfortune.” The parallel Siku transcription reads 吝自吉而趨凶, “humiliation proceeds from good fortune toward misfortune,” agreeing with the cycle already explained in Zhu’s preceding gloss and Original Meaning. The English adopts 吉; the Chinese source remains unchanged.

[^j13-three-ultimates]: 三極 is kept distinct from 三才, “Three Powers,” and 太極, “Supreme Ultimate.” Zhu assigns two lines each to Earth, the human being, and Heaven, then says that each possesses the ultimate principle. The concluding Master Cheng gloss below instead explains 極 through 中, the appropriate center of each position and time. That difference is retained; the translation does not assume that every commentator means the same thing by 極.

[^j13-order-pondering]: 序 is the fitting order of situations, phases, and line positions here; it need not mean only the received sequence of sixty-four hexagrams. 玩 means sustained, attentive dwelling upon the text, with both understanding and delight. “Ponder” preserves this active attention rather than suggesting idle play. The two uses of 居 are distinguished by the source itself: one’s situation, and repose before action.

[^j13-dragon-order]: Zhu’s spoken list is 潛、見、飛、躍, “hiding, appearing, flying, leaping,” although the fourth-line leap precedes the fifth-line flying dragon in Qian. The enumeration is retained as spoken, not reordered into line sequence. “Hengqu” is Zhang Zai, whose words are cited here by his place-associated name.

[^j13-study-use]: The text distinguishes general study in repose from divinatory consultation in an actual affair. Cai Yuan’s concentration on a particular line is his formulation here, not a complete set of rules for every possible changing-line configuration. The blessing quotes Great Possession’s top-line statement. The commentators’ claim of unfailing auspiciousness expresses their account of fully realized learning, not an empirical guarantee made by this translation.

[^j13-historical-examples]: The base abbreviates 知莊子 to 莊子. This is Zhi Zhuangzi, Xun Shou of Jin, not the Daoist philosopher Zhuang Zhou: his use of The Army changing to Approach appears in the Zuo Commentary, Duke Xuan, year 12 (https://zh.wikisource.org/zh-hant/春秋左傳註疏/宣公/十二年). Yu’s point is grammatical and practical: 在 identifies a textual example, whereas 遇 describes a figure encountered in consultation. His concise descriptions of the other cases are preserved without expanding them into reconstructed narratives.

[^j13-earlier-later]: 先天 and 後天 here distinguish the diagram-making and statement-appending phases of the sages’ work, associated in the surrounding discussion with Fuxi and the Zhou sages. “Earlier Heaven” and “Later Heaven” are retained as traditional terms, not treated as dates before and after the physical creation of Heaven.

[^j13-judgment-line]: 彖 here means the hexagram Judgment, not the separately treated Commentary on the Judgments translated in Juans Nine and Ten. 爻 is used elliptically for the line statement. Zhang’s “no fixed actual event” means that an image is not exhausted by one concrete occurrence; he does not deny that the Changes concerns real affairs.

[^j13-mending]: 補 preserves the image of mending, developed by Yang Wanli through torn clothing. 無咎 is not automatically a promise of favorable external results. The compilers below explicitly distinguish it from seeking good fortune and point to combinations such as “misfortune, no blame.” The text’s ethical and prognostic vocabularies overlap without being identical.

[^j13-position-reading]: The base’s 位元有當否 in Zhang Zhenyuan’s gloss contains an intrusive 元. The parallel transcription reads 位有當否, “positions have fittingness or unfitness,” adopted in English. No revision has been made to the Chinese file.

[^j13-small-great]: 齊 is disputed within the collected explanations: Zhu glosses it 定, settling or determining; Wang Su glosses it 正, setting right; Wang Shenzi glosses it 均, equalizing. The main text follows Zhu while retaining the others’ distinct arguments. “Small” and “great” here can name yin and yang rather than physical size. Wang’s account of a minority line governing a figure should not be turned into an exceptionless rule for every hexagram without regard to context.

[^j13-dividing-point]: 介 is a dividing point or minute distinction, not necessarily the same word as 幾, the incipient tendency. The commentators connect them because a tiny differentiation can become a consequential course of action. 震 here is the rousing or alarm that moves one to correction, not the trigram name. The parallel transcription contains a 集說 heading and a Yu Fan gloss missing from the base immediately after Zhu’s explanation.

[^j13-yu-supplement]: SUPPLEMENTARY COLLECTED EXPLANATION. The parallel Siku transcription inserts a gloss attributed to Yu Fan before Han Kangbo: “Rousing means movement. Whenever there was anything not good, he never failed to know it; once he knew it, he never did it again. ‘No blame’ means making good one’s faults; therefore it rests in regret.” Chinese: 虞氏翻曰震動也有不善未嘗不知之知之未嘗復行无咎者善補過故存乎悔也. It is translated here only, not silently added to the source-aligned main text. The allusion to knowing and not repeating a fault recalls the Appended Statements’ account of Yan Hui.

[^j13-practical-examples]: The four allusions are to Enthusiasm’s third line, Zhun’s third line, Approach’s third line, and Return’s third line. Wang Dizhi distinguishes precaution at the decision point from repentance after a fault. The examples depend on the lines’ own language and should be read alongside the fuller translations in Juans One and Three.

[^j13-danger-ease]: Zhu’s informal distinction between “good” and “not good” hexagrams is preserved as his explanation, including its unqualified wording. It is not silently converted into a universal ranking that overrides individual line statements. Xiang and Pan emphasize capacities and changing circumstances instead. “Dangerous” and “easy” statements concern what the language indicates, not how difficult the Chinese is to read.

[^j13-no-blame-distinction]: The compilers make a consequential distinction between advantage or a favorable outcome and freedom from fault. A divinatory “misfortune” can therefore coexist with “no blame.” Their demand for repentance also makes no blame something that can be attained through correction, rather than an assertion that nothing was ever wrong.

[^j13-weaving]: 彌綸 joins comprehensiveness with articulated order. Zhu glosses 彌 through closing a seam and 綸 through arranging silk threads; Su invokes warp and weft. “Weave together … in all its fullness” preserves both dimensions, which a bare “include everything” would lose. 準 can be a measure, a correspondence, or equality of scope; the commentators’ different emphases remain visible.

[^j13-book-sage]: The Chinese leaves the subject of several following clauses unstated. Zhu speaks of the sage’s activity; Yu Yan and the compilers emphasize the Changes itself, while arguing that sage and book share the same principle. The main rendering uses an impersonal “one” where English requires an agent, following the activity described without silently suppressing this interpretive issue. 窮理、盡性、至命 are distinct: fathoming principle, fully realizing nature, and reaching the mandate.

[^j13-souls]: The main text’s “wandering soul” is 游魂. The commentaries distinguish hun 魂, associated here with yang, movement, intention, and awareness, from po 魄, associated with yin, bodily substance, and sensory capacity. The Chinese terms are retained in those explanations to avoid importing a single Western doctrine of an immortal, immaterial soul. 鬼神 is translated literally as “ghosts and spirits”; the text is not being reduced either to modern psychology or to a generic supernaturalism.

[^j13-extending-returning]: Zhu associates 神 with 伸, extending, and 鬼 with 歸, returning. His account connects gathering into living form with spirit and dispersal with ghost. 精氣 is split by Zhu into yin essence and yang qi; Su’s 精氣 below can instead be read as the compound “essential qi.” The differing syntax follows their own explanations.

[^j13-two-accounts]: Su places ghosts with embodied things and spirits with transformation; Zhu’s preceding gloss associates the gathering into things with spirit and dispersal with ghost. These are not silently made identical. Su classifies through body/po and mobile hun, while Zhu’s preceding explanation emphasizes the processes of extension and return. Both passages are present in the base and parallel transcription; an interpretive difference is not treated as a typo.

[^j13-two-five]: 二五之氣 abbreviates yin and yang and the five phases, not the second and fifth hexagram lines. Chen’s reflection concerns fulfillment of what one has received and acceptance of transformation at death. His account is translated as historical cosmology and ethics, not supplied with a modern physiological explanation. “Five phases” does not mean five chemical elements.

[^j13-ear-reading]: The base has 耳之所之聰 where the parallel Siku transcription reads 耳之所以聰, matching the preceding construction about the eyes. The English follows the latter: “what makes the ears hear acutely.” This is a transcription correction supported by that parallel witness, not a claim to have checked the historical printed leaf.

[^j13-wholeness]: 全而生之 and 全而歸之 evoke receiving one’s life whole and returning it whole, a filial and ethical account of beginning and end. Zheng’s concise statement is preserved rather than expanded into a doctrine of bodily survival after death.

[^j13-wisdom-humaneness]: 知 in the ethical pair 知仁 means wisdom. Zhu assigns wisdom to Heaven and humaneness to Earth here, but reverses their yin–yang alignment in the next chapter when a different contrast is at issue; he explicitly explains that difference there. The main translation supplies the sage as the acting subject for readable English, while Yu Yan takes the subject as the book of the Changes. The compilers argue that the sage and the book cannot be separated in this account. 命 is “mandate,” including the conditions one receives from Heaven, not a command to abandon responsible action.

[^j13-love]: 仁 and 愛 are not collapsed into one word. Zhu calls humaneness the principle of love, and love its activity. 安土 is not rendered as attachment to native soil: Zhu explains it as being at peace wherever one finds oneself. That inward composure is explicitly compatible with concern for others and for the world.

[^j13-ranging]: 旁行 admits adaptive action outside the usual course. Zhu reads this through wisdom exercising discretion; the compilers prefer mature humaneness acting responsively, joined to rightness. Their concluding reservation concerns the force of 行, “act,” not a denial that wisdom matters. 無憂 means freedom from anxious disturbance here, not indifference: the compilers retain 憂世之誠, sincere concern for the world.

[^j13-numinous]: 神 here is “the numinous,” naming the unfathomable efficacy of transformation, rather than one personal deity or the human hun-soul alone. 方 and 體 admit both absence of a visible location or bodily form and absence of any fixed location or form; Kong explicitly preserves both readings. This translation keeps “fixed” in the canonical sentence but translates his fuller distinction. 易 can name change itself or its book; Lin Xiyuan explicitly moves between the two senses.

[^j13-framing]: 範圍 retains the images of a casting mold and an enclosing boundary. 曲成 means accomplishing the particular fulfillment of every thing, not forcing all things into the same shape. Zhu connects the phrase with Peace’s “fashioning and completing” Heaven and Earth’s Way. 至命, “reaching the mandate,” is presented as the culmination of fathoming principle and realizing nature, not as a separate occult technique.

[^j13-hidden-reading]: Lin Xiyuan’s base gloss has 幽朋, where the parallel Siku transcription has 幽眀, the variant writing of 幽明, “hidden and manifest.” The English adopts that supported correction. This is not a claim of facsimile verification.

[^j13-one-yin-yang]: The compact 一陰一陽 admits both alternation and correlative opposition; the compilers explicitly insist on both. “One yin, one yang” preserves that openness instead of limiting the sentence to “first yin, then yang.” The text calls this the Way, while Zhu and Cheng distinguish the qi that alternates from the principle through which it does so.

[^j13-above-below]: 形而上 and 形而下 are rendered as “above form” and “below form.” The passage does not place the Way in a separate spatial realm: Cheng says there is no Way apart from yin and yang, and Zhu says neither the Way nor concrete things can stand without the other. 器 literally means a vessel or implement but here denotes what has determinate form; “concrete thing” avoids suggesting only manufactured tools.

[^j13-goodness-nature]: The pronoun 之 refers back to the Way. The English leaves continuation and completion open to the commentators’ different emphases: Zhu describes generation and determinate embodiment; the compilers emphasize an inheritance joining Heaven and humanity. 性 is nature, especially the principle constituting a being, not merely temperament. “Goodness” is the claim made by these commentators, not an independent empirical finding about human behavior.

[^j13-sincerity]: 誠 is rendered “sincerity,” following the project’s terminology, but here it includes the real, unfeigned fulfillment of principle in nature, not only honesty in speech. Master Zhou is Zhou Dunyi. His passage quotes Qian’s Judgment Commentary and joins it to these lines of the Appended Statements; the translation preserves that synthesis as his.

[^j13-pearl]: 氣質 here is the endowment or constitution of qi. Zhu’s water-and-pearl image distinguishes the goodness of nature from the clarity or turbidity of its manifestation; it does not say that differences of conduct are unreal. The same distinction is subsequently invoked by the compilers to explain partial human apprehension of the Way.

[^j13-inheritance]: The editorial argument turns on 繼 as continuation and succession, spanning what is entrusted, 付, and what is received, 受. “Continuing the parental line” renders 繼體, preserving the kinship analogy. The statement about not hearing the Master on nature and Heaven’s Way alludes to the Analects; here it is a rhetorical contrast with this passage’s fullness, not a new proof of the Appended Statements’ authorship.

[^j13-partial-vision]: The humane and the wise here are people with partial strengths, not two fully realized sages. Their limitations are distinguished from the common people’s unexamined daily participation. Zhu expressly reconciles the changed yin–yang assignments by distinguishing clarity/turbidity from movement/stillness. The translation therefore preserves the apparent reversal rather than standardizing it away.

[^j13-bao-ba]: 保氏八 is retained as Bao Ba. The book’s own commentator index lists 保氏 八 公孟 普庵, confirming that 八 is part of the name as transmitted here, not a count or an accidental intrusion. No conjectural personal-name substitution is made.

[^j13-manifest-hidden]: The subject is the Way or creative transformation, not a newly introduced person. 用 is “working” here; 業 is accomplished work. Zhu and Wu explain why the completion of work is associated with storage and concealment, whereas flourishing virtue is associated with outward generation. The main sentence retains that counterintuitive relation instead of replacing it with an easier but different inside/outside scheme.

[^j13-without-intention]: 無心 does not simply mean unconsciousness, and 無為 does not mean doing nothing. The contrast is between the uncontrived operation of Heaven and Earth and the sage’s consciously caring but unforced action. “Deliberate” and “through contrivance” clarify this local contrast; the phrases should not be turned into a single definition applicable mechanically to every philosophical school.

[^j13-wang-kaichong]: 王氏凱沖 is Wang Kaichong, as in this book’s commentator index. The index itself says his chronological placement is uncertain and that his words appear in Li Dingzuo’s collected explanations. The English does not identify him with another similarly named Wang or assert a more precise biography.

[^j13-manifest-reading]: The base prints 顯渚仁 at the beginning of this Zhu quotation. The parallel Siku transcription reads 顯諸仁, matching the canonical sentence. The supported correction gives “manifestation in humaneness”; 渚, an islet, has no place in this construction.

[^j13-four-beginnings]: Compassion, shame and aversion, deference, and discrimination of right and wrong are the four moral beginnings invoked in the Mencian tradition. Zhu here gathers them under compassion’s differentiated activity rather than treating the words as interchangeable. 一事各成一仁 is kept as each affair realizing a particular humaneness; it is not silently changed to 理, “principle,” although Zhu uses that word in the next sentence.

[^j13-possession-renewal]: 富有 here means plenitude—nothing lacking—not private wealth. 日新 is continual renewal, not novelty pursued for its own sake. 業 is rendered “work” because the commentators explicitly mean what has been accomplished; 德 is the realized efficacy called virtue. Their dimensions of fullness and renewal recall Chapter One’s greatness and endurance.

[^j13-generating]: 生生 is unceasing generation: life and transformation issuing anew, not only reproduction of organisms. Zhu explains it as yin generating yang and yang generating yin, and applies it both to the world’s principle and to the book. The repetition in Chinese gives the process its continuing force.

[^j13-images-patterns]: 效 is explicitly glossed 呈, “present” or “display,” not merely imitate. 法 here means the detailed, visible pattern of formation, not a legal code. Qian’s images and Kun’s embodied patterns continue the opening chapter’s distinction between celestial images and terrestrial forms.

[^j13-numbers-action]: The numerical language concerns divination and the yin–yang transformations represented by the stalk procedure, not modern statistical inference. 通 is rendered as carrying change through to free passage, rather than only understanding it, because the text turns from consultation to action. Zhu’s unusual assignment of the still-undecided affair to yang and the decided affair to yin is retained exactly; it must not be reversed to fit a preconceived active/passive scheme.

[^j13-zhang-zhongding]: Zhang Zhongding is Zhang Yong, named by his posthumous title. The anecdote in the primary collection 五朝名臣言行録 explains that an official matter belongs to yang before its words are written down and to yin afterward: before determination it can be adapted; afterward names and decisions must be maintained. See 五朝名臣言行録, “尚書張忠定公” (https://www.shidianguji.com/zh/book/SBCK042/chapter/SBCK042_19), and Hui Dong’s quotation of the anecdote in 周易本義辨證, juan 5 (https://www.eee-learning.com/book/6121). Zhu invokes this as an analogy, not a new yarrow-stalk rule.

[^j13-gu-limit]: The compact 變不與時偕極 occurs in both consulted transcriptions. “Change does not reach a final limit together with the time” keeps that wording; its precise relation to the ensuing 通之 is less explicit than the surrounding glosses. No unattested replacement of 極 or insertion of a missing word has been adopted. The overall contrast is between a situation’s limit and renewed scope for action through change.

[^j13-both-present]: Master Zhang and Hengqu are Zhang Zai. 兩在 is rendered “present in both,” following Zhu’s ensuing account of the one circulating through yin and yang; it can also suggest both poles being present within the unity. Zhu’s more extensive discussion in 朱子語類, juan 98 (https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/朱子語類/098), distinguishes unity’s numinous efficacy from the successive transformations of the two. No separate supernatural agent is introduced into the English.

[^j13-far-near]: “Still and correct” is not changed to “quiet contemplation”: Zhu explains that principle is already present in concrete things. The compilers map distant, near, and between Heaven and Earth onto fathoming principle, realizing nature, and reaching the mandate. Their horizontal and vertical language organizes the argument, not a modern coordinate system.

[^j13-great-broad]: 大 and 廣 are distinguished as greatness and breadth because the commentators distinguish Qian’s substantial oneness from Kun’s receptive capacity. Each has movement and stillness; Kun is not reduced to inert matter. “Generates greatly” and “generates broadly” preserve the parallel without asserting that the two contributions are identical.

[^j13-odd-even]: 奇 and 耦 refer here to the undivided yang line and the divided yin line, as well as their traditional numerical characterization as odd and even. Zhu moves between diagram, qi, and world. His account of Heaven enclosing Earth and qi issuing through it is reproduced as the cosmological model of the commentary, not silently recast as modern astronomy or geology.

[^j13-matching]: 配 means matching or corresponding, not a claim of literal identity in every respect. Zhu’s gloss includes both Heaven’s Way and human affairs; Wu’s stresses Heaven and Earth’s consummate virtue. 易簡 carries the same ease-and-simplicity sense as in Chapter One, completing the argument’s return to its beginning.

[^j13-final-structure]: “These last three chapters” means Chapters Four–Six, not the entire upper Appended Statements. The compilers read them as an extended exposition of Chapter Two’s images of transformation, day and night, and the Three Ultimates. Their alternation between humaneness/rightness and humaneness/wisdom is preserved; no uniform pair has been substituted.
