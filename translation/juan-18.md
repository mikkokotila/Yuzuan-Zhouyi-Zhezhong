## Sequence of the Hexagrams

#### Introductory Explanations

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:001 -->

**Sequence of the Hexagrams · 序卦傳**[^j18-titles]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:002 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:002 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:003 -->

**Kong Yingda:** Han Kangbo says, “What the Sequence of the Hexagrams makes clear is not the riches within the Changes. It follows the order of the hexagrams, drawing upon their images to illuminate their meanings.” Now examine the sixty-four hexagrams: they form pairs, each related either by reversal or by transformation. Reversal means that viewing a figure from opposite ends produces two hexagrams, as with Difficulty at the Beginning and Unformed Understanding, Waiting and Contention, The Army and Holding Together. Transformation applies when turning a figure over still gives only the same hexagram; its counterpart is then made by changing its lines, as with Qian and Kun, Kan and Li, Great Excess and Nourishment, Inner Sincerity and Small Excess. If the sage, when originally fixing their order, had used the meanings Confucius gives in the Sequence, there would have been no reason for every pair to be related by reversal or transformation. Thus Kangbo’s statement—that it follows the hexagrams’ order and draws upon images to illuminate meanings—is surely not unfounded.[^j18-pairing]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:004 -->

**Master Zhang:** The Sequence describes the hexagrams as following one another. When the sages made the Changes, it necessarily had an order.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:005 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu:** Asked, “Some say the Sequence is not a sage’s work. Is that credible?” He replied, “That is the view of Cheng of Shasui. Earlier scholars said it did not contain the sage’s riches. I think one may say it is not the sage’s finest essence, but one may not say it is not the riches within the Changes. Master Zhou distinguished ‘finest essence’ from ‘riches within’ very clearly. The Sequence is precisely the riches within the Changes: all manner of affairs are intermingled and contained there.”[^j18-essence]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:005 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:006 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu:** Asked, “What is meant by the finest essence of the Changes?” He replied, “Such a passage as ‘the Changes has the Supreme Ultimate, which generates the Two Modes; the Two Modes generate the Four Images; the Four Images generate the eight trigrams.’ This is the finest essence of the Changes.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:006 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:007 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu:** Asked, “The Sequence also reveals waning and waxing, advance and retreat. Surely one cannot say this is not its finest essence?” He replied, “This is precisely all manner of affairs intermingled and contained within it: precisely its riches. Its finest essence is that from a single source things are generated without limit.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:007 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:008 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu:** Asked, “The Upper Classic contains thirty hexagrams and the Lower Classic thirty-four. Why are their numbers unequal?” He replied, “Some hexagrams are paired by direct opposition, others by reversal. Qian and Kun, Kan and Li, Nourishment and Great Excess, Inner Sincerity and Small Excess—these eight are paired by direct opposition. Such figures do not change when reversed; viewed from either end, they remain eight hexagrams. The other fifty-six are paired by reversal. These all change when reversed, so, counted from either end, they amount to twenty-eight figures. Combining the directly opposed figures with those paired by reversal gives thirty-six in all. In the Upper Classic there are six unchanged by reversal: Qian, Kun, Kan, Li, Nourishment, and Great Excess. The twenty-four beginning with Difficulty at the Beginning and Unformed Understanding become twelve under reversal. Twelve plus six makes eighteen. In the Lower Classic there are two unchanged figures, Inner Sincerity and Small Excess. The thirty-two beginning with Influence and Constancy become sixteen under reversal. Sixteen plus two likewise makes eighteen. Their numbers are therefore by no means unequal.”[^j18-eighteen]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:008 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:009 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu:** Asked, “There are one or two puzzling points in the Sequence. Of the sixty-four hexagrams, why is Influence alone not named?” He replied, “The Way of husband and wife is Influence.” Asked, “Is it like the Upper Classic, which does not name Qian and Kun but speaks of Heaven and Earth, making Qian and Kun apparent?” He replied, “Yes.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:009 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:010 -->

**Xiang Anshi:** The origin of the names Upper and Lower Classic has not been established. Yet from the Sequence one can know with certainty that the text was divided into two parts.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:010 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:011 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** The hexagrams must have their own reasons for being ordered as they are; Confucius connects them through their meanings. Those connections suffice to reveal the fullness and emptiness, waning and waxing of Heaven’s Way; the gain and loss, preservation and ruin of human affairs; and the rise and decline, order and disorder of states. Kong’s and Zhu’s explanations are both right. Yet one must understand that, even if another sequence were made, its principles could still be connected throughout. In the stalk method, one hexagram can change into any of the sixty-four. Whatever is encountered, the original and resulting figures can give rise to one another’s meanings. Only thus can the Changes encompass principle and rightness and exhaust the transformations of affairs. Hence “all that can be accomplished in the world is fulfilled.” Confucius uses the order of the Sequence to make the pattern clear: he shows one corner. To understand it with numinous insight is to know that the Way of the Changes circulates everywhere, answering to the time without a fixed course. It is also to know that divinatory methods change and open passages, and can be extended through analogous cases. This meaning reaches the utmost of the Changes’ interconnections.[^j18-zhen-hui]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:011 -->

#### The Upper Classic

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:012 -->

### Once there are Heaven and Earth, the myriad things are born. What fills the space between Heaven and Earth is none other than the myriad things. Thus Difficulty at the Beginning follows. Zhun means fullness.[^j18-sequence-formula]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:012 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:013 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:013 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:014 -->

**Xiang Anshi:** Zhun does not literally mean fullness. At the time of Zhun, firm and yielding first join; Heaven and Earth mingle densely, thunder and rain stir and surge, and their qi is seen filling everything. For this reason it is called fullness. “Fullness” describes its qi; “the first birth of things” its time; “difficulty” its affairs. The meaning of Zhun itself is tangled profusion and interwoven complexity.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:014 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:015 -->

### Zhun is the first birth of things. Things newly born must be unformed; thus Unformed Understanding follows. Meng means being unformed: the youth of things. Young things cannot go unnourished; thus Waiting follows. Xu is the Way of food and drink. Food and drink must give rise to contention; thus Contention follows.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:015 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:016 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:016 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:017 -->

**Kong Yingda:** The earlier statement “Zhun means fullness” explains why Zhun follows Qian and Kun; that explanation is complete. The further statement “Zhun is the first birth of things” introduces what follows: “things newly born must be unformed.” It takes only the meaning of first birth, not a second explanation of Zhun’s name.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:017 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:018 -->

**Zhu Zhen:** Meng is obscurity and unknowing. What is born must begin in obscurity, as do curled shoots, embryos, and eggs; thus Meng follows. Meng is youthful unknowing: things are as young as this.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:018 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:019 -->

**Zhu Zhen:** Food and drink must give rise to contention: dry provisions occasion fault; pork and wine breed calamity. Whatever has blood and qi must have a contentious heart. Thus Contention follows.[^j18-food]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:019 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:020 -->

### Contention must bring a multitude into action; thus The Army follows. Shi means a multitude. A multitude must have something to hold together around; thus Holding Together follows. Bi means holding together.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:020 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:021 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:021 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:022 -->

**Han Kangbo:** If the multitude rises without holding together, there is no means of ending the struggle. Only by drawing close and holding together can it find peace.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:022 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:023 -->

**Xiang Anshi:** The Army and Holding Together are reversals of one another. The Army takes its name from the military groupings wu, liang, zu, lü, shi, and jun. Holding Together takes its name from the residential groupings bi, lü, zu, dang, zhou, and xiang. The Army means setting the multitude right; Holding Together chiefly concerns mutual closeness.[^j18-military-households]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:023 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:024 -->

### Holding together must bring accumulation; thus Small Restraint follows. Once things have accumulated, there is ritual propriety; thus Treading follows. Through treading there is peace, and then security; thus Peace follows.[^j18-treading-peace]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:024 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:025 -->

**Original Meaning.** Master Chao says that Zheng’s text lacks the two characters “and there is peace.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:025 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:026 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:026 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:027 -->

**Yao Xin:** Nothing is better than ritual propriety for securing those above and governing the people. Only with ritual propriety is there peace; only with peace is there security.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:027 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:028 -->

**Xiang Anshi:** Lü does not literally mean ritual propriety. Yet what people tread in their conduct is never outside ritual. What lies outside ritual is not where one ought to tread; thus Lü is explained as possessing ritual propriety. Heaven above and lake below also express ritual’s names and distinctions.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:028 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:029 -->

**Hu Yigui:** From Qian and Kun through Treading are ten changes: yin and yang qi have completed one circuit.

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:030 -->

### Tai means free passage. Things cannot pass freely forever; thus Obstruction follows. Things cannot remain obstructed forever; thus Fellowship follows. Things must gather around one who joins in fellowship with others; thus Great Possession follows. One who possesses greatness must not become full of oneself; thus Modesty follows. One who possesses greatness and can be modest must find ease; thus Enthusiasm follows.[^j18-ease-and-work]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:030 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:031 -->

**Original Meaning.** Guo Yong says: Possessing greatness with modesty cuts off the burden of self-satisfaction. Thus one moves freely, without pressure, at leisure and at ease.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:031 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:032 -->

### Ease must bring a following; thus Following follows. One who gladly follows others must have work to undertake; thus Decay follows. Gu means affairs to be attended to. Only through undertaking affairs can there be greatness; thus Approach follows. Lin means greatness.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:032 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:033 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:033 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:034 -->

**Han Kangbo:** Work capable of greatness arises from undertaking affairs.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:034 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:035 -->

**Zhu Zhen:** Gladly following others must entail something to serve: the minister serves his ruler, the son his father, the wife her husband, and the pupil his teacher. Without delight in what one serves, would one be willing to follow?[^j18-service-punctuation]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:035 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:036 -->

**Xiang Anshi:** Gu does not literally mean affairs. When things decay, a multitude of affairs arises. Work arises because of decay; thus Gu is placed before the work.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:036 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:037 -->

**Xiang Anshi:** Lin does not literally mean greatness. The great approach the lowly from above, and the small from a position of greatness. Whatever is called approaching in this sense is the business of the great; thus it is explained through greatness. By contrast, “Feng means greatness” is a genuine definition of the word.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:037 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:038 -->

**Wu Cheng:** It is through the work required by Decay that Approach’s flourishing greatness becomes possible.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:038 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:039 -->

### Once things are great, they can be viewed; thus Viewing follows. Once they can be viewed, there can be union; thus Biting Through follows. He means joining. Things cannot merely join without due form; thus Adornment follows. Bi means adornment. Once adornment is carried to its utmost, free passage is exhausted; thus Splitting Apart follows. Bo means stripping away.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:039 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:040 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:040 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:041 -->

**Cui Jing:** Those great in virtue and work can serve as a sight for others to behold.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:041 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:042 -->

**Su Shi:** The relationships between ruler and minister, father and son, husband and wife, and friends are what is meant by joining. Acting straight from one’s feelings is joining without due form; adorning feeling through ritual is adornment. Without form, joining is easy; ease breeds mutual disrespect. Mutual disrespect makes separation easy. With adornment, joining is difficult; its difficulty brings mutual respect, and mutual respect allows the relationship to endure. But when adornment reaches an extreme, form prevails and substance declines: hence Splitting Apart.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:042 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:043 -->

**Zhang Shi:** Adornment prizes form. When form is excessive, it in turn diminishes substance and obstructs free passage. Thus carrying adornment to its utmost brings free passage to an end.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:043 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:044 -->

### Things cannot remain exhausted forever. Splitting Apart reaches its limit above and returns below; thus Return follows. With return, one no longer acts falsely; thus Without Falsity follows. Only when there is no falsity can there be accumulation; thus Great Restraint follows.[^j18-bo-punctuation]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:044 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:045 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:045 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:046 -->

**Cui Jing:** When things return to their root, they become sincere and real. Thus it says that with return there is no falsity.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:046 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:047 -->

**Master Zhou:** A movement that is not good is falsity. Returning from falsity, one is without falsity. Without falsity, one is sincere. Thus Without Falsity follows Return.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:047 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:048 -->

**Guo Zhongxiao:** Vigor is Heaven’s virtue. Great Restraint stills vigor: it stores up Heaven’s virtue. Thus it says, “Firm and vigorous, substantial and true, shining with brilliance, daily renewing its virtue.” Without storing up Heaven’s virtue, what appears in one’s activity cannot be free of falsity. Thus Heaven’s virtue is still in Great Restraint and active in Without Falsity.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:048 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:049 -->

**Yan Yansheng:** Only without falsity can one accumulate. What is accumulated is virtue; thus it is called great.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:049 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:050 -->

**Yu Qishu:** From undertaking affairs comes greatness; from greatness, something to be viewed; from being viewed, union; from union, adornment. This is what is called the thinning of wholeheartedness and trustworthiness and the beginning of falsity. Thus a single change produces Splitting Apart. When splitting is followed by return, the genuine alone remains, without falsity.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:050 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:051 -->

**He Kai:** Not acting falsely and being without falsity must be distinguished. Only by not acting falsely can one become free of falsity.[^j18-not-false]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:051 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:052 -->

### Once things have been accumulated, they can nourish; thus Nourishment follows. Yi means nourishing. Without nourishment there can be no action; thus Great Excess follows. Things cannot remain excessive forever; thus Repeated Danger follows. Kan means entrapment. What is trapped must have something to cling to; thus Clinging follows. Li means clinging.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:052 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:053 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:053 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:054 -->

**Su Shi:** What is nourished but not employed must ultimately move. Movement without cease must ultimately go too far.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:054 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:055 -->

**Yan Yansheng:** Nourishment is how the noble person completes himself; action is how he responds to things. Yet in repose he stands in the mean, and in action he follows the mean. How could he seek to surpass things? When responding to change, however, there are times when he goes beyond the ordinary. Thus Great Excess follows.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:055 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:056 -->

**Lin Xiyuan:** Without single-minded concentration one cannot proceed directly to fulfillment; without gathering inward one cannot issue outward. Thus there must be nourishment before action is possible: without nourishment there can be no action. Mencius says, “Only when there are things a person will not do can that person do something.” This is precisely the principle. Thus Great Excess follows. Great Excess is action: possessing its capacity, meeting its time, and undertaking its affairs. Such action has its root in nourishment.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:056 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:057 -->

**Jiang Bao:** Without nourishment, one’s foundation is not established, and one cannot act to meet great affairs. Only when nourishment is full and action follows will that action greatly surpass what others can do.

#### The Lower Classic

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:058 -->

### Once there are Heaven and Earth, there are the myriad things; once there are the myriad things, there are male and female; once there are male and female, there are husband and wife; once there are husband and wife, there are father and son; once there are father and son, there are ruler and minister; once there are ruler and minister, there are higher and lower; once there are higher and lower, ritual propriety and rightness have their place. The Way of husband and wife cannot but endure; thus Constancy follows. Heng means endurance.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:058 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:059 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:059 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:060 -->

**Gan Bao:** This explains fully how the Three Bonds and Six Orders of the human Way have their origin. Human beings possess the natures of male and female, yin and yang; thus there is naturally the Way of husband and wife joining together. Yin and yang transform and generate, blood and bodily form passing from one generation to the next; thus there is naturally the closeness of father and son. The ruler is established on the model of the father, the minister on the model of the son; thus the positions of ruler and minister must arise. With those positions comes an ordering of higher and lower. Given higher and lower positions, ritual must establish their form, and rightness determine what is fitting. This shows that the former kings’ institutions were drawn from the actual dispositions of human relationships. The Upper Classic begins with Qian and Kun, the root of life; the Lower Classic with Influence and Constancy, the beginning of the human Way. When the Changes arose, Yin’s last age suffered Daji’s calamity, while Zhou’s flourishing virtue owed much to the achievements of its three mothers. This says that Heaven cannot generate without Earth, nor husband bring things to completion without wife. Their utmost mutual need is the beginning of kingly teaching. Thus the Odes places Guanju at the beginning of the Airs of the States, and the Changes fully discusses in Influence and Constancy the origins of ritual and rightness.[^j18-kinship]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:060 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:061 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu:** Asked, “In ‘ritual propriety and rightness have their place,’ Master Lu gives two pronunciations for cuo. How should it be read?” He replied, “Simply as cuo meaning to place: ritual and rightness have somewhere to be put into effect.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:061 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:062 -->

**Wu Cheng:** This explains why Influence begins the Lower Classic. Husband and wife mean the hexagram Influence. The preceding references to Heaven, Earth, the myriad things, and male and female describe what makes husband and wife possible. The following references to father and son, ruler and minister, higher and lower describe what husband and wife bring about. When husband and wife exist, their offspring establish the relation of father and son. Passing from household to state, people are not literally father and son, yet the distinction between honored ruler and subordinate minister resembles that relation. Passing from state to the world, people are not all ruler and minister, yet the distinction between those above and below resembles theirs. Ritual and rightness distinguish the ranks of honored and subordinate, high and low. Cuo means placing. Qian, Kun, and Influence are not named as hexagrams because, as the opening figures of the Upper and Lower Classics, they are spoken of in a special manner.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:062 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:063 -->

Things cannot remain strong forever; thus Advance follows. Advancing must bring injury; thus Darkening of the Light follows. Yi means injury. Those injured outside must return to their family; thus The Family follows.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:065 -->

How could someone injured in Darkening of the Light fail to return to the family?

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:066 -->

Jian means difficulty. Things cannot remain in difficulty forever; thus Release follows. Xie means relaxation.[^j18-release]

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:069 -->

**Master Zhou:** Division in the family must begin with the women. Thus Opposition follows The Family: two women dwell together, but their intentions do not follow the same course.

**Conversations of Master Zhu:** Asked, “I suspect huan does not mean slowness here, but slackness. Is this why it says ‘Release means relaxation’?” He replied, “It means looseness.” Asked, “As in letting things become lax?” He replied, “Yes.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:071 -->

**Xiang Anshi:** Zhun is always spoken of as difficulty, and Jian is also called difficulty, because both hexagrams contain Kan. Yet Zhun moves within danger: it proceeds through hardship. Jian sees danger and stops: it is simply obstructed and unable to advance, not immersed in hardship in the same sense. Thus one situated in Zhun must organize and order affairs to bring relief; one encountering Jian waits for the obstruction to loosen before advancing.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:072 -->

Guai means breaking through. Breaking through must bring an encounter; thus Coming to Meet follows. Gou means encountering.[^j18-breach]

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:074 -->

**Zhu Zhen:** Prolonged increase must fill things up; fullness must cause a breach, as with an embankment. Thus Breakthrough follows.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:075 -->

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:076 -->

<a id="sequence-general"></a>

#### General Discussion

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:110 -->

**General Discussion — Wang Tong, in the Zhongshuo:** Praising the Changes, on reaching the Sequence he said, “Great indeed is the way times give birth to one another! One who comprehends it may engage with incipient change.” On reaching the Miscellaneous Hexagrams, he said, “Moving in every direction without drifting away: one who holds fast may preserve rightness.”[^j18-wang-tong]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:110 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:111 -->

**Master Shao:** Qian and Kun are the root of Heaven and Earth; Kan and Li their activity. Thus the Changes begins with Qian and Kun, reaches its middle in Kan and Li, and ends with After Completion and Before Completion. Peace and Obstruction stand in the midst of the Upper Classic, Influence and Constancy at the beginning of the Lower. All speak of their activity.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:111 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:112 -->

**Master Shao:** Qian, Kun, Kan, and Li provide the activity of the Upper Part; Dui, Gen, Zhen, and Xun provide that of the Lower. Nourishment, Inner Sincerity, Great Excess, and Small Excess are the upright figures of the two parts.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:112 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:113 -->

**Master Shao:** From Qian and Kun through Kan and Li, the subject is Heaven’s Way. From Influence and Constancy through After Completion and Before Completion, the subject is human affairs.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:113 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:114 -->

**Master Cheng — On the Meaning of the Upper and Lower Parts:** Qian and Kun are the Way of Heaven and Earth, the root of yin and yang; thus they begin the Upper Part. Kan and Li are yin and yang taking substantial form; thus they end the Upper Part. Influence and Constancy are the Way of husband and wife, the root of generation and nurture; thus they begin the Lower Part. Before Completion combines Kan and Li; After Completion brings Kan and Li into intercourse. Their combination and intercourse generate things: this is yin and yang’s accomplished work. Thus they end the Lower Part. Once the hexagrams had been divided between the two parts, their meanings were developed to provide their sequence: this is the Sequence of the Hexagrams. The division is based on yin and yang: those in which yang flourishes belong above, those in which yin flourishes below. This flourishing may be determined from the trigrams or from the lines. Trigrams and lines are interpreted differently. In Splitting Apart, considered through its trigrams, yin grows and yang is stripped away. Considered through its lines, yang reaches the top, and a single yang governs the many yin. In Great Strength, considered as a hexagram, yang grows strong; considered through its lines, yin flourishes above. Each interpretation applies in its own place; they do not impair one another.[^j18-cheng-classification]

Qian is the father: none stands higher. Kun is the mother: none but Qian can rival her. Thus hexagrams containing Qian belong to the Upper Part, and those containing Kun to the Lower. Yet in Return yang is born, in Approach it grows, in Viewing it flourishes, and in Splitting Apart it reaches its utmost. Thus, although these contain Kun, they belong above. In Coming to Meet yin is born, in Retreat it grows, in Great Strength it flourishes, and in Breakthrough it reaches its utmost. Thus, although these contain Qian, they belong below. The remaining hexagrams containing Qian all belong to the Upper Part: Peace and Obstruction, Waiting and Contention, Small Restraint and Treading, Fellowship and Great Possession, Without Falsity and Great Restraint. Those containing Kun but placed in the Upper Part all have one yang. When a hexagram has five yin and one yang, the one yang governs them; thus all one-yang hexagrams belong above: The Army and Modesty, Enthusiasm and Holding Together, Return and Splitting Apart. The remaining hexagrams containing Kun belong below: Advance and Darkening of the Light, Gathering and Pushing Upward.

Hexagrams with one yin and five yang all contain Qian; their yang is also numerous and flourishing. Though the many yang delight in one yin, they merely delight in it: this is not like one yang governing the many yin. Wang Bi’s claim that the one yin governs them is wrong. Thus the one-yin hexagrams all belong above: Small Restraint and Treading, Fellowship and Great Possession. Among hexagrams with two yang, those containing Kun belong below. Small Excess does not contain Kun, but it is a hexagram in which yin exceeds; it too belongs below. The other two-yang hexagrams all have a yang born below that reaches above, and both their constituent trigrams are yang. Yang flourishes, so they all belong above: Difficulty at the Beginning, Unformed Understanding, Nourishment, and Repeated Danger. “Yang born below” means Zhen or Kan is below: Zhen begins at the bottom, Kan in the middle. “Reaching above” means a yang reaches the top or obtains its correct position. Born below and reaching above, yang unfolds abundantly. If yang is born below but does not reach above, while yin is numerous and yang few and yang also loses its correct position, yang is weak: this describes Shock and Release. If there is yang above but none below, it lacks a root: this describes Keeping Still and Limping. Zhen, Kan, and Gen are yang when considered as trigrams, but through their lines each expresses a first, slight change. Zhen has no yang above, Gen none below, and in Kan yang is trapped: none of these is flourishing. In doubled Kan alone, yang reaches above; thus it is flourishing.

Among hexagrams with two yin, those containing Qian plainly have flourishing yang: Waiting and Contention, Great Restraint and Without Falsity. Those without Qian in which yang nevertheless flourishes are Great Excess and Clinging. In Great Excess yang flourishes in the middle, and the yin above and below are weak. When yang occupies top and bottom, it provides the framework for yin: this is Nourishment. Yin occupying top and bottom cannot govern yang; instead it is weak. Only when there are two yin above and two below, with just two yang in the middle, does yin prevail: this is Small Excess. The names Great Excess and Small Excess make this apparent. In Clinging, both constituent trigrams have yang above and below, with yin attaching to them: yang flourishes. In the other two-yin hexagrams, both constituent trigrams are yin, and yin flourishes. All belong below: The Family and Opposition, Revolution and The Cauldron, Penetration and Joy, and Inner Sincerity.

In hexagrams of three yin and three yang the two are equal; precedence is therefore determined by their meaning. The distinction between yin and yang, honored and subordinate, and the ordering of male and female, elder and younger, are Heaven and Earth’s great constant principles. When a younger male stands above an older female, yang prevails. In Decay the youngest yang stands above the eldest yin; in Adornment the youngest son stands above the middle daughter. Both have flourishing yang. Though Kan is a yang trigram, its yang is submerged and trapped by yin. Paired with a yin trigram, it gives flourishing yin. Thus figures with equal yin and yang that contain Kan all belong below: Oppression and The Well, Dispersion and Limitation, After Completion and Before Completion. Someone asks, “When one trigram is Kan, yang is considered trapped. Why, when both are Kan, is yang instead considered flourishing?” The reply: “With one Kan, yang is trapped by yin and is also paired with a yin trigram. With two Kans, yang is born below and reaches above; moreover, both trigrams are yang. It may therefore be called flourishing.”

A male above a female is the ordinary order, not yet flourishing. If yang loses its correct position and yin instead occupies the honored place, yang is weak. Thus Constancy, Decrease, The Marrying Maiden, and Abundance all belong below. A female above a male means yin prevails; every figure with the female above belongs below: Influence, Increase, Gradual Progress, The Wanderer, Oppression, Dispersion, and Before Completion. Only in Following and Biting Through does the male place himself below the female; this is not the female overcoming the male. Thus Following’s Judgment Commentary says, “Firmness comes and places itself below yieldingness”; Biting Through’s says, “Yieldingness obtains the center and moves upward.” The eldest yang cannot be matched by a younger yin. Because the eldest son places himself below the middle or youngest daughter, he is said to lower himself to her. But if their ages match and their powers are equal, yin above overbears, while yang below is weak: Influence and Increase are such cases. Influence also has the image of placing oneself below the female, but not of the elder lowering himself to the younger. Rather, the two youngest influence and join with one another, allowing the female to overbear. Hence the warning that steadfastness in what is right is beneficial. In Oppression the female is younger than the male, yet yang is trapped and covered by yin: there is no meaning of willingly lowering oneself to the other.

Someone asks, “Small Excess has two yang amid four yin and is therefore called flourishing yin. Why is Inner Sincerity, with two yin amid four yang, not called flourishing yang?” The reply: “Yang’s constitution is solid; Inner Sincerity is empty at the center.” “Then are the four yin within Nourishment not empty?” “Nourishment’s two constituent trigrams are both yang; its root and tip are both yang: this is the utmost of yang’s flourishing. Inner Sincerity’s two constituent trigrams are both yin. Its two yang above and two below do not form the image of root and tip. Because its center is empty it is called Inner Sincerity. That yin flourishes in it is therefore evident.”[^j18-cheng-ending]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:114 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:115 -->

**Xiang Anshi:** The Upper Classic speaks of Heaven and Earth generating the myriad things, qi flowing into form. Thus it begins with Qian and Kun and ends with Kan and Li, explaining the root of transformation through qi. The Lower Classic speaks of things generating one another, form transmitting qi. Thus it begins with Influence and Constancy and ends with After Completion and Before Completion, explaining the Way of husband and wife.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4710:115 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4710:116 -->

**Cai Qing:** Some connections in the Sequence are reversals; others are continuations. A reversal is change after reaching the utmost; a continuation occurs before the utmost has been reached. The whole falls within these two patterns.

### Great Restraint is timeliness; Without Falsity, calamity.[^j18-time-calamity]

On the left of the Earlier Heaven diagram, these yield eight hexagrams: Return, Nourishment, After Completion, The Family, The Marrying Maiden, Opposition, Breakthrough, and Qian. ## Translator’s Endnotes

The base is the repository’s unchanged 易學網 text. Selective comparison used [the Siku Quanshu transcription, fixed revision 528619](<https://zh.wikisource.org/w/index.php?title=御纂周易折中_(四庫全書本)/卷18&oldid=528619>), additional primary texts, and the historical page images identified below. Attested corrections, repunctuation, contextual repairs, and unresolved readings are distinguished. No proposed rearrangement of the final eight hexagrams has been adopted. This is not a systematic facsimile collation.

[^j18-titles]: 序卦傳 is the Sequence of the Hexagrams; 雜卦傳 is the Miscellaneous Hexagrams. The latter rearranges and contrasts the figures; “miscellaneous” does not mean random or inconsequential. This juan contains both Wings and all their accompanying commentary. Hexagram titles follow Juans One–Eight, while the explanations distinguish a title’s ordinary meaning from a meaning drawn from the situation it depicts. Traditional attributions are translated as the commentators make them, not independently established here.

[^j18-pairing]: Kong distinguishes 覆, turning a six-line figure upside down, from 變, replacing each yin line with yang and each yang line with yin. For example, reversing Zhun gives Meng. Qian, Kun, Kan, Li, Nourishment, Great Excess, Inner Sincerity, and Small Excess are unchanged by reversal and are paired by changing all their lines instead. The terminology 反、對、正對、反對 varies in the later quotations; the operation described, rather than the English label alone, must determine the meaning.

[^j18-essence]: 精 and 蘊 are deliberately distinguished as “finest essence” and “riches within.” Zhu rejects the inference that an explanation is worthless because it does not state the generative principle in its simplest form. His reference to Master Zhou is to Zhou Dunyi; Cheng of Shasui is Cheng Jiong, not one of the two Cheng brothers.

[^j18-eighteen]: Zhu counts figures up to reversal, keeping the eight reversal-invariant figures individually. The Upper Classic gives 24/2 + 6 = 18; the Lower Classic gives 32/2 + 2 = 18. Altogether this is 56/2 + 8 = 36. These are counts under an explicitly stated equivalence, not a claim that the received text has only thirty-six hexagrams.

[^j18-zhen-hui]: 貞 and 悔 in the compilers’ account of divination denote the original and resulting hexagrams, not “steadfastness” and “regret” as verdicts. Their point is that interpretation must find a meaningful relation between any cast figure and its transformation; the received sequence illustrates such relation without exhausting it. “Showing one corner” alludes to the teaching image of inferring the other three from one.

[^j18-sequence-formula]: 故受之以 is rendered “Thus … follows”: each succeeding hexagram receives or takes over the situation just described. The repeated 必, “must” or “necessarily,” expresses the text’s pattern of development, not a modern statistical law. Qian and Kun are represented by Heaven and Earth without being named; Influence is likewise represented later by the relationship of husband and wife.

[^j18-food]: Zhu Zhen’s “dry provisions occasion fault” recalls the Odes, [Famu](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/詩經/伐木), where failure in friendship can arise even over ordinary provisions. 豕酒 is translated literally as pork and wine; no specific historical episode has been confidently identified here. The commentary’s universal claim about contention belongs to its interpretation of the sequence, not an empirical rule about eating.

[^j18-military-households]: Xiang preserves two different institutional series. Wu, liang, zu, lü, shi, and jun are successive military groupings; bi, lü, zu, dang, zhou, and xiang are successive residential groupings. The Chinese for the two lü and two zu differs. The translation retains the names rather than forcing exact equivalences with modern military or administrative ranks. The Chinese series are 伍、兩、卒、旅、師、軍 and 比、閭、族、黨、州、鄉.

[^j18-treading-peace]: Zhu reports Chao’s statement that Zheng’s text omits 而泰, “and there is peace.” The received base wording is retained in the main translation; this historical omission is not silently adopted. Xiang distinguishes 履, treading or conduct, from 禮, ritual propriety: the former is explained through the latter but is not simply a dictionary synonym.

[^j18-ease-and-work]: 豫 is translated as the hexagram title Enthusiasm, but in this sequence its contextual sense is ease or glad readiness. 蠱 likewise bears the project title Decay while being explained through 事, work or affairs arising from decay. Xiang repeatedly distinguishes a lexical definition from an explanation of a hexagram’s situation. These differences have not been flattened into uniform English synonyms.

[^j18-service-punctuation]: Zhu Zhen’s sentence is repunctuated 臣事君，子事父，婦事夫，弟子事師: minister serves ruler, son father, wife husband, and pupil teacher. The base’s breaks after 臣事、君子事、父婦事、夫弟子事 and before 師 split the parallel clauses incorrectly. The parallel transcription confirms the same character sequence; no character substitution is needed.

[^j18-bo-punctuation]: At the transition to Return, the English reads 物不可以終盡。剝窮上反下: things cannot remain exhausted; Splitting Apart reaches its limit above and returns below. This is repunctuation of the transmitted characters, not an emendation of them. The base instead breaks after 剝. The return to the bottom also evokes yang’s passage from Splitting Apart’s top line to Return’s first line.

[^j18-not-false]: He Kai distinguishes the active 不妄, not acting falsely, from 无妄, being without falsity. His base phrase 由不以妄 is corrected to 由不妄, following the parallel transcription. The distinction is retained rather than replacing both expressions with the single hexagram title. Jiang Bao (姜寶), cited below, is not Gan Bao (干寶), who appears at the opening of the Lower Classic discussion.

[^j18-kinship]: The opening of the Lower Classic treats the marital pair as the basis of family, government, and ritual order. Gan Bao’s Three Bonds and Six Orders are a traditional vocabulary of hierarchical relationships, not six hexagram lines. His contrast of Daji with the Zhou’s three exemplary mothers, and Zhou Dunyi’s subsequent claim about women and household division, belong to the commentators’ gendered moral history; they are preserved as their claims, not adopted as established explanations of social events. The Guanju ode is named with 關雎; the base’s 關睢 is a graphic error. The title is confirmed by the separate [Guanju text](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/詩經/關雎); this is a title normalization, not a claim that a different Zhezhong witness has been found.

[^j18-returning-home]: “Returning to the family” has both a social and a cultivation reading here: Yan contrasts natural kinship with association for profit, while Guo reads return as turning the inquiry back upon oneself. He Kai distinguishes Jin, Advance, preceded by Great Strength, from Jian, Gradual Progress, preceded by Keeping Still. The English preserves those different routes rather than treating the two titles as interchangeable.

[^j18-release]: 緩 is rendered “relaxation,” embracing release from difficulty and the slackening that can then cause loss. Zhu explicitly rules out mere slowness and accepts the sense of looseness. Xiang also distinguishes Zhun’s active engagement within danger from Jian’s being stopped by danger; “difficulty” is not identical in their explanations.

[^j18-breach]: 決 is rendered “breaking through” in the canonical chain, while Zhu Zhen makes its hydraulic sense explicit: a full embankment bursts. Yu Yan’s deep valley becoming a height and high bank becoming a valley echo the Odes, Shiyue zhi jiao; the image explains reversal, not a mechanical guarantee that every personal loss will be followed by gain. See the Odes, [Shiyue zhi jiao](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/詩經/十月之交), 高岸為谷，深谷為陵.

[^j18-vessels]: “Presiding over the vessels” evokes the cauldron and the heir’s responsibility for ancestral ritual. The eldest son is Zhen’s family image. 歸 links return, finding a destination or allegiance, and a woman’s marriage; the compilers deliberately extend the relation to a minister finding a ruler. The English retains these shifts rather than translating every occurrence as an ordinary journey home.

[^j18-zhang-shi]: The base attributes the gloss on the Wanderer to 張氏械, an apparent misprint. The parallel Siku transcription reads 張氏栻, Zhang Shi, matching the surrounding attributions. The English adopts that supported correction. The reflexive pronoun 己 in nearby discussions is also restored where the base prints 已.

[^j18-trust-excess]: Han Kangbo invokes 貞而不諒, “steadfast in what is right, not bound by indiscriminate fidelity.” Thus a good quality can become excess when held without judgment. Xiang reads 有其信 as claiming or relying upon one’s own trustworthiness; Cai likewise warns against acting without further examination. These are their readings of the transition from Inner Sincerity to Small Excess, not a blanket condemnation of sincerity.

[^j18-ending-open]: The text’s final 未濟 keeps the sequence open. Xiang’s account of union followed by separation draws a further analogy with Influence followed by Constancy: enduring relations require appropriate distinctions as well as feeling. “Separation” here should not automatically be read as a recommendation to terminate a marriage or other relationship.

[^j18-wang-tong]: Wang Tong’s Zhongshuo is cited for paired appraisals of the two Wings. 幾 is incipient change, the first stirrings before an event becomes fully manifest. 存義 is preserving rightness. The phrasing recalls the Wenyan’s account of knowing what is approaching and carrying an undertaking through to completion.

[^j18-cheng-classification]: This long essay explains the Upper/Lower division by a layered notion of yang or yin “flourishing”: the number and position of lines, the sex and relative age of the constituent trigrams, and the relation between them all enter. A “yang trigram” means Qian or one of the three sons, not necessarily a trigram with a numerical majority of yang lines. The essay’s later statements that “all” one-yin figures, or all Upper figures containing Kun, have a certain placement must be read with its earlier named exceptions. Coming to Meet and Breakthrough, for example, were explicitly assigned to the Lower Part. The argument is translated in full, not silently regularized into one counting rule.

[^j18-cheng-ending]: Near the end of Cheng’s essay, both transcriptions read 中乎中虛. The [historical page image 1882](https://www.eee-learning.com/eeebook/middleyi/1882.png) reads 中孚中虛: Inner Sincerity is empty at the center. The English follows the page image. In the same passage, 則陰在上為陵陽在下為弱 is repunctuated as 則陰在上為陵，陽在下為弱: yin above overbears, yang below is weak. These are respectively a facsimile-supported graphic correction and a punctuation decision. Neither changes the Chinese source file.

[^j18-misc-reversal]: The first quoted conversation contains a problem shared by both transcriptions and [page image 1883](https://www.eee-learning.com/eeebook/middleyi/1883.png): it says that turning Gen, Dui, Zhen, and Xun over gives Inner Sincerity, Nourishment, Small Excess, and Great Excess. Simple reversal cannot do that. Gen reverses to Zhen, and Dui to Xun; their doubled six-line figures likewise reverse among themselves. The wording is retained as a problematic report, not converted into an invented procedure. It may compress a further discussion of compound figures, but that cannot be securely reconstructed from this passage alone.

[^j18-twenty-eight]: The base, the parallel Siku transcription, and page image 1883 all read 二十四, twenty-four, before saying that reversal yields fifty-six figures. The English reads twenty-eight as an explicit contextual correction: 28 × 2 = 56. Both the next source quotation and the earlier Upper/Lower calculation give twenty-eight. This is not an alternative reading recovered from another Zhezhong witness. The analogies that follow concern expanding a trigram’s strokes into six-line figures, not ordinary reversal.

[^j18-ancient-glosses]: Long Renfu cites brief hexagram glosses from the Spring and Autumn commentary tradition as examples of older divinatory usage. His point is that a one-word gloss need not be a complete definition of a hexagram; it can preserve a particular traditional association. His claim that Confucius preserved rather than invented this material remains his claim.

[^j18-giving-seeking]: Zhu’s first explanation assigns giving to the one who approaches and seeking to those who come to view. The alternative and Guo Yong’s gloss allow both giving and seeking in each hexagram. The main sentence retains the open 或與或求, rather than identifying one hexagram exclusively with giving and the other with seeking.

[^j18-child-clarity]: Su Shi’s 童明 is shared by the two Zhezhong transcriptions. “Childlike clarity” is a provisional close rendering; the precise expression remains uncertain. No unattested substitution of 蒙 or another graph is made. His contrast is between the still-undifferentiated condition and the goodness or understanding already capable of becoming manifest.

[^j18-turning-points]: Qian Zhili treats Decrease/Increase and Obstruction/Peace as paired turning points rather than isolated verdicts. The twelve-hexagram counts here are inclusive: Qian through Obstruction, and Influence through Increase. In the Miscellaneous order, ten figures run from Holding Together/The Army through Decrease/Increase, and ten from Influence/Constancy through Obstruction/Peace. This differs from counting the ten transitions mentioned earlier by Hu Yigui.

[^j18-time-calamity]: 時 is rendered “timeliness,” the apt moment of action after capacity has been cultivated. 災 is calamity arriving from outside even without wrongdoing; 眚 is rendered “fault” in Guo’s quotation because he contrasts it with self-caused harm. The text distinguishes these ideas without implying that actual suffering always reveals a sufferer’s moral character. The ox allusion is to Without Falsity’s third line; the heavenly highway to Great Restraint’s top line.

[^j18-colorless]: “Without color” is deliberately retained as the paradoxical description of Adornment. Zhu’s “white receives colors” and Guo’s reference to the white adornment of the top line explain it through a plain ground or intact substance, not through hostility to all beauty. The compilers apply the same measure to food and clothing: each should fulfill its purpose without excess.

[^j18-old-and-repair]: 故 in this cluster is read as what is old or previously fixed, following Yu Yan and the compilers. They apply it to inherited views that prevent one from following another. Zhu’s terse 隨前无故，蠱後當飭 contrasts freedom from prior encumbrance with subsequent repair; the exact temporal force of 前 is compressed. Later 多故 under Abundance instead means many affairs or complications, not many old opinions.

[^j18-struck-down]: 誅 carries a force of punishing or striking down, but Zhu and Guo here gloss it as 傷, injury. The translation does not imply that the obscured or injured person deserves punishment. The contrast is between light appearing above Earth and light entering beneath it.

[^j18-encounter]: “Encounter” under Oppression is not the same favorable promise one might import from the title Coming to Meet. Zhu, Zhu Zhen, and Guo explain the encounter as firmness meeting yieldingness and being covered; Zhang stresses the circumstances of the time. The word itself does not establish personal blame.

[^j18-outside-inside]: “Outside” and “inside” are deliberately verbal in the main translation: regarding someone as outside or bringing them within. Xu Zaihan explicitly rejects a merely spatial distinction and interprets the terms through closeness and estrangement. Xiang’s well, lake, and water explanations remain structural analogies of the trigrams.

[^j18-strength-stops]: The stopping of Great Strength is not translated away as a contradiction. Guo and He read it as restraint of force; the noble person’s strength includes knowing not to advance. Zhao cites the warning in its first and third lines against force in the toes or the use of strength, contrasting Retreat’s timely withdrawal.

[^j18-few-and-many]: The multitude in Great Possession and the fewness in Small Restraint are related by Gong Yuan to the single yielding line’s different roles: ruler in the fifth place, minister in the fourth. The compilers interpret “few” as insufficient power to restrain many yang lines. These are explanations of relations and capacity, not literal headcounts of people in an oracle.

[^j18-last-eight]: The final eight hexagrams are retained in the base’s order: Great Excess, Coming to Meet, Gradual Progress, Nourishment, After Completion, The Marrying Maiden, Before Completion, and Breakthrough. Zhu reports a conjecture of displaced slips but observes that the rhymes seem to support the received text. No rearrangement has been made. The woman’s “completion” concerns the marital reading, not a prediction of death; the man’s “exhaustion” is explained through incorrectly placed lines in Before Completion.

[^j18-nuclear]: Hu Bingwen proposes reading the final eight through 互體, overlapping inner trigrams. In the usual construction, lines 2–4 form the lower trigram and lines 3–5 the upper, producing a nuclear hexagram. The four central lines permit sixteen such figures; his two lists give eight on each side, and his final selection takes four from each. This checks the construction’s internal combinatorics; it does not establish that his conjecture explains the historical origin of the received order. Zhu’s uncertainty and Hu’s proposal remain distinct.
