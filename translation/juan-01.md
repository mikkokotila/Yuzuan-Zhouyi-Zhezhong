## 1. Qian 乾

<!-- Source: https://www.eee-learning.com/book/4542 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4542:001 -->

**Imperially Compiled Balanced Interpretations of the Zhou Changes — Juan One**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4542:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4542:002 -->

**The Zhou Changes: Upper Classic**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4542:002 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4542:003 -->

**Original Meaning.** *Zhou* is the name of a dynasty; *Changes* is the name of the book. Its figures were originally drawn by Fuxi. They embody both reciprocal interchange and transformation, and so the book is called the *Changes*. Its statements were attached by King Wen and the Duke of Zhou, and so it is associated with Zhou. Because the bundles of writing slips were large and heavy, it was divided into an upper and a lower part. The Classic consists of Fuxi's figures and the statements of King Wen and the Duke of Zhou. Together with the ten commentaries composed by Confucius, these make twelve parts. Over time, scholars considerably disordered them. In recent times Master Chao began correcting these errors, but did not entirely recover the ancient arrangement. Master Lü then revised it further, establishing the Classic in two juan and the commentaries in ten. Only then, it is said, was Confucius's original arrangement restored.[^j01-transmission]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4542:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4542:005 -->

### Qian: Great success. It is beneficial to be steadfast in what is right.[^j01-four-terms]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4542:005 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4542:006 -->

**Original Meaning.** The six strokes constitute the hexagram drawn by Fuxi. The unbroken stroke is odd: it represents yang's number.[^j01-stroke] Qian means strength in action, the nature of yang. In the accompanying designation, *Qian* names a three-line figure. “Below” means the inner trigram; “above,” the outer trigram. In the Classic itself, *Qian* names the six-line figure. Looking upward and examining what lay below, Fuxi saw that yin and yang have even and odd numbers. He therefore drew one odd stroke to represent yang and one even, divided stroke to represent yin. He saw that one yin and one yang each have the capacity to generate another yin and another yang. Thus, building upward from below, he doubled the figures twice, reaching three strokes and completing the eight trigrams. He saw that yang's nature is vigorous, and that its greatest embodiment is Heaven. He therefore named the figure of three odd strokes Qian and took Heaven as its likeness. Once the three strokes were in place and the eight trigrams complete, he again carried this doubling through three stages, making six strokes: above each of the eight trigrams he placed each of the eight in turn, producing the sixty-four hexagrams. In this hexagram all six strokes are odd, and both upper and lower trigrams are Qian. Yang is therefore pure and vigor at its utmost; neither the name Qian nor the image of Heaven changes. “Great success; it is beneficial to be steadfast in what is right” is the statement King Wen attached to determine the good or ill fortune of the hexagram as a whole—the so-called Judgment. *Yuan* means great; *heng*, free passage; *li*, fitting; *zhen*, correct and firm. King Wen held that the Way of Qian opens into great fulfillment and is perfectly right. Thus, when someone obtains this hexagram by divination and none of its six lines changes, the prognostication promises great success, but insists that benefit lies in being correct and firm. Only then can the outcome be secured. Here is the subtle intention with which the sages made the *Changes* to teach divination: to disclose the possibilities of things and bring undertakings to completion. The other hexagrams follow this pattern.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4542:006 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4542:007 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** When the sages of highest antiquity first drew the eight trigrams, the Ways of the Three Powers were fully present. They then doubled the figures to encompass all the transformations under Heaven; thus six strokes make a hexagram. Qian doubled makes Qian. Qian is Heaven. “Heaven” names its visible embodiment; “Qian,” its nature and the expression of that nature. Qian means vigor: vigor without cessation is what is called Qian. Speaking of Heaven comprehensively, we call it the Way—as in “even Heaven does not oppose him.” Distinguishing its aspects, we call it Heaven in respect of its embodiment; the Supreme Ruler in respect of its governance; ghosts and spirits in respect of its operations; the numinous in respect of its subtle efficacy; and Qian in respect of its nature and its expression. Qian is the beginning of the myriad things, and therefore represents Heaven, yang, the father, and the ruler. *Yuan, heng, li,* and *zhen* are called the four virtues. *Yuan* is the beginning of the myriad things; *heng*, their growth; *li*, their coming to fulfillment; *zhen*, their completion. Only Qian and Kun possess these four virtues in full. In the other hexagrams they vary with the matter at hand. Thus *yuan* has the particular sense of goodness and greatness; *li* chiefly concerns being correct and firm; and the character of *heng* and *zhen* accords with the particular circumstances. How broad, how great, are the meanings of the four virtues!

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4542:007 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4542:008 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4542:008 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4542:009 -->

**Kong Yingda:** Qian is the name of this hexagram. *Gua*, a figure, means *gua*, to hang up: it is as though images of things were hung up for people to see, hence the name.[^j01-gua] Although the two kinds of stroke represent the vital energies of yin and yang, they do not yet form images of the myriad things and cannot yet constitute a trigram. Three strokes are needed to represent the Three Powers and depict Heaven, Earth, thunder, wind, water, fire, mountain, and marsh. Only then is there a trigram. This is what the *Appended Statements* means by “The eight trigrams are set in order, and the images are within them.” Yet, although the initial three strokes contain the images of the myriad things, they do not fully exhaust the principles by which things change and develop. The figures were therefore doubled to six strokes, completing the forms and images of all things and encompassing everything that can be accomplished under Heaven. Thus six strokes make a hexagram. Qian originally represents Heaven. Heaven is formed by the accumulation of yang energies, and so all six lines of this hexagram are yang strokes. It is called Qian rather than Heaven because “Heaven” names an established embodiment, whereas “Qian” names that embodiment together with its activity. Thus the *Explanation of the Trigrams* says, “Qian is vigor”: Heaven's embodiment acts through vigor. The sages originally made the *Changes* to teach people. They wanted people to emulate Heaven's activity, not its physical form. Therefore they named the hexagram Qian, not Heaven.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4542:009 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4542:010 -->

**Master Shao:** Without understanding Qian, there is no way to understand the principles of human nature and the mandate of life.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4542:010 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4542:011 -->

**Classified Conversations of Master Zhu:** Qian is simply vigor; Kun is simply compliance. Pure yang is therefore vigorous; pure yin is therefore compliant. Nothing is more vigorous than Heaven, nothing more compliant than Earth.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4542:011 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4542:012 -->

**A question in the same work:** “What does it mean to say that Qian is Heaven's nature and its expression?” The reply: “Qian is vigor. The underlying constitution of vigor is its nature; the functioning of vigor is the expression of that nature.” He also said: “Nature and its expression are always interwoven here. The expression is simply nature issuing forth. Without nature, how could there be its expression? Vigor that never ceases—without this nature, how could it be so?”[^j01-nature]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4542:012 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4542:013 -->

**Another question:** “The *Original Meaning* says, ‘He saw that yang's nature is vigorous, and that its greatest embodiment is Heaven. He therefore named the figure of three odd strokes Qian and took Heaven as its likeness.’ Yet I venture to suggest that the hexagram statement does not seem to invoke an image. Should this not be discussed under the Great Image?” The reply: “As soon as this hexagram was established, the image was already there. That is why I speak of it here in advance.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4542:013 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4542:014 -->

**Asked about *yuan, heng, li,* and *zhen*, he replied:** “Originally these words simply meant great success and benefit in correctness; they were not distributed among the four seasons. Confucius appreciated these four words and was the first to discuss them as four distinct matters.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4542:014 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4542:015 -->

**He also said:** “In King Wen's original intention, the four words *yuan, heng, li,* and *zhen* meant the same in Qian and Kun as in the other hexagrams. Only when Confucius composed the *Commentary on the Judgments* and the *Words on the Text* did he interpret them as four virtues in Qian and Kun, while leaving the other hexagrams as before. The intentions of the two sages were not truly at odds: each was bringing a principle to light. Students today should first empty their minds of preconceptions and savor the words, apprehending each passage according to its own intention. Where the interpretations differ, they do not obstruct one another. One must not hastily impose one's own view and arbitrarily decide between them.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4542:015 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4542:016 -->

**Hu Bingwen:** The various commentators immediately interpret *yuan, heng, li,* and *zhen* as four virtues. Only the *Original Meaning* treats them as an oracular statement. Great fulfillment joined to perfect rightness is what Heaven's Way inherently is. Great success whose benefit necessarily lies in being correct and firm is what human affairs ought to be. Qian is the first hexagram of the *Changes*. Even someone who receives it, and whose undertaking succeeds greatly, cannot secure the outcome without being correct and firm. How much more is this true of the other hexagrams!

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4542:016 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4542:017 -->

**Cai Qing:** “Its greatest embodiment is Heaven.” Under Kun, too, we read that “among the embodiments of yin, none is greater than Earth.” Clearly we cannot simply equate Qian and Kun with Heaven and Earth. Whatever is supremely vigorous is Qian; whatever is supremely compliant is Kun. This is why Qian and Kun suffice for ten thousand applications, and why the *Commentary on the Judgments* specifically uses Heaven's Way to illuminate Qian and Earth's Way to illuminate Kun.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4542:017 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4542:018 -->

**Lin Xiyuan:** Qian's virtues are firmness and vigor. Firmness refers to its underlying constitution; vigor also embraces its activity. Firmness gives the capacity to stand established; vigor gives the capacity to act. When a person can establish himself and act, both resolve and vital energy attain their full reach. Once the root is established, the Way grows: no undertaking fails to take shape, no achievement fails to come to completion. Difficulty is scarcely perceived; nothing can obstruct the movement. It is like Heaven revolving and Earth turning, thunder striking and wind sweeping onward. Even “How open is Heaven's highway!” scarcely suffices as a comparison. This is not merely success, but great success. To be centered is to incline neither to one side nor the other; to be correct is to go neither too far nor fall short. Here again is the distinction between underlying constitution and activity. “Through correctness and greatness, the true disposition of Heaven and Earth can be seen”: this shows Qian's centeredness and correctness. The Way of Qian opens into great fulfillment and is perfectly right. Human beings, however, may fail to be right; hence the sage's warning.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4542:018 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4542:019 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** The scholars all interpret Qian's and Kun's *yuan, heng, li,* and *zhen* as four virtues. Master Zhu alone treats them as an oracular statement, on the same footing as in the other hexagrams; his account is sound. Yet although these four words convey only two propositions, they contain four distinct levels of meaning. How so? *Yuan* means great; *heng*, free passage; *li*, fitting; *zhen*, correct and firm. If a person can be supremely vigorous, the undertaking should achieve great success. Yet it must be fittingly grounded in correctness and firmness. These are the oracle's two propositions. But the *Changes* also speaks of “small success” and says that one “cannot persist.” A passage that opens only for a time yields a small success. Only when greatness is present can the success itself be great: greatness therefore precedes success. Mere unbending obstinacy is firmness without fitness. Only when what is fitting is present may one hold firmly to it: fitness therefore precedes firmness. This principle applies throughout the sixty-four hexagrams. Where the text says “great success,” the situation accords with it; where it says only “success” or “small success,” it falls short of it. Where it says “beneficial to be steadfast in what is right,” the situation accords with it; where it says “one cannot persist,” “do not maintain this constancy forever,” or “persistence brings misfortune,” “persistence brings danger,” or “persistence brings humiliation,” the situation runs counter to it. Qian and Kun are the progenitors of the other hexagrams: their success is invariably great, their constancy invariably fitting. King Wen attached a statement containing all four words. Confucius therefore traced them back to Heaven's Way and the depths of human nature, explaining them through the four virtues. In doing so, he was truly unfolding King Wen's meaning, while also establishing a model for the full and abbreviated, partial and complete statements throughout the sixty-four hexagrams. It is not that Confucius's account differs from King Wen's; nor does he explain Qian and Kun by a principle different from that used for the other hexagrams. Read Master Zhu's book in this light, and you may hope not to mistake its purpose.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4542:019 -->

### First Nine: A hidden dragon. Do not act.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4542:020 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4542:021 -->

**Original Meaning.** “First Nine” names the yang line at the bottom of the hexagram. Figures are always drawn upward from below, so the lowest line is called “first.” Among yang numbers, nine is old and seven is young. The old changes, while the young does not; hence a yang line is called “nine.” “A hidden dragon; do not act” is the statement attached by the Duke of Zhou to determine the good or ill fortune of a particular line—the so-called line statement. “Hidden” means concealed. The dragon is a yang creature. The initial yang is below and cannot yet be put to use. Its image is therefore a hidden dragon; its prognostication says, “Do not act.” Anyone who encounters Qian with this line changing should contemplate the image and ponder the prognostication. The other lines follow this pattern.[^j01-line-structure]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4542:021 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4542:022 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The lowest line is the first. Nine is yang's number at full strength, so it names a yang line. Principle has no visible form; images are therefore borrowed to disclose its meaning. Qian takes the dragon as its image. The dragon's numinous transformations are unfathomable, and so it represents the transformations of Qian's Way, the waxing and waning of yang energy, and the sage's advance and withdrawal. First Nine lies at the bottom of the hexagram, at the very beginning of things. Yang energy is only beginning to stir; the sage is in obscure and humble circumstances. Like a dragon concealed, he cannot yet put himself forward. He should nurture his powers in obscurity and await the time.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4542:022 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4542:023 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4542:023 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4542:024 -->

**Shen Linshi:** Calling it a dragon is the borrowing of an image. The energies of Heaven and Earth ascend and descend; the noble person's Way has its times of action and concealment. A dragon can fly and can hide, so it is borrowed as a likeness for the noble person's virtue. Since First Nine is still concealed, the text says, “Do not act.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4542:024 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4542:025 -->

**Kong Yingda:** There are two explanations for calling yang lines “nine” and yin lines “six.” First: Qian's trigram has three strokes and Kun's has six. Yang can embrace yin as well as itself, giving the number nine; yin cannot embrace yang, so its number is six. Second: old yang has the number nine and old yin the number six. Both old yang and old yin change, and the *Zhou Changes* takes changing lines as the basis for divination. Hence the terms nine and six. Old yang is numbered nine and old yin six because, in counting the yarrow stalks, nine groups of four give old yang and six groups of four give old yin. Young yang's designation as seven and young yin's as eight follow the same principle.[^j01-yarrow-count]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4542:025 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4542:026 -->

**Cui Jing:** Nine is the number of old yang, the changing yang on which a prognostication is based; hence yang is called nine. “Hidden” means concealed. The dragon is concealed below in the earth; its hidden virtue is not yet manifest. Thus the noble person veils his light and awaits the time. His course of action has not yet come to fruition, so the text says, “Do not act.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4542:026 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4542:027 -->

**Classified Conversations of Master Zhu — a question:** “Cheng's *Changes* explains the first, second, third, and fourth lines in terms of Shun. How can we know that they refer to him?” The reply: “This extends the meaning of the line images; it is not their original purport. In the *Changes*, images originally arose for the sake of divination, and prognostications arose from the images. Principles of the Way are already present within the oracular statements. Suppose one obtains Qian's First Nine. The initial yang is below and cannot yet be put to use. The image is a hidden dragon, and the prognostication says, ‘Do not act.’ Anyone who encounters Qian and receives this line should contemplate the image and ponder the prognostication: remaining obscure and refraining from action is appropriate. This is the original purport of the *Changes*. When the sage composed the *Commentary on the Judgments*, the *Commentary on the Images*, and the *Words on the Text*, he drew out the implications step by step, revealing limitless principles. This is why Cheng's *Changes* can elaborate them without end. Once the original purport of the *Changes* is understood, there is no harm in further elaboration. But to explain the *Changes* directly through these elaborations is to lose its original purport.”[^j01-shun]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4542:027 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4542:028 -->

**Li Shunchen:** The images of all six lines are taken from the dragon because of yang's inherent vigor. Concealment, appearance, vigilance, leaping, flight, and overreaching form a sequence from beginning to end and mark the course of its transformations.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4542:028 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4542:029 -->

**Liang Yin:** The teaching of the *Changes* is pure and clean, subtle and exact. Its images therefore borrow things without becoming tied to particular events; they encompass their meanings while adapting to each use. Nevertheless, Qian is pure yang, and its image is the dragon, so much of its meaning is directed toward the sage. Accordingly, in the *Words on the Text*, the Master illuminates it throughout through the affairs of sages. Yet look at the six lines: what their images disclose and their prognostications decide can be used by anyone. Are they for sages alone? Take First Nine's “A hidden dragon; do not act.” For a sage, this is the time of obscure and humble circumstances; for a noble person, withdrawal from the world without resentment; for a student, the nurturing of correctness while understanding is still unformed. Extend its application in this way: where could it not apply? Master Zhu discussed the *Changes* through images and prognostications, and was unwilling to confine it to particular events, because he feared that readers would become attached to those events and miss the meaning.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4542:029 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4542:030 -->

**Lin Xiyuan:** The dragon is not merely a yang creature: it is a yang creature whose numinous powers cannot be fathomed. Hence it represents the six lines of Qian. All six possess the Way of Qian, unlike the lines of other hexagrams. Thus the *Words on the Text* explains them through the sage. Among creatures, their counterpart is the dragon.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4542:030 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4542:031 -->

### Nine in the Second Place: A dragon appears in the field. It is beneficial to see a great person.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4542:031 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4542:032 -->

**Original Meaning.** “Second” means the second line counted upward from below; subsequent designations follow this rule. Nine in the Second Place is firm and vigorous, centered and correct. It emerges from concealment and leaves obscurity behind; its beneficence reaches other beings, who benefit from seeing it. Thus its image is a dragon appearing in the field, and its prognostication is “It is beneficial to see a great person.” Although Nine in the Second Place has not yet attained high station, the virtue of a great person is already manifest. An ordinary person is not equal to this description. Therefore, for someone who encounters this changing line, the meaning is simply that it is beneficial to see such a person—a great person still in a lower position. Here the line and the consulter stand to one another as host and guest: this forms a distinct interpretive pattern. Someone who already possesses the virtue of the appearing dragon, however, benefits from seeing the great person above, Nine in the Fifth Place.[^j01-great-person]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4542:032 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4542:033 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The field is the surface of the earth. The dragon has appeared above ground, and its virtue is already manifest. In terms of a sage's life, this is Shun's time of farming and fishing. He benefits from seeing a ruler of great virtue so that he may put his Way into practice. The ruler likewise benefits from seeing a minister of great virtue, so that together they may accomplish their work. All under Heaven benefit from seeing a person of great virtue and receiving his beneficence. The ruler of great virtue is Nine in the Fifth Place. Qian and Kun are pure in constitution: their lines correspond through shared virtue, without the distinction between firm and yielding.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4542:033 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4542:034 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4542:034 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4542:035 -->

**Zheng Kangcheng:** Among the Three Powers, the second place belongs to the Way of Earth. The earth's surface is the field; hence the word “field.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4542:035 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4542:036 -->

**Gan Bao:** The second place is above the ground, at the earth's surface. Yang energy is about to spread its influence, and the sage is about to become manifest. Hence “It is beneficial to see a great person.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4542:036 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4542:037 -->

**Kong Yingda:** Yang occupies the second place, so the line is called Nine in the Second Place. Yang energy emerges into view, so the text says, “A dragon appears.” A field is a place on the earth's surface where one can work and produce benefit. Yang energy emerges above the earth, so the text says, “in the field.” Both the first and second places belong to the Way of Earth. The second is above the first; hence “field.” “A dragon appears in the field” is an image from nature. “It is beneficial to see a great person” entrusts its meaning to human affairs. The time when the dragon appears in the field resembles a sage gradually coming forth after long concealment. Although not in the ruler's position, he possesses the ruler's virtue. Thus the multitude under Heaven benefits from seeing the great person represented by Nine in the Second Place. Earlier scholars said: “It is like the Master teaching by the Zhu and Si rivers and benefiting all under Heaven. Possessing the virtue of a ruler, he is called a great person.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4542:037 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4542:038 -->

**Cai Qing:** “Great person” normally designates someone complete in both virtue and position. Although Nine in the Second Place has not yet attained the position, the virtue of a great person is already manifest. As the saying goes, “Dwelling in benevolence and following righteousness, the great person's work is complete.” Hence this line too is called a great person.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4542:038 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4542:039 -->

### Nine in the Third Place: The noble person is tirelessly active all day, and vigilant at evening. Danger, but no blame.[^j01-third-punctuation]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4542:039 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4542:040 -->

**Original Meaning.** Nine is a yang line; the third is a yang position. Firmness is doubled, without centrality. The line occupies the top of the lower trigram—a dangerous place. Yet its nature and constitution are firm and vigorous, giving the image of tireless effort and anxious vigilance. Hence this prognostication. “The noble person” here refers to the consulter. It means that someone capable of such care and apprehension will incur no blame, even in a dangerous position.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4542:040 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4542:041 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Although the third is a human position, it is already at the top of the lower trigram: someone honored and conspicuous who has not yet left the lower sphere. This is the time when word of Shun's profound virtue reached those above. Unremitting effort and anxious vigilance by day and evening bring freedom from blame, even in a dangerous position. A person below whose kingly virtue has already become evident, and to whom all under Heaven are about to turn—his grounds for danger and apprehension are plain. Although the text speaks of a sage's affairs, how could it teach unless it set forth a warning? Such is the intention behind the making of the *Changes*.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4542:041 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4542:042 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4542:042 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4542:043 -->

**Zheng Kangcheng:** Among the Three Powers, the third place belongs to the human Way. Possessing Qian's virtue while standing in the human Way gives the image of a noble person.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4542:043 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4542:044 -->

**Kong Yingda:** Yang occupies the third place, so the line is called Nine in the Third Place. Because its position is not central, it is not called a great person. Because yang occupies its proper position, it is called a noble person. Being in a place of anxiety and danger, it is “tirelessly active all day”: throughout the whole day it vigorously strengthens itself, never ceasing. “Vigilant at evening” means that even as evening approaches, apprehensive vigilance remains. The position occupied by this hexagram's third nine really is dangerous. The *Words on the Text* also says, “Although in danger, no blame,” confirming that the danger is real. Judging from the surrounding wording, *ruo* should be taken as a grammatical particle. The various scholars all read it as “like”—as though danger were present, when in fact it was not. That interpretation, I fear, does not fully account for the principle.[^j01-jing-errors]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4542:044 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4542:045 -->

**Gong Yuan:** The third line stands at the top of the lower trigram, at a time of danger and apprehension. Only unceasing self-strengthening, care, caution, and reverent fear can avert blame.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4542:045 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4542:046 -->

**Yang Shi:** Only Qian's Nine in the Third Place speaks of a noble person, because the third nine occupies the human position. It is in this line that one treads what is right and occupies the middle. Thus, for Nine in the Fourth Place, the *Words on the Text* says, “Above, not in Heaven; below, not in the field; in the middle, not in the human sphere.” For Nine in the Third Place it says only, “Above, not in Heaven; below, not in the field.” Its statement that “the noble person practices these four virtues” refers to the noble person spoken of in Qian.[^j01-two-centers]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4542:046 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4542:047 -->

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4542:087 -->

**He also says:** Someone may object: “The good fortune of having no head comes from being firm yet capable of yielding; that is what makes it fortunate.[^j01-ji-correction] The benefit of the mare comes from being compliant yet capable of vigor; that is what makes it beneficial. Firmness capable of yielding and compliance capable of vigor are inherently different in nature and constitution. Why, then, does the *Commentary on the Spring and Autumn Annals* say, ‘Qian changing to Kun says, “A company of dragons appears, none at their head. Good fortune”’?” The reply: “When Qian changes to Kun, it does what Kun does, but it has come from firmness and differs from what was Kun from the outset. When Kun changes to Qian, it does what Qian does, but it has come from yielding and differs from what was Qian from the outset. Thus the good fortune of Qian's having no head can never be identical with the constancy of Kun's mare. The benefit of Kun's enduring constancy can never be identical with Qian's great success. The sage does not instruct us simply to examine the resulting hexagram for the prognostication. Instead, he separately sets forth the image and prognostication of the passage from this to that, precisely because the two cannot be treated as identical.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4542:088 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4542:089 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Although line statements illuminate the principles of a hexagram, their actual purpose is divination. Naming the lines nine and six therefore identifies their operative use. A line statement is used when its line moves, and is not used when it does not move. The hexagram statement, however, is used whether or not there is movement. When nothing moves, one divines from the original hexagram's Judgment. When there is movement, one combines the Judgments of the original and the resulting hexagrams. For example, when all six lines of Qian change, the result is Kun; when all six lines of Kun change, the result is Qian. Earlier scholars held that, when every line changes, the original hexagram should be set aside and the resulting hexagram consulted. Yet Qian and Kun embody the great meanings of Heaven and Earth: even when Qian changes to Kun, Kun's statement alone cannot suffice; even when Kun changes to Qian, Qian's statement alone cannot suffice. Thus “Using Nines” and “Using Sixes” were separately established as the oracular statements for complete change. This explanation has merit. Reasoning from principle, however, there is no justification for entirely discarding the original hexagram and not considering it, even when all its lines change—and this applies to every hexagram, not just Qian and Kun. The view that one must combine original and resulting hexagrams in divination is therefore nearer the truth. On this understanding, when Qian changes to Kun, one considers Qian's and Kun's statements together; when Kun changes to Qian, one likewise considers Kun's and Qian's statements together. Yet the passage from Qian to Kun signifies yang rooted in yin; the passage from Kun to Qian signifies compliance embodying vigor. Someone considering the hexagram statements together should understand this distinction. The statements “Using Nines” and “Using Sixes” were therefore established to bring it out. Though the company of dragons appears, no head appears: this is because yang is rooted in yin. To preserve constancy forever and conclude in greatness is compliance embodying vigor. Here again Qian and Kun establish a general rule for all sixty-four hexagrams. Thus, when Return changes to Encounter, it is appropriate, amid growth, to guard against decline. When Encounter changes to Return, it is appropriate, amid disorder, to plan for order. These meanings certainly do not belong to Qian and Kun alone, to the exclusion of the other hexagrams. The sage sets them forth in Qian and Kun only to establish the pattern. Yet even when Qian does not change, the principle of using nines is already present: Qian's origination has no starting point, which is precisely the subtlety of having no head. Even when Kun does not change, the principle of using sixes is already present: Kun's constancy can rest secure, which is precisely the Way of enduring constancy. The inherent union of yin's and yang's virtues is the workings of reciprocal interchange; what becomes still more evident through movement is the activity of transformation. Students of the *Changes* must especially understand this.[^j01-changing-rules]

## 2. Kun 坤

<!-- Source: https://www.eee-learning.com/book/4543 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4543:001 -->

䷁ **Kun 坤** — Kun below; Kun above.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4543:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4543:002 -->

### Kun: Great success. The constancy of a mare is beneficial. The noble person has somewhere to go. Taking the lead, one goes astray; following, one finds a master. Beneficial: to the southwest, finding companions; to the northeast, losing companions. Constancy at peace brings good fortune.[^j01-kun-punctuation]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4543:002 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4543:003 -->

**Original Meaning.** The divided stroke is even: it represents yin's number. Kun means compliance, the nature of yin. In the accompanying designation it names a three-line figure; in the Classic, a six-line figure. Among yin's embodiments, none is greater than Earth. This figure's three strokes are all even, so it is named Kun and takes Earth as its image. Doubling it produces Kun again: yin is pure, and compliance at its utmost. Thus neither its name nor its image changes. A mare is compliant yet vigorous in movement. Yang goes first; yin follows. Yang takes righteousness as its governing concern; yin takes benefit. Southwest is the yin direction; northeast, the yang direction. Being at peace is compliance in practice; constancy is vigor in maintaining one's course. Whoever encounters this hexagram receives a prognostication of great success, with benefit in taking compliance joined to vigor as the right course. If there is somewhere to go, taking the lead brings confusion; following brings attainment, with benefit as the governing aim.[^j01-kun-readings] Going southwest brings companions; going northeast loses them. In general, being able to rest at peace in what is right brings good fortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4543:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4543:004 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Kun is Qian's counterpart. Its four virtues are the same, but the character of its constancy differs. Qian's constancy is firm and strong; Kun's is yielding and compliant. A mare is yielding and compliant, yet vigorous in movement. Hence this image is chosen: “the constancy of a mare.” When the noble person's conduct is yielding and compliant, beneficial and steadfastly correct, it accords with Kun's virtue. Yin follows yang, waiting for the lead before responding. For yin to precede yang is to go astray; remaining behind attains its proper, constant order. “Governing benefit”: benefiting the myriad things belongs chiefly to Kun, for their generation and completion are both Earth's work. The minister's Way is likewise. The ruler commands; the minister carries out the command. To labor at the work is the minister's office. Southwest is the yin direction; northeast, the yang direction. Yin must follow yang, leaving and losing its companions of the same kind. Only then can it accomplish the work of transformation and nourishment, and attain the good fortune of constancy at peace. Attaining its constant order brings peace; resting in that order is constancy. Therefore there is good fortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4543:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4543:005 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4543:005 -->

The southwest is the region where nourishment is brought to completion; it shares Kun's Way. <!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4543:006 -->

Earth, in its own constitution, can also initiate and generate the myriad things, bringing each to successful development. Hence “great success,” as with Qian. The female is yielding in contrast to the male, so the text says, “The constancy of a mare is beneficial.” It says horse rather than ox because, although an ox is yielding and compliant, it cannot traverse the earth without limit and so cannot display Kun's virtue. A horse, though dull compared with a dragon, can still travel far, representing Earth's wide-ranging nourishment. “Taking the lead, one goes astray; following, one finds a master and benefit”: being utmost yin, it should wait for the lead and then respond. Whatever it undertakes, if it goes ahead of things, it becomes confused; if it follows behind, it finds a master and benefit. Yin cannot take the initiative, just as a minister cannot precede his ruler or the lowly precede the honored.

Finding companions, then, belongs to the minister's office; losing companions belongs to the minister's heart. He exercises that office with this disposition. **Yu Yan:** Kun follows Qian's vigor, so its prognostication too is “great success.” In the horse herds of the north, ten mares follow one stallion and do not enter another herd: this is the mare's constancy. Kun's Way is for yin to follow yang. If its constancy resembles the mare's following the stallion, it is beneficial; hence “The constancy of a mare is beneficial.” Wherever the *Changes* says “the noble person,” it refers to the consulter. “Has somewhere to go” means having a course of action. Kun follows Qian in movement. If it precedes Qian, it becomes confused and loses the Way; if it follows Qian, it gains Qian as its master and benefits. Hence “The noble person has somewhere to go. Taking the lead, one goes astray; following, one finds a master and benefit.” “Companions” are those of Kun's kind. Southwest is Kun's own region; Dui, Li, and Xun are all of its kind—this is finding companions. When it goes forth to follow Qian, the northeastern Zhen, Gen, and Kan are not of its kind—this is losing companions. Whether the noble person serves or withdraws, if he can rest at peace wherever he finds himself and always sustain himself through steadfast correctness, there is nowhere he can go without good fortune. Hence “To the southwest, finding companions; to the northeast, losing companions. Constancy at peace brings good fortune.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4543:016 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4543:017 -->

**Cai Qing:** A stallion would be all vigor, while a cow would be all compliance. A mare is compliant yet vigorous. The essential point is that its vigor does not stand outside its compliance: it is the vigor of compliance itself. Hence “constancy at peace.” Kun is the Way of Earth, the Way of the wife, and the Way of the minister. Without compliance it acts on its own authority and completes nothing; without vigor it cannot match Qian. Compliance joined to vigor is Kun's correctness.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4543:017 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4543:018 -->

**Zheng Weiyue:** Kun is Qian's partner. Kun's virtue is precisely Qian's virtue, except that it receives it with yielding compliance and carries it through to completion. To carry through to completion is vigor; hence “The constancy of a mare is beneficial.” Kun's Way follows Qian, and Qian is Kun's master. Thus taking the lead brings confusion, while following finds the proper master. “To the southwest, finding companions” means leading one's own kind in following yang: the Way of serving a ruler together with others. “To the northeast, losing companions” means breaking with one's own kind to follow yang: the Way expressed by “dispersing one's group” and “companions disappear.” These are all the correctness of yin's Way. To rest at peace in them is what brings good fortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4543:018 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4543:019 -->

**Qiao Zhonghe:** Only by uniting with Qian does Kun find its master. Having found its master, southwest and northeast are both beneficial directions, and finding companions and losing companions are both fortunate affairs. This is the wife's Way and the minister's Way: the wife follows the husband, the minister follows the ruler—that is all.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4543:019 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4543:020 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** “Following, one finds a master” should be read on the authority of Confucius's *Words on the Text*. Kun is the Way of Earth and the Way of the minister; Qian is its master. Taking the lead leaves it without a master, hence its confusion. Following allows it to find the one it should take as master. The word “beneficial” should be read with the next two clauses: in the southwest, it is beneficial to find companions; in the northeast, it is beneficial to lose them. “Finding companions” and “losing companions” correspond directly to the preceding “finding a master.” Someone serving a master recognizes only the master and does not cherish companions of his own kind as a private faction. Yet there are times when finding companions is appropriate. The southwest is where Kun performs its labors on Qian's behalf. Without the combined strength of many, the work cannot be accomplished. Finding companions here is precisely how the master's undertaking is completed: finding companions is thus finding the master. The east, however, is the initial stage of receiving the command, and the north the season of reporting completion. Receiving orders and returning credit for the work, one has no private claim. What place is there, then, for talk of companions and one's own kind? Here one must lose companions before finding the master. A minister who understands this can bring forward others of his kind and help them advance without forming a partisan clique; he can also stand apart, solitary and independent, without cultivating an ostentatious difference. Thus among the hexagram lines are “companions gather together”, “companions arrive,” “with their kind,” and “with one's neighbors”: all express finding companions. There are also “companions disappear,” “dispersing the group,” and “leaving one's kind and rising”: all express losing companions. This meaning agrees both with King Wen's trigram diagram and with Confucius's *Commentary on the Judgments*. It is therefore first brought to light in this hexagram, and the minister's Way throughout the sixty-four hexagrams follows it.[^j01-companion-crossrefs]

### First Six: Treading on frost: hard ice is coming.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4543:021 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4543:022 -->

**Original Meaning.** Six names a yin line. Among yin numbers, six is old and eight young; hence a yin line is called six. Frost is the condensation of yin energy; when that energy flourishes, water freezes into ice. In this line yin is first arising below. Its beginning is exceedingly slight, but its momentum must grow strong. Thus its image is like treading on frost and recognizing that hard ice is on its way. Yin and yang are the foundation of creative transformation. Neither can exist without the other; their waxing and waning follow a constant order that human beings cannot increase or diminish. Yet yang governs generation, yin destruction; their kinds are therefore distinguished as wholesome and harmful. When the sages made the *Changes*, they explained the indispensability of both through such pairs as vigor and compliance, benevolence and righteousness, showing no exclusive preference. But at the junctures of waxing and waning, where wholesome and harmful divide, they never failed to express the intention to support yang and restrain yin. Their purpose in assisting transformation and nourishment, and participating with Heaven and Earth, is profound. No prognostication is explicitly stated because the injunction to take care over the smallest beginnings is already visible in the image.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4543:022 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4543:023 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** A yin line is called six: yin at full strength. At eight, yang has already arisen, so yin is no longer pure in its fullness. When yin first arises below, it is exceedingly slight. At its first appearance, the sage gives a warning because it is about to grow. Yin's first condensation forms frost. On treading frost, one should know that yin will gradually strengthen until there is hard ice. It is like a petty person: however insignificant at first, he must not be allowed to grow, for growth leads to ascendancy.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4543:023 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4543:024 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4543:024 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4543:025 -->

**Wang Yinglin:** Qian's First Nine is Return. “A hidden dragon; do not act” has the same meaning as closing the passes. Kun's First Six is Encounter. “Treading on frost: hard ice is coming” has the same warning as “the woman is strong.”[^j01-initial-lines]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4543:025 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4543:026 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Speaking of yin and yang within the human person, the heart-mind's numinous intelligence is yang; the five sense faculties and all the bodily members are yin. Speaking of the classes of human relationships, the ruler, father, and husband are yang; the minister, son, and wife are yin. The heart-mind's numinous intelligence operates through the body. The ruler's and father's affairs are carried out through ministers and sons. The husband's household is completed through his wife. These are all great principles of Heaven and Earth: how could either side exist without the other? Yet the heart-mind is called the greater part, and the sense faculties and bodily members the lesser part. The ruler, father, and husband are called the Three Guiding Bonds and are honored; ministers, sons, and wives are chiefly to follow compliantly and occupy the lower place. From these distinctions between greater and lesser, honored and lowly, obedience and defiance divide, good and evil arise, and good fortune and misfortune are determined. Suppose that, within the person, the faculty of the heart-mind governs and all bodily members obey; and that, in human relationships, the Ways of ruler, father, and husband prevail, while ministers, sons, wives, and concubines heed their commands. Yin then unites its virtue with yang: what is there to condemn in yin? Only when ears, eyes, and limbs each indulge their own desires instead of serving the Heaven-given governing faculty, and ministers, sons, concubines, and wives pursue private purposes instead of receiving direction from ruler and father, can yin come to encroach upon yang and the perverse begin to injure the right. Within one person this is the struggle between principle and desire, from which good and evil arise. Within the state it is the alternating ascendancy of public and private interest, by which order and disorder develop. Thus Confucius's *Words on the Text* speaks of the accumulation of good and evil and the gradual developments involving ruler and father, minister and son. Its intention is profoundly earnest. When we speak, then, of yang as wholesome and yin as harmful, is yin truly harmful in itself? Follow yang, and there is no harmfulness. When we speak of supporting yang and restraining yin, must yin necessarily be restrained? If there is a way to transform it, restraint is unnecessary. Such is the main purport of this line's “Treading on frost: hard ice is coming.” Trace its sources and ramifications, and the warnings of Yao, Shun, and Yu about the precarious and the subtle, the injunctions in the *Great Learning* and the *Doctrine of the Mean* to be watchful in solitude, and the *Spring and Autumn Annals*' safeguards concerning names and proper station all prove to be inward and outward expressions of the same thing. Every passage in the sixty-four hexagrams concerning the juncture of yin and yang should be understood in this light.[^j01-moral-yinyang]

### Six in the Second Place: Straight, square, great. Without practice, nothing is unfavorable.[^j01-practice]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4543:027 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4543:028 -->

**Original Meaning.** Yielding compliance, correctness, and firmness are Kun's straightness. Bestowing determinate forms is Kun's squareness. A virtue that accords with the boundless is Kun's greatness. Six in the Second Place is yielding and compliant, centered and correct, and possesses the Way of Kun in its purity. Its virtue is therefore straight within, square without, and ample in greatness. Without waiting for learning or practice, nothing is unfavorable. If the consulter possesses this virtue, the prognostication is accordingly so.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4543:028 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4543:029 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The second is a yin position and is below; hence it is Kun's governing line. Speaking comprehensively of Kun's Way, to be centered and correct while remaining below is the Way of Earth. The three terms “straight,” “square,” and “great” describe its virtue and activity, fully encompassing Earth's Way. Because it is straight, square, and great, nothing is unfavorable even without practice. “Without practice” means that it is natural. In Kun's Way, it acts without anyone making it act; in the sage, it means according effortlessly with the Way. “Straight, square, great” is what Mencius calls “supremely great and supremely firm, through uprightness.” Because we are speaking of Kun's constitution, “square” replaces “firm,” just as “mare” qualifies “constancy.” When speaking of vital energy, greatness comes first: greatness is the constitution of that energy. In Kun, straightness and squareness come first, because greatness arises through them. Straight, square, and great suffice to encompass Earth's Way; it remains only for people to recognize it. Qian and Kun are pure in constitution, and correspondence is considered through position. The second line governs Kun, so correspondence with the fifth is not taken up, nor is the ruler's Way assigned to the fifth. In Qian, by contrast, the second and fifth correspond.

**Editorial Judgment.** Qian is round, Kun square. Squareness is Kun's virtue, paired with roundness. Hence “Supremely still, yet square in virtue.” Straightness, however, is Qian's virtue; hence “Qian, in its movement, is straight.” Greatness too is Qian's virtue; hence “Great indeed is Qian's origination!” Now Six in the Second Place possesses Kun's virtue in its purity, so squareness is indeed its native character. Why, then, does the statement begin with “straight” and end with “great”? Because every square thing must begin with straightness as its root and end with greatness as its fullest development. Thus in mathematics we speak of line, surface, and solid. Without the straightness of the line, a square surface cannot be formed. By accumulating square surfaces, one can form the magnitude of a solid. Kun takes Qian's virtue as its own: through straightness it forms squareness; through squareness it forms greatness. It follows the natural course of Heaven's principle, adding and contriving nothing in between. Hence “Without practice, nothing is unfavorable.” Here *xi*, “practice,” means repetition, with the implication of adding or contriving something further. “Without practice, nothing is unfavorable” is precisely what is meant by “Kun accomplishes through simplicity.” If “without practice” meant having no need of learning, would “reverence to straighten the inner life, righteousness to square the outward conduct” require no application of the heart-mind?

### Six in the Fifth Place: A yellow lower garment. Supreme good fortune.[^j01-yellow-garment]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4543:055 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4543:056 -->

**Original Meaning.** Yellow is the central color; the lower garment is an adornment worn below. Six in the Fifth Place is yin in the honored position. The virtues of centeredness and compliance fill the inner life and appear outwardly. Hence this image, and a prognostication of the good fortune of great goodness. The consulter's virtue must be like this for the prognostication to be so. In the *Commentary on the Spring and Autumn Annals*, Nan Kuai, about to rebel, obtained this line through divination and took it as greatly auspicious. Zifu Huibo said: “For an undertaking of loyalty and trustworthiness, it may be so; otherwise you will certainly fail. To be strong outwardly and warm within is loyalty. To be harmonious and thereby follow what is right is trustworthiness. Hence ‘A yellow lower garment. Supreme good fortune.’ Yellow is the central color; the lower garment is an adornment worn below; origination is chief among good things. Without loyalty at the center, one does not obtain its color. Without reverence below, one does not obtain its adornment. If the undertaking is not good, one does not attain its consummation. Moreover, the *Changes* cannot be used to divine for treachery. If any of these three is lacking, then even if the divination yields this line, the promised result is not yet yours.” Afterward Nan Kuai did indeed fail. <!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4543:056 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4543:057 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Although Kun is the minister's Way, the fifth place is in fact the ruler's position. Hence the warning: “A yellow lower garment. Supreme good fortune.” Yellow is the central color; the lower garment is clothing worn below. Hold to the center and remain below, and there is supreme good fortune: that is, preserve one's proper station. *Yuan* means great and good. The line's image says only that keeping to the center and remaining below brings supreme good fortune; it does not unfold the whole implication. If the yellow lower garment brings supreme good fortune, then occupying the supreme position would bring great misfortune to all under Heaven: this can be understood. Later readers have failed to comprehend it, obscuring this meaning, so the matter must be distinguished. The fifth is the honored position. In other hexagrams, a six in the fifth may signify yielding compliance, luminous cultivation, or benighted weakness. In Kun, however, it signifies occupying the supreme position. Yin is the minister's Way and the woman's Way. Ministers occupying the supreme position include Yi and Wang Mang: such cases can at least be spoken of. Women occupying it include Nüwa and Lady Wu: these are transformations outside the constant order, not to be spoken of. Hence the warning of the yellow lower garment, without saying everything outright. Someone may object: “In Revolution, the affairs of Tang and Wu are fully stated. Why remain silent here alone?” The reply: “The fall of one house and rise of another belong to the constant order of principle. For yin to occupy the supreme position is a transformation outside that constant order.”[^j01-cheng-rulership]

**Collected Explanations.**

### Using Sixes: Enduring constancy in what is right is beneficial.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4543:072 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4543:073 -->

**Original Meaning.** “Using Sixes” means that, whenever yin lines are obtained in divination, sixes are used, not eights. This too is a general rule. It is set forth here because this hexagram is pure yin and stands at the beginning. Whoever encounters it with all six lines changing receives the prognostication of this statement. Yin's yielding character cannot firmly maintain its course; when it changes to yang, it can sustain enduring constancy. Thus the consulter is admonished that enduring constancy is beneficial. This is precisely Qian's “It is beneficial to be steadfast in what is right.” Yet because the change begins from Kun, it falls short of Qian's “great success.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4543:073 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Kun's “Using Sixes” is like Qian's “Using Nines”: it is the Way of employing yin. Yin's Way is yielding and finds enduring constancy difficult. Thus, in using sixes, benefit lies in maintaining correctness and firmness continually and for the long term.

## 3. Zhun 屯 — Difficulty at the Beginning

<!-- Source: https://www.eee-learning.com/book/4548 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4548:001 -->

![Zhun](../assets/eee-learning/b86f974f1184-yi03b.png) **3. Zhun 屯 — Difficulty at the Beginning** — Zhen below; Kan above.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4548:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4548:002 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Concerning Zhun, the *Sequence of the Hexagrams* says: “Once Heaven and Earth exist, the myriad things come into being. What fills the space between Heaven and Earth is simply the myriad things. Therefore they are followed by Zhun. Zhun means fullness; Zhun is the first coming-to-be of things.” At their birth, things are knotted together and have not yet opened into free development; thus they fill and obstruct the space between Heaven and Earth. Once they develop freely and flourish, the sense of obstruction disappears. Heaven and Earth generate the myriad things, and Zhun is their first arising; therefore it follows Qian and Kun. In terms of the two images, the rising of clouds and thunder is the first intercourse of yin and yang. In terms of the two trigrams, Zhen is their first mingling below, Kan their first mingling in the middle. Yin and yang join, producing clouds and thunder. At the beginning of their union, clouds and thunder answer one another, but have not yet produced nourishing rain: hence Zhun. If the nourishing rain had already come, the hexagram would be Release. Movement within danger is another meaning of Zhun. When yin and yang do not join, there is Obstruction; when they have begun to join but have not yet achieved free development, there is Zhun. As a time, it is a period when all under Heaven is beset by difficulties and has not yet attained flourishing peace.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4548:002 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4548:003 -->

### Zhun: Great success. It is beneficial to be steadfast in what is right. Do not undertake to go anywhere. It is beneficial to establish a lord.[^j01-establish-lord]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4548:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4548:004 -->

**Original Meaning.** Zhen and Kan are names of three-line figures. In Zhen, one yang moves beneath two yin; its virtue is movement and its image thunder. In Kan, one yang is trapped between two yin; its virtues are entrapment and danger, and its images cloud, rain, and water. Zhun is the name of the six-line figure. It means difficulty: things have begun to grow but have not yet opened into free development. The written character therefore depicts a shoot piercing the earth, newly emerged but not yet extended. In this hexagram Zhen encounters Kan: Qian and Kun first join and encounter danger and entrapment. Hence its name, Zhun. Zhen's movement is below, Kan's danger above: this is the capacity to move within danger. The capacity to move may allow success, but being in danger makes it appropriate to preserve correctness rather than advance precipitately. Thus someone who obtains it in divination receives a prognostication of great success and benefit in correctness, but must not hastily set out. Moreover, First Nine is yang beneath yin and is the line that constitutes the hexagram's governing principle. It gives the image of someone whose worth enables him to place himself below others, win the people, and become their ruler. Thus someone divining about the establishment of a ruler who encounters this hexagram receives good fortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4548:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4548:005 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Zhun contains a Way to great success. In dealing with it, benefit lies in steadfast correctness: without correctness and firmness, how could difficulty be overcome? While difficulty is beginning, one cannot yet set out. Can the difficulties of all under Heaven be overcome by one person's strength alone? Assistance must be widely sought; hence “It is beneficial to establish a lord.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4548:005 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4548:006 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4548:006 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4548:007 -->

**Classified Conversations of Master Zhu:** Zhun is the time when yin and yang have not yet achieved free passage. In Limping, obstruction occurs in the midst of an ongoing course. In Oppression, resources are exhausted.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4548:007 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4548:008 -->

**A question:** “The Judgment says, ‘It is beneficial to establish a lord.’ Why does the *Original Meaning* take First Nine, yang beneath yin, as the governing line that constitutes the hexagram?” The reply: “The line that constitutes a hexagram's governing principle is always discussed under the hexagram statement. Zhun's First Nine and ‘It is beneficial to establish a lord,’ the fifth line of Great Possession, and the second line of Fellowship all follow this pattern.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4548:008 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4548:009 -->

**Another question:** “Does Zhun's ‘It is beneficial to establish a lord’ follow the same divinatory pattern as Qian's ‘It is beneficial to see a great person’?” The reply: “Yes. If someone divining about becoming a ruler receives it, the lord to be established is that person. If someone divining about establishing a ruler receives it, the lord to be established is the ruler.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4548:009 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4548:010 -->

**Zhao Rumei:** The hexagram statement gathers up the great meaning of the whole hexagram; the line statements investigate what it points to, distinguishing and illuminating it through the images and meanings of the six lines. Thus “good fortune; nothing is unfavorable” expresses success and benefit. “Lingering,” “milling about,” “recognizing the first signs, better to let go,” and “small correction” all express “Do not undertake to go anywhere.” The establishment of a lord in the first line makes explicit that the hexagram Judgment's “It is beneficial to establish a lord” was pronounced for that line. The other hexagrams follow this pattern.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4548:010 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4548:011 -->

**Hu Bingwen:** Zhun and Meng follow Qian and Kun. Their upper and lower trigrams include Zhen, Kan, and Gen, formed through Qian's and Kun's intercourse. Zhen is their initial intercourse, and therefore comes first. First Nine, one yang beneath yin, is the line that constitutes the hexagram's governing principle. “Great success” belongs to Zhen's movement; “beneficial to be steadfast in what is right” is said because Zhen encounters Kan. The text does not say that going forward is unfavorable, but that one must not lightly undertake it. The *Changes* twice says, “It is beneficial to establish a lord.” In Enthusiasm, the establishment of a lord belongs to Zhen above; in Zhun, to Zhen below. Zhen is the eldest son, and “thunder startles those a hundred li away”: both give the image of a territorial lord.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4548:011 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4548:012 -->

**Cai Qing:** Although both Zhun and Limping are glossed as difficulty, their meanings differ somewhat. Oppression differs again. Zhun is difficulty when one first sets foot on the road; Limping is difficulty midway; Oppression is final exhaustion, when the difficulty is most severe.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4548:012 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4548:013 -->

**He also says:** “It is beneficial to be steadfast in what is right” and “Do not undertake to go anywhere” are two expressions of one meaning. Thus the *Commentary on the Judgments* explains only the former.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4548:013 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4548:014 -->

**He also says:** The *Original Meaning*'s “yang placing itself beneath yin” and the *Commentary on the Images*' “the honored placing himself below the lowly” under First Nine both concern virtue, not rank. Hence “someone whose worth enables him to place himself below others, win the people, and become their ruler.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4548:014 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4548:015 -->

### First Nine: Lingering. It is beneficial to remain steadfast in what is right. It is beneficial to establish a lord.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4548:015 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4548:016 -->

**Original Meaning.** “Lingering” describes difficulty in advancing. At the beginning of these difficulties, yang is below and belongs to the trigram of movement, while its corresponding line above is yielding yin within danger and entrapment. Hence the image of lingering. Yet it occupies its proper position, so the prognostication favors remaining steadfastly correct. It is also the line that constitutes the hexagram's governing principle: yang places itself beneath yin, and the people turn toward it. This is the image of a lord. Hence this further image and prognostication: if the consulter is like this, it is beneficial to establish that person as lord.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4548:016 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4548:017 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The first is a yang line below: a person of firm, clear-sighted ability occupying a low position in an age of difficulty. He cannot immediately go forth and relieve those difficulties, so he lingers. At the beginning of difficulty, to advance abruptly instead of lingering is to plunge into danger. It is therefore appropriate to remain correct and make one's resolve firm. People facing difficulty seldom manage to preserve correctness. Without a steadfast and firm commitment, they will lose righteousness; how then could they relieve the difficulties of their time? Living in an age of difficulty, and himself obstructed below, he needs assistance. This is the Way both of dwelling amid difficulty and of overcoming it. Hence the meaning of establishing a lord is taken up: seeking assistance.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4548:017 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4548:018 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4548:018 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4548:019 -->

**Classified Conversations of Master Zhu — asked about “It is beneficial to establish a lord”:** “This phrase in the Judgment takes its meaning from First Nine, the line that constitutes the hexagram's governing principle. One yang beneath two yin gives the image of a worthy person placing himself below others, and of the people turning toward him. Hence the *Image* says, ‘The honored places himself below the lowly and greatly wins the people.’”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4548:019 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4548:020 -->

**Xiang Anshi:** Every hexagram has a governing line, which possesses the virtue of that hexagram. Thus Qian's fifth nine possesses Qian's virtue and is the line of heavenly virtue; Kun's second six possesses Kun's virtue and is the line of Earth's Way. Zhun is governed by First Nine, so its line statement entirely resembles the hexagram statement. “Lingering; it is beneficial to remain steadfast in what is right” means “Do not undertake to go anywhere.” It then also says, “It is beneficial to establish a lord”: there can be no doubt.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4548:020 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4548:021 -->

**Hu Bingwen:** Some of King Wen's hexagram statements speak specifically with reference to the line that constitutes the hexagram's governing principle. The Duke of Zhou first makes this clear in this line statement. Zhen governs the hexagram, and the first line governs Zhen. “Lingering” is “Do not undertake to go anywhere”; “beneficial to remain steadfast in what is right” is “beneficial to be steadfast in what is right.” When the hexagram says it is beneficial to establish a lord, it concerns the undertaking: establishing the first line as lord is beneficial. When the line says it is beneficial to establish a lord, it concerns the person: someone with the first line's ability is beneficial to establish as lord. The line's “lingering” speaks chiefly to the person who is to become lord: that person should proceed slowly. The hexagram's “beneficial to establish a lord, but not to rest easy” speaks chiefly to the one establishing the lord: that undertaking should not be delayed.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4548:021 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4548:022 -->

**Cai Qing:** “Remaining steadfastly correct” means not advancing precipitately when the time and circumstances do not yet permit advance. The line's lingering is what the hexagram calls Zhun. The line's benefit in remaining steadfastly correct is the hexagram statement's “It is beneficial to be steadfast in what is right; do not undertake to go anywhere.” “It is beneficial to establish a lord” should also be read as an image, with the prognostication contained within it—like “the son can manage the household.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4548:022 -->

### Six in the Second Place: Troubled and turning back. Riders mill about—not raiders, but seeking marriage. The woman remains steadfast and does not pledge herself. After ten years, she pledges herself.[^j01-zhun-marriage]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4548:023 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4548:024 -->

**Original Meaning.** “Milling about” describes being scattered and not advancing. *Zi*, “pledge herself,” means to consent to marriage. The *Rites* says, “When a woman is betrothed, she receives the hairpin and an adult name.” Six in the Second Place is yielding yin, centered and correct, with a corresponding line above. Yet it rides upon the firmness of the first line, and is therefore obstructed, turning back and unable to advance. The first line, however, is no raider: it only seeks a marriage alliance with her. She preserves correctness and therefore does not consent. When ten years have passed, the numerical cycle is exhausted and the principle reaches its limit: the improper suitor departs, the proper counterpart joins her, and she may consent. The line has this image and uses it to admonish the consulter.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4548:024 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4548:025 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The second line is yielding yin in an age of difficulty. Although its proper counterpart is above, it is pressed by the firm first line; hence its difficulty and turning back. *Ru* is a descriptive particle. Being on horseback means wishing to travel. She wishes to follow her proper counterpart, but again mills about and cannot advance. *Ban* has the sense of separation: dismounting separates the person from the horse. In this difficult time, the second line cannot rescue itself, but it is central, correctly placed, and has a counterpart above: it does not lose righteousness. Yet it is pressed by the nearby first line. Yin is sought by yang, and the yielding is overborne by the firm. Yieldingness in a time of difficulty already makes self-deliverance hard; the additional pressure of firm yang creates the obstruction. Were she not pressed by this raider's interference, she would go to seek her marriage partner.[^j01-zhun-readings] The marriage partner is her proper counterpart; the raider is one who approaches without rightful grounds. The second line keeps to centeredness and correctness and does not join casually with the first; hence she does not bear children. If she remains steadfast and unchanged until ten years have passed, difficulty at its limit must open into free passage. She then obtains her proper counterpart and bears and nurtures children. Even a woman, yielding yin, will eventually attain fulfillment if she can preserve her resolve and integrity. How much more a noble person who holds to the Way without turning aside! One may ask: “The first line is a worthy, clear-sighted, firm, and correct person. Why is it now a raider who presses and encroaches upon another?” The reply: “Here the meaning is taken solely from the second line's yieldingness beside firmness; the first line's particular virtue is not being considered. This is how the *Changes* draws its meanings.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4548:025 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4548:026 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4548:026 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4548:027 -->

**Zhang Jun:** “The woman remains steadfast and does not pledge herself; after ten years, she pledges herself” means that the second line holds to integrity and preserves its resolve in an age of hardship, without losing steadfast correctness. It is like the Grand Duke by the seashore, Yi Yin in the fields of Shen, or Kongming at Nanyang: righteousness forbids a careless alliance. This is the woman's constancy.[^j01-withdrawn-worthies]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4548:027 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4548:028 -->

**Classified Conversations of Master Zhu:** Master Geng explains “the woman remains steadfast and does not pledge herself” through the phrase “on betrothal, she receives the hairpin and an adult name.” Remaining steadfast and not receiving that name means not yet consenting to marriage. This fits the meaning of marriage alliance. Yichuan takes *zi* in the sense of bearing and nurturing children.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4548:028 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4548:029 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** The *Changes* says “not raiders, but seeking marriage” in three places: Zhun's second line, Adornment's fourth, and Opposition's top line. The *Original Meaning* and Cheng's Commentary differ; students may choose which to follow. Yet Adornment has no image of initial difficulty or estranged opposition, which makes some explanations of its line hard to sustain. Consider the wording closely: “Troubled and turning back; riders mill about” has precisely the same literary pattern as “Adorned, yet white; a white horse flies.” Both continue with “not raiders, but seeking marriage.” Thus “Troubled and turning back” and “Adorned, yet white” should each end a clause: they describe how the two lines themselves are situated. “Riders mill about” and “a white horse flies” should each be read with the following “not raiders, but seeking marriage”: the mounted figure over there is not a raider but my marriage partner. Here the milling riders signify the fifth line; Adornment's flying white horse signifies the first. “Not raiders, but seeking marriage” simply identifies the proper counterpart whom one may follow. This hexagram has thunder below and clouds above. Thunder's sound circles and returns, so “lingering” and “turning back” describe the lower trigram. Clouds spread and scatter, so “milling about” describes the upper trigram. Both the fourth and top lines speak of milling riders; the fifth line's milling riders are spoken of under Six in the Second Place. This too may be retained as one possible explanation.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4548:029 -->

### Six in the Third Place: Pursuing deer without a forester, one only enters deep into the woods. The noble person recognizes the first signs: better to let go. Going on brings humiliation.[^j01-deer]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4548:030 -->

**Original Meaning.** Yielding yin occupies the lower trigram, neither centered nor correctly placed, and has no proper counterpart above. Reckless movement brings distress: hence the image of pursuing deer without a forester and becoming trapped in the woods. The noble person recognizes the first signs and sees that it is better to abandon the pursuit. To go on chasing and refuse to let go must bring shame and humiliation. This is how the consulter is admonished.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4548:031 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4548:032 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Six in the Third Place is yielding in a firm position. Its yieldingness cannot rest content amid difficulty; its firm position, without centeredness or correctness, leads it to move recklessly. Though greedy for what it seeks, it can neither rescue itself nor find a corresponding ally. Where can it go? It is like pursuing deer without a forester. Anyone entering mountains and forests needs a forester as guide. Without one, there is nothing but entrapment in the wooded thickets. The noble person sees the first, subtle stirrings of the matter and recognizes that it is better to give up rather than pursue. Going on merely brings exhaustion and humiliation.

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4548:033 -->

### Six in the Fourth Place: Riders mill about, seeking marriage. Going brings good fortune; nothing is unfavorable.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4548:035 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4548:036 -->

**Original Meaning.** Yielding yin dwells amid difficulty and cannot advance upward; hence the image of milling riders. Yet First Nine preserves correctness below and corresponds with this line. Thus the prognostication is that seeking the marriage alliance below brings good fortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4548:036 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4548:037 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Six in the Fourth Place, yielding and compliant, occupies a position close to the ruler and enjoys favor above. Yet its ability is insufficient to relieve the difficulties, so it wishes to advance and then stops again: riders milling about. Since it cannot by itself relieve the difficulties of the time, it can succeed by seeking a worthy person to assist it. The worthy person of firm yang in the first place is its proper counterpart—its marriage partner. If it seeks this firm-yang alliance and goes together with it to assist the firm-yang, centered, and correct ruler, relieving the difficulties of the time, there will be good fortune and nothing unfavorable. Someone occupying the office of a high minister may lack the ability to relieve the difficulties unaided; but if he seeks worthy people below, draws close to them, and employs them, what could not be accomplished?

**Collected Explanations.**

**Hu Bingwen:** The general rule for lines is that upward movement is “going” and downward movement “coming.” Yet the fourth six's descent to follow the first is also called “going,” because from the standpoint of my approaching another, the wording must be “going,” not “coming.” Likewise, Waiting's top six says, “three people come.” From the standpoint of others approaching me, it can be called “coming,” not “going.”

### Nine in the Fifth Place: Its rich moisture is held back. Constancy in small matters brings good fortune; constancy in great matters brings misfortune.[^j01-small-great]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4548:040 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4548:041 -->

**Original Meaning.** Although Nine in the Fifth Place occupies the honored position with firm yang, centeredness, and correctness, it is a time of difficulty, and it is trapped within danger. It has the second six as its proper counterpart, but that line is yielding yin and weak in ability, unable to provide sufficient aid. First Nine wins the people below, and the multitude turns to it. The fifth nine belongs to Kan's constitution: it has rich, nourishing moisture but cannot bestow it. Hence the image of its rich moisture being held back. If the consulter is dealing with a small matter, preserving correctness may still bring good fortune. In a great matter, even correctness will not avert misfortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4548:041 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4548:042 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The fifth occupies the honored position correctly in a time of difficulty. With the assistance of a worthy person of firmness and clear understanding, it could relieve the difficulty. Because it lacks such a minister, however, “its rich moisture is held back.” Even in an age of hardship, the ruler's title and position need not be diminished. It is only that his actions cannot take effect and his virtuous beneficence cannot reach those below: this is his rich moisture being held back—the ruler's difficulty. If his beneficence cannot reach below, authority is no longer in his own hands. To try to rectify this suddenly after authority has passed from him is a way of courting misfortune. The affairs of Duke Zhao of Lu and the Duke of Gaogui Township illustrate this. Thus small correction brings good fortune. Small correction means setting things right gradually, as Pan Geng and King Xuan of Zhou cultivated virtue, employed the worthy, restored the government of the former kings, and brought the lords back to court. It means bringing matters around gently through the Way, without violence or haste. Nor does it mean resting complacently without acting, like the Tang emperors Xizong and Zhaozong. Inaction leaves one perpetually obstructed until destruction comes.[^j01-rulers-in-difficulty]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4548:042 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4548:043 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4548:043 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4548:044 -->

**Xiang Anshi:** Zhun does not take the fifth nine as its governing line because it establishes a lord to govern. The fifth is already in a high position; it is not a lord being newly established. First Nine moves within danger and therefore governs the overcoming of difficulty. At the obscure and unsettled beginning of Heaven's creative work, everything rises from below. If the fifth could govern the affair, there would be no Zhun.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4548:044 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4548:045 -->

**Wei Liaoweng:** The *Rites of Zhou* speaks of “great divination,” meaning divination about major affairs such as moving the state or establishing a ruler. The fifth is within danger: it is unfavorable to undertake substantial action. Only small matters are possible, not great ones. “Constancy in small matters brings good fortune; constancy in great matters brings misfortune” resembles the *Documents*' “Acting within brings good fortune; acting outside brings misfortune,” and “Stillness brings good fortune; action brings misfortune.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4548:045 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4548:046 -->

**Zhao Rumei:** I am still within danger, and my virtuous beneficence has not yet reached the people. The many yin below are unenlightened and have not yet come to trust me. Only by making government and instruction lenient, simplifying proclamations and commands, and allowing them gradually to come within my ordering of affairs can good fortune be attained. If I suddenly employ methods of strict regimentation and sweeping reform, people will be frightened and scatter in confusion. What greater misfortune could there be? Thus a newly established state uses a lenient penal code.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4548:046 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4548:047 -->

**Liang Yin:** Small correction means setting things right gradually. It brings good fortune because one remains in one's position and does what can be done. Great correction brings misfortune because the time and circumstances have already been lost, and the matter cannot be forced. Do what can be done when it can be done, and things follow. Attempt what cannot be done when it cannot be done, and there is misfortune. Can one fail to be careful?

### Top Six: Riders mill about; tears of blood stream down.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4548:048 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4548:049 -->

**Original Meaning.** Yielding yin, with no corresponding ally, occupies the end of difficulty. There is nowhere to advance: only anxiety and fear remain. Hence this image.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4548:049 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4548:050 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** This yielding-yin six occupies the end of difficulty and the extreme of danger, without a corresponding ally. Remaining where it is brings no peace; moving offers nowhere to go. It mounts a horse, wishing to depart, then mills about again without advancing. Its extremity of distress reaches the point of streaming tears of blood: difficulty at its utmost. Were it firm yang and supported by others, difficulty at its limit could be overcome.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4548:050 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4548:051 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4548:051 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4548:052 -->

**Liang Yin:** The extreme of difficulty should be the time of success. Yet Top Six is yielding yin and has no corresponding ally. It cannot leave danger behind: where, then, is its time of success? Kan is the trigram of blood and also of increased anxiety. Hence the image of streaming tears of blood.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4548:052 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4548:053 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** “A hexagram denotes a time; a line denotes a position.” These are explicit teachings of the sage's Classic, on which scholars of successive ages have based their explanations; they cannot be altered. Yet after long transmission, narrowness of vision often constrains interpretation and leads it into error. How? What commentators call “a time” becomes one particular historical moment; what they call “a position” becomes a position within that one moment. Thus they fix Zhun as an age of many troubles and take all six positions as people living in that same age and dealing with those troubles. On this basis, they make the second line obstructed by the first, and the fifth pressed by the first. The six lines of a hexagram are thereby reduced to use within a single situation, and the explanations become inconsistent and fail to accord with principle. This is a failing in the discussion of the Classic. The “times” spoken of in the hexagrams of the *Changes* belong to everyone. With Zhun, a scholar has a scholar's difficulty: living in straitened circumstances, not yet recognized. Rulers and ministers have their difficulties: purposes not yet fulfilled, achievements not yet completed. Even lowly commoners and merchants experience Zhun when the year is unprosperous or trade goes poorly. The statements attached by the sage can encompass the inexhaustible circumstances of all under Heaven through ten thousand generations. Were they devised for just one time or one affair? Understand this, and the first line has its own difficulty: virtue capable of action, but the time not yet arrived. The second has its own difficulty: a Way capable of finding a fitting union, but a time that calls for waiting. The fifth has its own difficulty: beneficence not yet able to spread far, so its action must be gradual. The other three lines follow the same principle. All belong to Zhun and therefore all have an image of difficulty; their positions differ, and each consequently has its own principle for dealing with difficulty. Where meanings are drawn from supporting, riding, neighboring, and corresponding, these too are symbolic configurations.[^j01-line-relations] Thus the second line's riding upon firmness gives only an image of multiple difficulties; it does not specifically point to the first line's being a lord. The fifth line's withheld moisture gives only an image of passage not yet opened; it is not caused by the first line's being a lord either. To say now that the second is blocked by the first and the fifth oppressed by it makes the first line the great obstruction of the whole hexagram, and the *Changes* a book for an age of decline. Could that be the sage's intention? The principles of all sixty-four hexagrams should be viewed by this example. Then the words need not become obstructed, and their meanings may be apprehended.[^j01-situation-position]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4548:053 -->

## 4. Meng 蒙 — Unformed Understanding

<!-- Source: https://www.eee-learning.com/book/4549 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4549:001 -->

![Meng](../assets/eee-learning/8f66a9f2b474-yi04b.png) **4. Meng 蒙 — Unformed Understanding** — Gen above; Kan below.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4549:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4549:002 -->

**Meng** — Kan below; Gen above.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4549:002 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4549:003 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Concerning Meng, the *Sequence of the Hexagrams* says: “Zhun means fullness; Zhun is the first coming-to-be of things. Whatever comes into being must at first be unformed. Therefore Zhun is followed by Meng. Meng means obscurity: the immaturity of things.” Zhun is the first arising of things. Newly arisen, they are young and small, their understanding still veiled and undeveloped; hence Meng follows Zhun. The hexagram has Gen above and Kan below. Gen is mountain and stopping; Kan is water and danger. Danger lies below the mountain. Encountering danger, one stops, not knowing where to go: this is the image of Meng. Water is something that must flow. When it first emerges, it has not yet found its destination, hence Meng; as it advances, the meaning becomes successful passage.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4549:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4549:004 -->

### Meng: Success. It is not I who seek the unknowing child; the unknowing child seeks me. At the first divination, I answer. A second and a third are irreverent; to irreverence I give no answer. It is beneficial to be steadfast in what is right.[^j01-meng-question]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4549:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4549:005 -->

**Original Meaning.** Gen too is the name of a three-line figure. One yang stops above two yin; its virtue is stopping and its image mountain. Meng means obscurity: at the beginning of life, things are veiled and have not yet become clear. In this hexagram Kan encounters Gen. Danger below the mountain is the setting of Meng; danger within and stopping without are its meaning. Hence the name Meng. From “success” onward, the text gives the prognostication. Nine in the Second Place governs the inner trigram. Firmness at the center gives it the ability to open another's unformed understanding, and it corresponds with Six in the Fifth Place as yang with yin. Thus someone encountering this hexagram has a Way to success. “I” is the second line. “The unknowing child,” young and immature in understanding, is the fifth. If the consulter is clear-sighted, others should seek me, and the success belongs to them. If the consulter lacks understanding, I should seek others, and the success belongs to me. When others seek me, I should respond according to whether a response is appropriate. When I seek others, I should bring the utmost purity and singleness of purpose to my questioning. Both the clear-sighted person's nurturing of the unformed and the unformed person's self-cultivation benefit from correctness.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4549:005 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4549:006 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Meng contains the principle of opening and development: this is the meaning of success. The hexagram's capacities accord with the center as the time requires; this is the Way to bringing about success. Six in the Fifth Place is the principal figure who is unformed; Nine in the Second Place is the one who opens that understanding. “I” refers to the second line. The second is not the principal unformed figure. Because the fifth follows the second compliantly and humbly, the second becomes the one who opens its understanding, and the words are spoken chiefly from its standpoint. “It is not I who seek the unknowing child; the unknowing child seeks me”: the fifth occupies the honored position and possesses yielding, compliant virtue, but is still unformed. It properly corresponds with the second and shares its central virtue, so it can use the second's Way to open its understanding. The second, below, possesses the virtue of firmness at the center and is trusted and sought by the ruler. It should preserve itself through the Way and wait until the ruler seeks it with complete sincerity before responding. Only then can its Way be put into practice. It is not I who seek the unknowing child; the unknowing child comes to seek me. *Shi* means deciding through divination. “At the first divination, I answer” means that I answer when sought with complete sincerity and undivided intention. A second and third approach become profanation and disrespect, so I do not answer. The Way of opening unformed understanding benefits from steadfast correctness. Moreover, although the second is firm and central, it occupies a yin position, so a warning is appropriate.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4549:006 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4549:007 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4549:007 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4549:008 -->

**Classified Conversations of Master Zhu:** When someone comes to seek me, I should judge whether a response is appropriate and then answer. That is, I consider the person seeking me to open an unformed understanding: if there is the sincerity of the first divination, I answer; if there is repeated, tiresome importunity, I do not. When I seek another, I should bring the utmost purity and singleness of purpose to my questioning. That is, when I seek another to open my understanding, I should fully embody the sincerity of the first divination and avoid the irreverence of repeated demands.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4549:008 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4549:009 -->

**Xiang Anshi:** Wait until the learner seeks instruction before teaching. The learner's heart then responds without resistance. Guide with undivided purpose, and the reception of instruction will be like an answering echo.[^j01-meng-readings]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4549:009 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4549:010 -->

**Hu Bingwen:** Once Heaven and Earth exist, there are rulers and teachers. Qian and Kun are followed by Zhun, which takes Zhen's single yang as its governing principle and says, “It is beneficial to establish a lord”: this is the ruler's Way. Meng follows, taking Kan's single yang as its governing principle and saying, “The unknowing child seeks me”: this is the teacher's Way. Both the ruler's and the teacher's Ways benefit from steadfast correctness.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4549:010 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4549:011 -->

**Yu Yan:** “Irreverence” is the same word as in the “Lesser Rules of Conduct”: “Do not treat the spirits irreverently.” “I give no answer” has the same meaning as the *Odes*, “Xiao Min”: “My tortoise is already weary; it will not tell me its counsel.”[^j01-xiaomin] At the first divination, the intention is concentrated and single, so there is an answer. A second and third become tiresome and irreverent, so there is none. The Way by which an unknowing child seeks a teacher is the same as that by which a person seeks the spirits.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4549:011 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4549:012 -->

**Lin Xiyuan:** If the unknowing child does not seek me, there is no eagerness to ask or willingness to learn. How could I obtain the learner's attendance and bring about trust in me? If the learner seeks me but sincerity is not yet complete, there is no diligence in concentrating the heart and applying the will. How could I rouse that indolence and secure a hearing? Wait until the learner seeks me before opening the understanding: trust will then be deep, and a word, once offered, will enter at once. Wait until sincerity is complete before opening the understanding: the desire for the Way will then be keen, and one opening will bring comprehension. This is how the unformed attain success.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4549:012 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4549:013 -->

### First Six: Opening unformed understanding. Punishment is beneficial. Release the fetters and shackles. To press on brings humiliation.[^j01-meng-punishment]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4549:013 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4549:014 -->

**Original Meaning.** Yin in the lowest place is unformed understanding at its deepest. A consulter encountering this should open that understanding. The way to do so, however, is to administer a sharp correction, then leave the person alone for a while and observe what follows. To keep pressing without relenting brings shame and humiliation. This is how the consulter is admonished.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4549:014 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4549:015 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The first line is dark yin below: the unformed understanding of the common people. The line describes how to open it. To open the common people's understanding, one should make penal prohibitions clear and display them, causing people to know fear; afterward one follows this with instruction and guidance. From antiquity, sage kings, in governing, established punishments to bring their people into order and made moral instruction clear to improve their customs. Punishments were established before instruction took effect. Although the sages esteemed virtue rather than punishment, they never abolished either in favor of the other. Thus, at the beginning of government, establishing laws comes first. At the beginning of addressing ignorance, inspiring fear through punishment serves to remove the fetters and shackles of that ignorance. Fetters and shackles mean constraints. Unless the constraints of darkened understanding are removed, sound teaching has no way to enter. Once people are guided by penal prohibitions, even if their hearts do not yet understand, they will follow out of fear of authority and dare not indulge their unenlightened desires. Gradually they can then come to know the good Way and change their erring hearts, making the transformation of habits and customs possible. But if government relies exclusively on punishment, the ignorant may be afraid without ever becoming enlightened. They will merely seek to escape punishment and have no sense of shame; the work of government and moral transformation cannot be completed. Hence pressing on in this fashion brings humiliation.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4549:015 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4549:016 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4549:016 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4549:017 -->

**Wang Anshi:** If one does not discern the problem early and correct it while it is small, the difficulty of ignorance reaches its extreme. At its beginning, to fail to set the law right and punish small faults, but instead release the fetters and shackles and let the person go unchecked, is the Way to humiliation.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4549:017 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4549:018 -->

**Wang Zongchuan:** What is called punishing people means setting the laws right and making them visible, establishing safeguards and restraints, making offenses understood, and prohibiting them in advance. Those whose understanding is veiled thereby learn caution and fear. They may wish to indulge themselves but dare not do so; afterward they gradually come to know the good Way and can be transformed. At such a time, if one releases their fetters and shackles instead of restraining them in advance, then, once this point has been passed, they can no longer be controlled. Thus, at the beginning of opening unformed understanding, applying punishment is considered beneficial, while releasing the fetters and shackles is considered humiliating.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4549:018 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4549:019 -->

**Hu Bingwen:** “Punishment is beneficial” means administering a sharp correction. “Release the fetters and shackles” means leaving the person alone for a while to observe what follows. To correct sharply without any respite, proceeding with unrelieved severity, is to be ignorant of the Way expressed by “Reverently spread the five teachings, with lenience.” Hence humiliation.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4549:019 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4549:020 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** According to the two Masters Wang, “Punishment is beneficial; release the fetters and shackles and let matters proceed, and there is humiliation” simply consists of an affirmative statement followed by its negative counterpart. It is just like “The army goes forth under discipline” and “Losing discipline brings misfortune.”

### Nine in the Second Place: Embracing the unformed: good fortune. Taking a wife: good fortune. The son can manage the household.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4549:021 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4549:022 -->

**Original Meaning.** Nine in the Second Place, firm yang, governs the inner trigram and directs the many yin. It bears responsibility for opening unformed understanding. Yet its charge is wide, and the natures of things are unequal: it cannot insist on one uniform outcome from all. The line's virtue is firm without excess, giving the image of a capacity to embrace and accommodate. Yang receiving yin also gives the image of taking a wife. Occupying a lower position while being able to undertake the affairs of those above gives the image of a son who can manage the household. Thus, if the consulter possesses this virtue and encounters the appropriate affair, the outcome is like this, and fortunate.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4549:022 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4549:023 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** “Embracing” means containing and accommodating. The second line lives in a time of unformed understanding, possessing firmness and clarity of ability. It corresponds with the ruler, Six in the Fifth Place, and shares the virtue of centrality: it is the one charged with the work of the time. It must broaden its capacity to accommodate, treating the benighted and foolish with compassion. Then it can open the understanding of all under Heaven and accomplish the work of educating the unformed. Its Way is extensive, its application wide: thus there is good fortune. The hexagram has only two yang lines. Top Nine is firm to excess; only Nine in the Second Place has the virtue of firmness at the center and corresponds with the fifth. Employed by the time, it alone possesses clarity. If it relies on that clarity and insists on taking everything upon itself, its virtue will not be expansive. Thus it should accept what is good even from a woman, despite her yielding obscurity; its clarity will thereby become broad. The other lines are yin, which is another reason for speaking of a wife. Even Yao and Shun, whose sageliness none under Heaven could match, “inquired discerningly of the common people,” drawing on others in the practice of goodness. If the second can embrace and receive, it can accomplish its ruler's affairs, just as a son can manage his household. Since the fifth is yielding yin, the entire achievement of opening its understanding rests with the second. Speaking in terms of a household, the fifth is the father and the second the son. The second's ability to direct the work of education is the son's ability to manage the household.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4549:023 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4549:024 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4549:024 -->

**Yang Wanli:** The fifth seeks the second; the second does not seek the fifth. Why, then, does the text say, “The son can manage the household”? Even if he brings his ruler to the stature achieved through Yi Yin or the Duke of Zhou, this remains within the minister's and son's proper duty. It is only like a son's managing his household, not an achievement to claim as his own.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4549:025 -->

Occupying a lower position while taking responsibility for the affairs of the higher gives the image of the son managing the household. This is said in terms of correspondence with the fifth.

**Liang Yin:** Yang is firm and clear; yin is yielding and obscure. Thus yin represents those whose understanding is unformed, and yang those who open it. The hexagram has only two yang lines. Nine in the Second Place occupies the center with firmness, governs the inner trigram, and corresponds with the fifth. Who but this second nine could bear the responsibility of opening unformed understanding and fully realize its Way? Yang receiving yin is taking a wife, which speaks of mutual accord in their intentions. Undertaking affairs while occupying a lower position is the son's ability to manage the household, which speaks of effective ability. With such a prognostication, the good fortune is clear.

### Top Nine: Striking at ignorance. It is not beneficial to become a raider; it is beneficial to ward off raiders.

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4549:052 -->

**Original Meaning.** Firmness occupies the top and treats the unformed with excessive rigidity, giving the image of striking at ignorance. Only by warding off external enticements and preserving the original purity intact can even an excess of strictness and vigilance be fitting. This is how the consulter is admonished. It applies to all affairs, not only to teaching others.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4549:052 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4549:053 -->

Shun's campaign against the Youmiao and the Duke of Zhou's punishment of the Three Overseers were warding off raiders. ## 5. Xu 需 — Waiting

<!-- Source: https://www.eee-learning.com/book/4550 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4550:001 -->

![Xu](../assets/eee-learning/39d9a8749e7b-yi05b.png) **5. Xu 需 — Waiting** — Kan above; Qian below.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4550:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4550:002 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Concerning Xu, the *Sequence of the Hexagrams* says: “Meng means obscurity: the immaturity of things. Immature things cannot go unnourished. Therefore Meng is followed by Xu. Xu is the Way of food and drink.” Young and immature things must await nourishment before they can reach completion. What is needed to nourish them is food and drink; hence “Xu is the Way of food and drink.” Clouds rise into Heaven, giving the image of moisture steaming upward. Food and drink moisten and benefit living things, so Xu is the Way of food and drink and follows Meng. The hexagram's general meaning, however, is waiting. The *Sequence* merely takes up the greatest of our needs. Qian's vigorous nature must advance, yet it stands below Kan's danger. Danger obstructs it; hence it must wait before advancing.[^j01-waiting-need]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4550:002 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4550:003 -->

### Xu: There is sincerity. Radiant success. Steadfast correctness brings good fortune. It is beneficial to cross the great river.[^j01-fu]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4550:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4550:004 -->

**Original Meaning.** Xu means waiting. Qian encounters Kan: Qian is vigorous, Kan dangerous. To meet danger with firmness without rushing forward and becoming trapped in it is the meaning of waiting. Sincerity is trustworthiness held within. In this hexagram, Nine in the Fifth Place has Kan's solid center: firm yang, centered and correct, occupying the honored position. This gives the image of possessing sincerity and attaining correctness. Kan's water lies ahead, and Qian's vigor faces it: the image of being about to cross water without advancing heedlessly. Thus, if the consulter has something to await and can maintain sincerity, there will be radiant success. If correctness is also attained, there will be good fortune, and crossing the great river will be beneficial. Correctness and firmness are unfavorable nowhere; crossing a river especially values the ability to wait, rather than seek speed and plunge into danger.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4550:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4550:005 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Xu means having to wait. In terms of the two trigrams, Qian's firmness and vigor advance upward and encounter danger; they cannot yet proceed. Hence the meaning of waiting. In terms of the hexagram's capacities, the fifth occupies the ruler's position and governs Xu. It possesses the virtues of firmness and vigor, centeredness and correctness, while sincerity and trustworthiness fill its interior. A solid center means possessing sincerity. With sincerity there is radiance and the capacity for successful passage; attaining steadfast correctness brings good fortune. Waiting in this way, what could one fail to accomplish? Even danger presents no insurmountable difficulty. Hence it is beneficial to cross the great river. Whenever the text says “steadfast correctness brings good fortune,” one must distinguish between “already correct and also fortunate” and “fortunate if correctness is attained.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4550:005 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4550:006 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4550:006 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4550:007 -->

**Classified Conversations of Master Zhu:** Xu means patient endurance. Firmness encounters danger; with the time as it is, one can only endure patiently and wait. People crossing rivers, for example, often bring upon themselves capsizing and drowning because they cannot be patient. Thus the hexagram Xu speaks at the outset of benefit in crossing the great river.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4550:007 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4550:008 -->

**Xiang Anshi:** Waiting does not mean never advancing. It means possessing substance within, encountering danger, and waiting before advancing. Anyone who waits does so because there is some substantial ground within for what is awaited. If I truly possess it and can only wait a little, radiant success must become possible. If I possess nothing, what is there to await? Hence “Xu: there is sincerity; radiant success.” Radiant success must not lead to self-satisfaction; one must carry it through with reverence and care. Hence “Steadfast correctness brings good fortune.” Truly practice this, and the waiting will not be empty, nor the advance end in drowning. Hence “It is beneficial to cross the great river.” “There is sincerity; radiant success; steadfast correctness brings good fortune” is the Way of waiting. “It is beneficial to cross the great river” is its result.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4550:008 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4550:009 -->

**Hu Bingwen:** Waiting without substance offers no time of radiance and success. Waiting without correctness offers no ground for good fortune and benefit. There are people whose hearts are sincere but whose handling of affairs is not entirely correct. Thus the text speaks both of sincerity and of steadfast correctness.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4550:009 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4550:010 -->

**Lin Xiyuan:** In undertaking anything, people generally demand completion at once. Yet obstacles often make immediate completion impossible; the circumstances leave no choice but to wait. Those compelled to wait, however, are often unhappy about it. Their waiting need not proceed from inward sincerity: they cannot escape urgency and eager expectation. Their hearts therefore do not open. Much within remains dark, cramped, and obstructed rather than bright and spacious. Thus the sage specifically brings out the meaning of possessing sincerity. When circumstances do not yet permit action, rest content with righteousness and what is allotted; wait composedly for the meeting of opportunity, without anxious insistence or swollen expectations. The waiting will then proceed from reality, not pretense. The heart is at ease and grows better day by day; one's inner bearing is clear and unimpeded. Is this not radiance and spaciousness? Yet even if the heart is at peace with waiting, the undertaking may not arise from correctness, and so may still fail to come to completion. Everything awaited must arise from what is right, without courting danger in the hope of a lucky escape. Then, as the work deepens, its effects are attained; when the time moves, the undertaking rises. What was formerly awaited is now all coming into order. Hence good fortune.

**Editorial Judgment.** The meaning of waiting is exceedingly broad. In its greatest applications, nothing surpasses the kingly Way's bringing about transformation over a long period, without hastening after shallow, immediate achievements; or the learning of the sages, which dwells in virtue with spacious patience, without falling into the faults of fixing upon results and forcing their growth.[^j01-forcing-growth] Of this hexagram's lines, only Nine in the Fifth Place occupies the honored position with firmness and vigor, centeredness and correctness, and can fully realize the Way of waiting. Thus the *Commentary on the Judgments* singles out this line to embody the meaning of the hexagram statement, and the *Great Image Commentary* likewise draws specifically on this line's words to encompass the whole meaning of Xu. Following Zhun and Meng, government and teaching have both been established. What remains is rest and recuperation, nurture and the flourishing of life: allowing people to enjoy their enjoyments and benefit from their benefits, steeping them in benevolence and shaping them through righteousness, until generations change and customs are transformed. Does this not belong to waiting? To contemplate Xu without understanding this line, and merely subsume it under the other lines' partial concern with dealing with danger, would leave no difference between Xu and Limping or Oppression.

**General Discussion.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4550:051 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4550:052 -->

**Jiang Tisheng:** Xu means waiting. Possessing firmness and vigor of ability, one encounters danger and entrapment ahead and should bear with it, await the time, employ yieldingness, and give priority to stillness.[^j01-xu-final-reading] If one fails to assess the time and circumstances, relies on firmness, and advances abruptly in anger and impatience, defeat and destruction are certain. First Nine is still far from danger and avoids blame through constancy. Nine in the Second Place draws nearer danger but also attains good fortune in the end through employing yieldingness and preserving the center. Nine in the Third Place has already come close against danger; its *Image* says that reverence and care avert defeat. Six in the Fourth Place has already been injured by danger, but through yieldingness and noncontention can emerge from the pit. Top Six is at the extreme of dangerous entrapment, yet also attains good fortune in the end through the capacity for respect. Thus, in a time of waiting, those who can bear patiently and maintain reverence can all escape calamity. Great indeed is the meaning of Xu's time!

## 6. Song 訟 — Contention

<!-- Source: https://www.eee-learning.com/book/4551 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4551:001 -->

![Song](../assets/eee-learning/8f190844506c-yi06b.png) **6. Song 訟 — Contention** — Qian above; Kan below.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4551:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4551:002 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Concerning Song, the *Sequence of the Hexagrams* says: “Food and drink must give rise to contention. Therefore Xu is followed by Song.” What people need is food and drink. Once there are needs, dispute and contention have a source; hence Song follows Xu. The hexagram has Qian above and Kan below. In terms of the two images, Heaven's yang moves upward, while water's nature is to descend. Their courses oppose one another, producing contention. In terms of the two trigrams, firmness is above and danger below. When firmness and danger meet, can contention be absent? Moreover, a person who is treacherous within and forceful without thereby gives rise to contention.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4551:002 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4551:003 -->

### Song: There is sincerity, but it is obstructed. Be vigilant: keeping to the middle brings good fortune; pursuing the matter to the end brings misfortune. It is beneficial to see a great person. It is not beneficial to cross the great river.[^j01-contention]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4551:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4551:004 -->

**Original Meaning.** Song means disputing and arguing a case. Qian is above, Kan below. Qian is firm, Kan dangerous: firmness above controls those below, while danger below watches for an opening against those above. The figure also means danger within and vigor without, or danger in oneself and vigor in the other. All are ways that lead to contention. Nine in the Second Place has a solid center but no corresponding ally above; it also belongs to the trigram of increased anxiety. Moreover, in hexagram transformation, Song comes from Withdrawal: firmness comes to occupy the second place, at the center of the lower trigram.[^j01-song-transformation] Thus there is an image of possessing sincerity yet meeting obstruction, being able to feel apprehension and attain the center. Top Nine is excessively firm and occupies the extreme of contention, giving the image of pursuing contention to its furthest end. Nine in the Fifth Place occupies the honored position with firmness and vigor, centeredness and correctness: the image of a great person. Firmness riding upon danger, solidity treading over entrapment, gives the image of crossing the great river being unfavorable. Thus the consulter is warned that a dispute is bound to be involved, and that good or ill fortune depends on the position occupied.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4551:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4551:005 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The Way of contention must have sincerity and substance behind it. Without substance within, it is merely false accusation and reckless assertion—the Way to misfortune. The hexagram's solid center gives the image of sincerity. Contention means disputing with another and awaiting a decision from someone else. Even where sincerity exists, there must still be obstruction and a lack of free passage; without obstruction, the matter would already be clear and there would be no dispute. Since the case is not yet distinguished, good or ill fortune cannot be assured. Hence apprehensive vigilance. “Keeping to the middle brings good fortune” means that attaining the center is fortunate. “Pursuing the matter to the end brings misfortune” means carrying it to its utmost. Contention seeks to distinguish what is crooked from what is straight; hence it is beneficial to see a great person.[^j01-song-readings] A great person can decide the dispute through firmness, clarity, centeredness, and correctness. Contention is not a peaceful and harmonious affair. One should choose secure ground on which to stand, not fall into danger. Hence crossing the great river is unfavorable.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4551:005 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4551:006 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4551:006 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4551:007 -->

**Kong Yingda:** “Obstructed” means blocked; “vigilant” means apprehensive. A dispute must never be raised recklessly: there must be trustworthiness and substance. If, when obstructed by circumstances, one can remain apprehensive and stop midway, good fortune is attained. “Pursuing the matter to the end brings misfortune”: contention must not be prolonged. If one carries the dispute through to its very end, there will be misfortune even with obstruction and vigilance. “It is beneficial to see a great person”: once a dispute exists, a great person is needed to decide it. If one goes from contention into danger and difficulty, calamity is certain. Hence crossing the great river is unfavorable.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4551:007 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4551:008 -->

**Hu Yuan:** Sincerity is trustworthiness proceeding from within. When people raise a dispute, they must possess such inward trustworthiness but be obstructed by another, leaving no alternative. Yet even if one has sincerity and substance and is obstructed by someone else, one must still be fearful, watchful, and careful, not daring to rest complacently. Only thus may one hope to escape disaster. Stop midway as well, and good fortune may be attained. The great river means great danger and difficulty. To pass through danger and overcome difficulty, the dispositions of those involved must accord, their purposes and energies harmonious and united. Only then can the crossing be accomplished. In a time of contention, their dispositions oppose one another and lack accord. To seek passage through danger and difficulty is therefore impossible.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4551:008 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4551:009 -->

**Classified Conversations of Master Zhu:** Hexagram statements generally draw their meanings from more than one source. In Song, “There is sincerity, but it is obstructed; be vigilant; keeping to the middle brings good fortune” takes its image from Nine in the Second Place's solid center and Kan's increased anxiety. “Pursuing the matter to the end brings misfortune” takes its image from Top Nine's carrying contention to its extreme. “It is beneficial to see a great person” takes its image from Nine in the Fifth Place's firmness and vigor, centeredness and correctness, in the honored position. “It is not beneficial to cross the great river” again takes its image from firmness riding on danger and solidity treading over entrapment. These meanings are drawn in different ways. Yet not every case must be interpreted like this; it is only one pattern. A hexagram statement such as this is especially orderly, because the meanings drawn from the individual lines all agree with those lines' own statements. There are also cases in which a line's meaning is drawn upon but differs from the meaning of its own statement.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4551:009 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4551:010 -->

**Xiang Anshi:** “It is beneficial to see a great person”: such a person may refuse to contest the matter, like Zhi Buyi; reconcile the parties, like Zhuo Mao; transform their hearts, like Wang Lie; or make the case clear, like Zhong You. All benefit those involved in contention.[^j01-peacemakers] “It is not beneficial to cross the great river”: success in traversing danger lies in unity of heart. Can this be a time for fighting one another?

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4551:010 -->

## 7. Shi 師 — The Army

<!-- Source: https://www.eee-learning.com/book/4552 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4552:001 -->

![Shi](../assets/eee-learning/b48a97f1a1f3-yi07b.png) **7. Shi 師 — The Army** — Kan below; Kun above.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4552:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4552:002 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Concerning Shi, the *Sequence of the Hexagrams* says: “Contention must bring a multitude into action. Therefore Song is followed by Shi.” An army arises because there is conflict; hence it follows Contention. The hexagram has Kun above and Kan below. In terms of the two trigrams, water within Earth gives the image of a gathering multitude. In terms of their meanings, there is danger within and compliance without: to proceed along a dangerous Way through compliance is the meaning of the army. In terms of the lines, one yang governs the many yin, giving the image of directing a multitude. In Holding Together, one yang governs the many yin from above: the image of a ruler. In The Army, one yang governs them from below: the image of a commander.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4552:002 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4552:003 -->

### Shi: Steadfast correctness. A seasoned elder brings good fortune and no blame.[^j01-seasoned-elder]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4552:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4552:004 -->

**Original Meaning.** Shi means a multitude of troops. Kan is below, Kun above; Kan is danger, Kun compliance; Kan is water, Kun earth. In antiquity, military forces were lodged within the farming population: utmost danger lay hidden within great compliance, and the unfathomable was concealed within utmost stillness. Moreover, this hexagram has only one yang, Nine in the Second Place, at the center of the lower trigram: the image of a commander. The five yin above and below follow compliantly: the image of the multitude. The second nine, firm and below, conducts affairs; the fifth six, yielding and above, entrusts it with the task. This is the image of a ruler appointing a commander to send forth an army. Hence the hexagram's name, Shi. “A seasoned elder” is a designation for someone mature and experienced. In employing an army, benefit lies in attaining correctness and entrusting the task to such a person. Only then are good fortune and freedom from blame attained. The consulter too must be admonished in this way.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4552:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4552:005 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The Way of the army takes correctness as its foundation. To raise an army and mobilize the multitude, bringing affliction upon all under Heaven, without correctness would win no following from the people; it would merely drive them by force. Thus steadfast correctness is the army's governing concern. Yet even when its action is correct, its leader must be a seasoned elder before there can be good fortune and no blame. There can be good fortune with blame, and freedom from blame without good fortune. Only good fortune together with no blame is wholly good. “A seasoned elder” designates someone respected and authoritative. Unless the commander of the army and director of the multitude is respected, trusted, feared, and obeyed, how can people's hearts follow? Thus, when Sima Rangju was raised from humble obscurity and entrusted with the troops, he requested Zhuang Jia as a commander because the people's hearts were not yet obedient.[^j01-commanders] The seasoned elder need not have long occupied exalted rank. If the multitude stands in awe of and submits to his ability, strategy, virtue, and achievements, that is sufficient. After Rangju executed Zhuang Jia, the multitude became awed and obedient: he was then a seasoned elder. Likewise, the Marquis of Huaiyin rose from humble obscurity to become a great general because his planning and conduct could inspire respect and awe.

### First Six: The army goes forth under discipline. Without sound order, misfortune.[^j01-army-discipline]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4552:009 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4552:010 -->

**Original Meaning.** Discipline means law. “Without sound order” means what is not good. Master Chao says: “Earlier scholars often wrote *bu*, ‘not,’ for *fou*; this is correct.” The first line of the hexagram is the beginning of the army's undertaking. The Way of sending forth an army requires care at the beginning. Under discipline there is good fortune; without sound order, misfortune. The consulter is admonished to take care at the beginning and keep to the law.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4552:010 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4552:011 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The first is the army's beginning, so it speaks of the rightness of raising an army and the Way of conducting it. Regarding a state's raising an army, to accord with righteousness and principle is to act under law and discipline: it means moving to suppress disorder and punish violence. If the movement is not righteous, then even success is a Way to misfortune. Success here means victory; misfortune means afflicting the people and injuring righteousness. Regarding the conduct of an army, discipline means commands and regulations. The Way of conducting an army is founded on commands and regulations, by which the multitude is controlled. Without discipline, even success is misfortune: even a victory remains a Way to misfortune. There have been cases of armies directed without proper rules that luckily escaped defeat and even won. This is what the sage warns against.

**Wang Bi:** This is the army's beginning, when its ranks are brought into order. Bring the multitude into order through discipline; lose discipline, and there is defeat and dispersal.

### Nine in the Second Place: At the army's center, good fortune. No blame. The king thrice bestows his commission.[^j01-army-center]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4552:016 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4552:017 -->

**Original Meaning.** Nine in the Second Place stands below, and the many yin turn toward it. It possesses the virtue of firmness at the center, corresponds upward with the fifth, and is favored and entrusted with responsibility. Hence this image and prognostication.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4552:017 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4552:018 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** In The Army, only Nine in the Second Place is yang, and the many yin turn toward it. The fifth occupies the ruler's position and is its proper counterpart. The second is therefore the army's commander, exercising sole authority over its affairs. To stand below yet exercise sole authority is permissible only in the army. From antiquity, an appointed commander has been allowed independent control of affairs beyond the threshold. Within the army, exercising this authority while attaining the middle Way brings good fortune and no blame. Relying arrogantly on sole authority would lose the Way of a subordinate; lacking such authority would leave no means of achieving the task. Thus attaining the center is fortunate. In the Way of the army, good fortune comes when authority and harmony are both fully present. Having handled the matter with complete excellence, the commander can accomplish the task and bring peace to all under Heaven. Hence the king bestows favor and commissions as many as three times. Whenever a matter reaches three, it reaches fullness. Six in the Fifth Place above not only relies exclusively on this commander but also grants abundant marks of favor. Unless the ceremonial honors are commensurate, authority lacks weight and those below will not trust it. Other hexagrams also have a second nine entrusted by a fifth six, but only in The Army does it exclusively govern affairs and receive the allegiance of the many yin. Thus the meaning here is greatest. In the minister's Way, one dares not exercise exclusive authority in other affairs; only those beyond the threshold permit it. Yet even when command lies in one's own hands, whatever the army's strength enables one to accomplish has been provided by the ruler and belongs to one's official duty. Some scholars, discussing Lu's sacrifice to the Duke of Zhou with the rites and music of the Son of Heaven, argue that, because the Duke accomplished what no minister could accomplish, he could use rites and music forbidden to ministers. This is ignorance of the minister's Way. Occupy the Duke of Zhou's position, and one performs the Duke of Zhou's work. Whatever that position enables one to do is precisely what one ought to do. The Duke of Zhou merely fulfilled his office. The son's Way is the same. Mencius alone understood this meaning when he said, “Serving one's parents as Master Zeng did is acceptable.” He never regarded Master Zeng's filial devotion as exceeding what was due. Whatever a son can do is what he ought to do.[^j01-delegation-duty]

**Collected Explanations.**

The problem is not only that its ability is yielding yin, without centeredness or correctness. In military affairs, responsibility must be undivided. The second, with the ability of firmness at the center, is already trusted and relied upon by those above. It must have sole charge before success can be achieved. If one additionally lets a multitude of others direct the undertaking, this is the Way to misfortune. *Yu shi* means “many masters,” pointing to the third line. <!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4552:028 -->

**Original Meaning.** Camping to the left means withdrawing to an encampment. Yielding yin is not central, but occupies a yin position and attains correctness. Hence this image. Assessing what is appropriate and advancing or withdrawing accordingly is what ought to be done; hence no blame. Advance when the opportunity is visible; withdraw when difficulty is recognized: this is the army's normal course. Here only the appropriateness of the withdrawal is considered, not whether the line's ability is adequate. To judge that victory is impossible and withdraw with the army intact is far better than defeat and destruction. It is withdrawal when advance is possible that incurs blame. Profound is the benevolence with which the *Changes* brings out this meaning for later generations.

### Six in the Fifth Place: There is game in the fields. It is beneficial to seize it. No blame. The elder son leads the army; younger sons carry corpses. Even with steadfast correctness, misfortune.[^j01-field-game]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4552:033 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4552:034 -->

**Original Meaning.** Six in the Fifth Place governs the employment of the army. Yielding, compliant, and central, it does not initiate hostilities. The enemy attacks, and it responds only because there is no alternative. Hence the image of game in the fields, and the prognostication that capturing it is beneficial and without blame. *Yan* is a grammatical particle. The elder son is Nine in the Second Place; the younger sons are the third and fourth lines. The consulter is also warned to keep the delegation of authority undivided. If one entrusts the work to a noble person but also lets petty people interfere, one is making the army return bearing corpses. Thus even steadfast correctness cannot avert misfortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4552:034 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4552:035 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The fifth is the ruler's position, governing the raising of the army, so it speaks of the Way to raise an army and appoint its commander. An army should be raised only when the Man and Yi disturb the Xia, or raiders, robbers, and malefactors harm the people, and they cannot be won over and brought into accord.[^j01-army-ethics] Only then is a declared charge advanced and punishment carried out. It is like birds and beasts entering cultivated fields and damaging the crops: if righteousness calls for hunting and capturing them, then hunt and capture them. Acting in this way alone avoids blame. To act lightly and afflict all under Heaven would incur great blame. “Taking up the words” means advancing the declared charge: making the offenses clear and campaigning against them. The Qin emperor and Han Wudi were like men exhausting the mountains and forests in search of game, not dealing with game that had entered the fields. In appointing a commander and entrusting the army, one should have the elder son lead. The second stands below and governs the army: it is the elder son. If the younger sons are made its many masters, then even a correct undertaking brings misfortune. “Younger sons” means all who are not the senior. From antiquity, divided command has led to disastrous defeat: examples are Xun Linfu of Jin at the battle of Bi and Guo Ziyi of Tang in the defeat at Xiangzhou.

**Cheng's Commentary.** The top is the army's end and the completion of its achievement. The great ruler rewards those who have served through titles and commissions. Petty people easily become arrogant and overfull even in ordinary times; how much more when they rely on their achievements! If one did speak in terms of the line, Six is yielding at the utmost of compliance. **He also says:** Men of intelligence and courage are not necessarily complete in all their abilities and virtues. **Editorial Judgment.** “Do not employ petty people” does not mean employing them and then withholding fiefs, nor granting fiefs and then withholding employment. It means not employing them from the outset. The statements that a seasoned elder brings good fortune and younger sons bring misfortune already apply when the army first goes forth. Masters Hu and Lin both accord with the hexagram's intention. Yet here the words “petty people” seem to have a wider scope. The passage is not concerned exclusively with those who have rendered military service. Rather, it says that, after disorder has been settled, offices must be established for the worthy alone. Petty people must not again be employed, lest they become the root of another day's disorder. Thus, in Release, when difficulty has already been pacified, the text still insists that “petty people withdraw.” In After Completion, after “three years, he conquers it,” it again insists, “Do not employ petty people.” All express this same intention.

## Translator’s Endnotes

The base text is the repository’s [YiXueWang transcription][E]. **W** denotes the [parallel *Siku Quanshu* transcription on Wikisource][W], consulted selectively, not treated as an infallible critical text. “Editorial Judgment” in the translation always belongs to the historical compilers; the numbered notes below belong to this translation. Attested corrections, retained variants, and interpretive choices are distinguished explicitly. No Chinese source file has been altered.

[^j01-transmission]: **Received authorship and arrangement.** This opening gives the tradition’s own account of Fuxi, King Wen, the Duke of Zhou, and Confucius. These attributions are retained as assertions of the source, not presented as independently established modern historical conclusions. Its twelve-part arrangement means the Upper and Lower Classic plus the ten commentarial parts, not twelve numbered juan of this compilation. The title *Zhezhong* 折中 suggests weighing and adjudicating inherited explanations; “Balanced Interpretations” does not imply that the editors consider every interpretation equally sound.

[^j01-four-terms]: **元亨利貞: one formula, different readings.** The oracle follows Zhu Xi’s two-clause reading: great success, with benefit in correctness and firmness. Cheng Yi unfolds four virtues: origination, successful development, benefit or fulfillment, and steadfast completion. The editors then argue that two propositions can contain four levels of meaning. No single English formula can reproduce all three analyses simultaneously; the commentaries retain the distinctions. “Success” translates free, unobstructed development, not merely profit or personal advantage. The interpretation of 貞 here follows these commentators’ received text; it is not a claim to settle every possible earlier meaning of the word.

[^j01-stroke]: **Stroke rather than numeral.** The base has 一者 where W prints the unbroken-line sign ⚊者. “The unbroken stroke” renders the graphical sense required by the explanation. An “odd” line is undivided; an “even” line is divided into two segments. The terminology connects the visible figure with number rather than calling yang numerically odd in every conceivable context.

[^j01-gua]: **A wordplay and two scales.** 卦, *gua*, is explained through 掛, *gua*, “hang up.” The English cannot preserve that sound-play without a note. The same Chinese word can designate a three-line trigram or a six-line hexagram; English distinguishes them where the context permits. The Three Powers are Heaven, Earth, and the human sphere.

[^j01-nature]: **Nature, expression, constitution, activity.** 性 and 情 are rendered here as “nature” and “the expression of that nature.” Translating 情 simply as “emotion” would narrow the discussion, especially when it concerns Heaven. 體 and 用 are “underlying constitution” and “activity” or “functioning”: distinguishable aspects, not necessarily two independently existing substances. “Vigor” for 健 emphasizes sustained effective activity rather than aggression or muscular force.

[^j01-line-structure]: **Reading the six places.** Lines are counted upward. Odd-numbered positions are yang positions; even-numbered positions are yin positions. A yang line in an odd place, or a yin line in an even place, is formally “correctly placed.” The second and fifth positions are the centers of the lower and upper trigrams. Neither positional correctness nor centrality alone guarantees good fortune: the circumstances and the line’s relationships matter. Nine and six identify changing yang and yin; seven and eight are their stable counterparts in the method described here. These conventional designations do not mean that a hexagram has nine or six lines of each kind.

[^j01-yarrow-count]: **The yarrow calculation.** Kong Yingda’s 九過揲 and 六過揲 are rendered as nine and six groups of four, referring to the counted stalks that yield the line’s numerical value: thirty-six and twenty-four respectively. They do not instruct the reader to perform nine or six complete line-generating procedures. The compilation’s later discussion of yarrow calculation belongs to [Juan Twenty](../source/juan-20.md); it is not translated in this installment. Here the point is why the old, changing forms supply the names “nine” and “six.”

[^j01-shun]: **Exemplary biography, not an exclusive identification.** Cheng Yi maps the lines onto stages of Shun’s traditional career: obscure labor, recognition, trials in office, and rulership. Zhu Xi’s reply insists that such applications develop the line images but do not exhaust their original oracular use. The translation preserves both positions. References to the sage’s life therefore do not require every consulter to identify with a prospective monarch, nor do they turn each line into a prediction of one historical event.

[^j01-great-person]: **The consulter and the figure encountered.** Zhu Xi allows the great person to be someone the consulter should meet, rather than the consulter’s own identity. This is the force of his “host and guest” distinction. Conversely, a consulter who already embodies the appearing dragon may be directed toward the person represented by the fifth line. “Centered and correct” in praise of Nine in the Second Place is not a denial that yang in an even position is formally misplaced: ethical rightness and positional correctness must not be mechanically equated.

[^j01-third-punctuation]: **Actual danger, not merely its appearance.** The clause 夕惕若厲 permits competing punctuation and grammatical analysis. The oracle follows Kong Yingda: 若 is a particle after 惕, and 厲 states the danger of the position. Thus “vigilant at evening. Danger, but no blame.” Reading 若 as “as if” would instead give vigilance as though danger were present. Kong explicitly rejects that reading here. “No blame” does not mean that the situation itself is safe.

[^j01-jing-errors]: **Two corrected characters in Qian, Nine in the Third Place.** In Kong Yingda’s explanation, the base has 終競此日; W has 終竟此日, “throughout the whole day.” In the *Classified Conversations* passage, the base has 競競戒惕; W has 兢兢戒惕, trembling care and vigilance. The English follows W in both places. These are character corrections, not attempts to reconcile the commentators’ differing interpretations of the line.

[^j01-two-centers]: **More than one kind of “middle.”** Yang Shi treats the third line as belonging to the middle, human tier of the Three Powers: places one–two are Earth, three–four human, five–six Heaven. This is not the same classification as the central places of the two trigrams, two and five. His account also concerns the appropriateness of a human position. Yang Xiong’s later reference to being beyond or short of the center instead relates the third to the second, and the fourth to the fifth. These senses should not be flattened into one numerical rule.

[^j01-leap]: **A possible leap, not assured flight.** 或 leaves advancement unsettled. 在淵 locates the dragon in the depths; “a leap from the depths” makes explicit the upward motion described by the commentaries while preserving its uncertainty. It does not say that the dragon is already flying. The source distinguishes evaluating the time for advancement from either rushing forward or refusing advancement in principle.

[^j01-overreach-reading]: **A retained variant.** Cheng’s explanation of Top Nine has 過時則亢 in the base, “once that time is passed, there is overreaching.” W instead has 過此則亢, “going beyond this, there is overreaching.” The English retains the base reading. Both make sense in context, so the difference has not been silently classified as a typo.

[^j01-no-head]: **Appearance and leadership.** In the oracle, 見 is read as “appear,” as in the dragon’s appearance in the second line. Cheng’s commentary instead develops 見 as “see” or “contemplate.” 無首 concerns having no one take the head or precedence; it does not describe mutilated dragons. The differing renderings in oracle and commentary deliberately preserve these distinct readings.

[^j01-ji-correction]: **言 corrected to 吉.** Lin Xiyuan’s explanation under “Using Nines” reads 剛而能柔則言也 in the base. W has 剛而能柔則吉也: firmness capable of yielding is fortunate. The English adopts 吉, “good fortune.” The parallel statement about compliance, vigor, and benefit reinforces this reading.

[^j01-changing-rules]: **Do not merge these divination rules.** Zhu Xi directs the reader who receives six changing nines in Qian, or six changing sixes in Kun, to the special “Using” statement without needing to consult the resulting hexagram. The imperial editors argue instead for considering the original and resulting Judgments together, including when every line changes. They retain the special statements to explain the character of the transition. These are genuine disagreements within the compilation, not different phrasings of a single agreed procedure. Their further examples, Return and Encounter, are likewise complementary hexagrams when all six lines change. The translation reports these traditional procedures; it does not independently establish their predictive efficacy.

[^j01-kun-punctuation]: **Where does “master” belong?** The oracle follows the editors in grouping 後得主, “following, one finds a master,” and allowing 利, “beneficial,” to govern the southwest and northeast clauses. Zhu Xi instead develops 主於利, taking benefit as the governing concern. Cheng also gives benefit a distinct role in the Earth’s and minister’s work. The following commentaries retain their own readings rather than being adjusted to the punctuation adopted in the translated oracle.

[^j01-kun-readings]: **Corrected readings in Kun.** The base’s 如有所住 in Zhu Xi’s opening explanation is translated with W’s 如有所徃, “if there is somewhere to go.” Cui Jing’s 安貞言也 is corrected from W’s 安貞吉也, “constancy at peace brings good fortune.” Under First Six, the commentator printed as 王氏應轔 is identified with W’s 王氏應麟, Wang Yinglin. The English changes only these readings at the relevant places; their Chinese forms remain untouched in the source file.

[^j01-kun-directions]: **Southwest and northeast as groupings.** The commentary invokes the Later Heaven trigram arrangement. Kun, Dui, Li, and Xun occupy the southwest, west, south, and southeast and form the yin grouping; Zhen, Gen, Kan, and Qian occupy east, northeast, north, and northwest and form the yang grouping. “Southwest” and “northeast” therefore gather larger directional sets in this explanation. The text also gives ethical and ministerial interpretations of finding and losing companions; it is not limited to a literal travel instruction.

[^j01-hierarchy]: **Historical hierarchy is not silently modernized.** The source explicitly places ruler over minister, father over son, and husband over wife. These are translated as the commentators’ social and ethical claims, not replaced by gender-neutral psychological metaphors or presented as present-day prescriptions. The reader should also retain the source’s distinction between compliance that contributes effectively and the inert weakness it criticizes. Neither deleting the hierarchy nor making all yieldingness passive would be faithful to the argument.

[^j01-companion-crossrefs]: **Companions are not invariably favorable or unfavorable.** The editors compare several passages to show that joining a group and leaving one can each be appropriate. Among the identifiable examples are “companions gather together,” Enthusiasm 16, Nine in the Fourth Place; “companions arrive,” Release 40, Nine in the Fourth Place; “companions disappear,” Peace 11, Nine in the Second Place; and “dispersing the group,” Dispersion 59, Six in the Fourth Place. In 朋盍簪, 盍 is taken in the sense of gathering, not translated as the interrogative “why not.” The final abbreviated allusion, 絕類上, is translated as given rather than assigned an uncertain line reference.

[^j01-initial-lines]: **The beginning of a force, not one changing line.** Wang Yinglin associates the first appearance of yang with Return, whose figure is one yang below five yin, and the first appearance of yin with Encounter, one yin below five yang. He is not saying that changing Qian’s first line produces Return, or Kun’s first line produces Encounter: those operations produce the opposite pair. The references to closing the passes and the strong woman belong respectively to Return’s Great Image and Encounter’s Judgment. The commentary is interpreting initial emergence and growth.

[^j01-moral-yinyang]: **The editors qualify their own moral symbolism.** Their argument does not simply identify an indispensable cosmic yin with intrinsic evil. They say that yin cooperating with yang need not be condemned, and that transformation may remove the need for suppression. Yet they do retain a hierarchy, both within the person and in social relationships. The “Three Guiding Bonds” are the ruler–minister, father–son, and husband–wife relations as they describe them. The references to watchfulness in solitude concern conduct where outward scrutiny is absent, not a command to withdraw from society. These qualifications matter to the passage’s reasoning; they do not erase its historical assumptions.

[^j01-practice]: **習 and effortless accomplishment.** Zhu Xi’s “without practice” concerns virtue already fully present, functioning without further rehearsal. The editors argue against taking the phrase as abolishing learning: they read 習 as repetition or an added, contrived operation, contrasting it with Kun’s natural simplicity. Their final question invokes “reverence to straighten the inner life, righteousness to square outward conduct.” The translation keeps the same compact oracle wording while allowing the commentaries to disagree about what it excludes.

[^j01-yang-position]: **An interpretive clarification, not an attested correction.** In the *Classified Conversations* passage under Kun’s second line, both the base and W say that the fifth “is a yang line” (陽爻). Since every line of Kun is yin, the English renders the apparent positional shorthand as “a yang position”: the fifth is an odd-numbered place. No witness consulted here supplies 陽位 in place of 陽爻. This clarification is therefore explicitly interpretive, not presented as a recovered textual reading.

[^j01-kun-completion]: **Achievement and completion.** 無成有終 permits distinct readings in the commentaries. The oracle follows the ministerial interpretation: do not claim the achievement as one’s own, but bring the entrusted work to completion. Zhu Xi instead describes the sequence as initial lack of achievement followed by eventual completion. Both are retained. “No achievement of one’s own” should not be mistaken for a recommendation to leave the work unfinished.

[^j01-kun-third-position]: **“Attained its position” need not mean parity-correct.** Cheng Yi calls the third line 得位 while discussing its place at the top of the lower trigram and the minister’s responsibilities. A yin line in the third, yang position is not formally correctly placed in the usual parity scheme. The English therefore says “has attained its position,” without silently changing the Chinese or forcing Cheng’s wording into Zhu Xi’s formal assessment. Compare the qualifications concerning the second line of Qian and Yang Shi’s human “middle.”

[^j01-yellow-garment]: **Yellow and the lower garment.** The imagery joins the central color, yellow, to 裳, a garment worn on the lower body. The commentators read centeredness together with remaining below. “A yellow lower garment” preserves both parts of that image. It is not translated as imperial robes: that would obscure the very distinction between inward centrality and subordinate outward station on which these explanations rely.

[^j01-nankuai]: **A favorable line does not license the consulter’s purpose.** The account of Nan Kuai is quoted as a divination precedent from the *Zuo* tradition. He receives Kun’s fifth line and takes its favorable words as sufficient; Zifu Huibo insists on loyalty, trustworthiness, reverence, and a good undertaking. The anecdote thus supplies Zhu Xi with a condition on applying the prognostication, not an unconditional promise that any person receiving the line will prosper. The translation retains that restriction without treating the reported outcome as independent evidence for predictive efficacy.

[^j01-cheng-rulership]: **Cheng’s restriction and the editors’ objection.** Cheng treats yin in the fifth position as a warning against a minister or woman occupying supreme power. His examples include the traditional figure Nüwa and Lady Wu; the latter is Wu Zetian. His judgment is translated as his own, not endorsed or softened into an unrelated maxim. Zhu Xi and the imperial editors explicitly resist the premise that every fifth line must depict a sovereign. The disagreement is part of the compilation’s meaning and should remain visible to the reader.

[^j01-dragon-battle]: **Dark and yellow; battle and legitimacy.** 玄 and 黃 associate the blood with Heaven and Earth in the imagery of the passage. The differing explanations turn on whether the dragon designates yang arriving to chastise yin, or the clash more generally. Hu Bingwen’s references to the *Spring and Autumn Annals* concern the way wording can preserve the formal status of a superior while describing a compromised situation. The tenth month, the branch Hai, and Qian’s northwestern position belong to the correlational explanation given here; they should not be conflated with a single universal calendar scheme.

[^j01-establish-lord]: **建侯 is political before it is metaphorical.** The literal image is establishing a territorial lord, not appointing a modern coach or finding an inner guide. Zhu Xi allows the consulter to occupy either side of the act: the person being established or the person establishing someone else. Cheng emphasizes obtaining assistance in overcoming widespread difficulty. His explanation is preserved alongside Zhu’s rather than replaced by it. Hu Bingwen’s “but not to rest easy” comes from the *Commentary on the Judgments* and is not an omitted clause of the hexagram’s core oracle.

[^j01-zhun-marriage]: **Three readings of the marriage scene.** Zhu Xi treats the first line as a suitor whose approach is not robbery but is nevertheless not the woman’s proper match. Cheng treats that first line as the obstructing “raider,” and the fifth as the marriage partner she seeks. The editors suggest that the riders themselves represent the proper partner, using parallels in Adornment and Opposition. These alternatives are not harmonized. 字 is also disputed: Zhu connects it with consent to betrothal and adult naming; Cheng reads the eventual outcome as bearing and nurturing children. The literal ten years is retained, while Zhu interprets it through a cycle reaching its limit.

[^j01-zhun-readings]: **Two corrected readings in Zhun.** In Zhao Rumei’s discussion of the hexagram’s governing line, the base has 卦象, “hexagram image”; W has 卦彖, “hexagram Judgment.” The English adopts the latter because the cited 建侯 statement belongs to the Judgment. In Cheng’s commentary on Six in the Second Place, 求子婚媾 is translated with W’s 求於婚媾, seeking the marriage partner. These changes do not settle the substantive disagreement about which line the rider represents.

[^j01-withdrawn-worthies]: **Waiting for the appropriate engagement.** Zhang Jun’s three examples are the Grand Duke, Yi Yin, and Kongming. Kongming is Zhuge Liang’s courtesy name. The passage invokes exemplary withdrawals before public service to illuminate the refusal of an improper alliance. It does not require the woman in the oracle to be identified with any one of these men, nor does it make the line a literal biography of a recluse. This is an analogical application of the image to conduct.

[^j01-deer]: **Deer, foothills, and an unverified witness.** The base reads 鹿, “deer,” and that is retained. Zhu reports Guo Jing’s proposal to read 麓, “foothills,” together with a changed wording in the *Image*, but immediately questions the evidence for the allegedly recovered text. The translation does not treat that purported autograph as verified. Wang Fusi is Wang Bi. 虞 is the forester or official guide, not “anxiety”; 幾 is the incipient sign or critical beginning that enables timely withdrawal.

[^j01-small-great]: **Small matters or gradual correction?** Zhu Xi takes 小貞 and 大貞 in terms of steadfast correctness in small and great undertakings. Cheng Yi reads them as gradual correction versus large-scale or abrupt rectification, and other commentators develop both approaches. The oracle follows Zhu’s reading; Cheng’s is not made to say the same thing. 膏, rich moisture, becomes beneficent influence in the commentaries. Its being held back need not imply a deliberate refusal to share.

[^j01-rulers-in-difficulty]: **Illustrative histories, not one composite event.** Cheng contrasts rulers attempting abrupt recovery of lost authority, rulers restoring order gradually, and rulers failing to act. The Duke of Gaogui Township is Cao Mao. Pan Geng, King Xuan of Zhou, and the Tang emperors Xizong and Zhaozong belong to different historical settings. Their appearances here serve Cheng’s argument about the pace of corrective action; the translation does not make his compressed judgments an exhaustive assessment of their reigns.

[^j01-line-relations]: **Supporting, riding, neighboring, corresponding.** 承 describes a lower line supporting the one above; 乘, an upper line riding on the one below; 比, proximity or neighboring; 應, correspondence across the two trigrams, normally between one and four, two and five, three and six. Complementary yin and yang frequently make a correspondence effective, but corresponding positions can also be discussed when their lines do not cooperate. The editors expressly call these symbolic configurations: they need not constitute six simultaneous people in a single story.

[^j01-situation-position]: **A “time” is a kind of situation.** The editors insist that the time denoted by a hexagram can belong to a scholar awaiting recognition, a minister whose work is unfinished, or a merchant facing poor trade. It is not restricted to one historical period. Likewise, the six positions disclose different ways of inhabiting such a situation, not necessarily six people acting simultaneously. Their objection is to an unnecessarily literal reconstruction that makes the first line the common cause of every other line’s trouble. This passage is an important statement of their interpretive method.

[^j01-meng-question]: **Who says “I”?** In the hexagram’s imagery, “I” is the clear-sighted second line, and the unknowing child the fifth. But Zhu Xi expressly allows the consulter to occupy either role. 童蒙 therefore does not always identify a literal child: it can describe a ruler or any learner whose understanding needs development. The passage links an answer to the seriousness of the approach. It should not be reduced to a mechanical permission to cast repeatedly until a desired answer appears; nor does it say that a learner may never ask a further genuine question.

[^j01-meng-readings]: **Two conjunctions and a sentence boundary.** Xiang Anshi’s 由其心相應 in the base is rendered with W’s 則其心相應, “the learner’s heart then responds.” Wang Zongchuan’s 由過此以往 is likewise rendered with W’s 則過此以往, “then, once this point has been passed.” In Lin Xiyuan’s opening explanation, the base’s punctuation separates 信 from 我. The English follows W’s continuous 信我, “trust me,” and reads the following 求而誠或未至 as the learner’s request whose sincerity is not yet complete. These repairs do not change the argument’s distinction between willingness to learn and sincerity in seeking instruction.

[^j01-xiaomin]: **The poem’s title corrected.** The base gives 小昱; W gives 小旻, *Xiao Min*, the title adopted here. The quoted image is a weary divinatory tortoise that will no longer disclose counsel. Yu Yan uses it to illuminate refusal after importunate questioning. Its appearance within a discussion of yarrow divination does not turn the tortoise into a yarrow-stalk oracle: the commentator is drawing an analogy between two forms of consultation.

[^j01-meng-punishment]: **Opposed interpretations of releasing the restraints.** Zhu Xi favors sharp correction followed by respite. Cheng Yi treats penal restraints as preparation for education and warns against relying on punishment alone. Wang Anshi and Wang Zongchuan instead make premature release of the restraints the cause of humiliation; the editors explain how their punctuation yields this contrast. The English preserves “punishment,” “fetters,” and “shackles,” rather than replacing their institutional and bodily force with modern therapeutic language. These are the historical commentators’ accounts, not present-day recommendations for treating learners.

[^j01-gold-man]: **Gold, wealth, or firmness.** 金夫 supports several explanations here. Zhu Xi imagines a man using gold to tempt the woman, invoking Qiuhu of Lu. Cheng identifies the attractive, wealthy figure with the second line. Wang Bi reads a firm male figure and identifies the relation with the top line. The editors favor Wang’s account because following the second, the proper teacher, should not itself count as misconduct. “A man of gold” in the oracle preserves the shared wording without prematurely deciding between these readings. “Loses possession of herself” retains the source’s bodily and ethical image.

[^j01-refusal-teaching]: **Teaching through refusal.** Zhao Rumei’s 不屑之教 invokes the Mencian idea that declining to instruct someone can itself be a form of instruction. Here it distinguishes rejection of the third line’s conduct from abandoning every possibility of its education. The same discussion insists that ignorance has different causes and therefore requires different responses. The translation preserves that argument without turning the historical phrase into a general instruction that a modern teacher should withhold help.

[^j01-meng-aptness]: **An attested correction and a retained difficulty.** Cai Qing’s concluding 此語最初 in the base is translated with W’s 此語最切, “this is especially apt.” Earlier in the same paragraph, however, both texts have 安己之心, a heart at peace with its own condition. That wording is retained, not conjecturally changed to 虛己, “emptying oneself,” even though the following contrast with self-reliance might invite such a conjecture. The translation therefore separates a witnessed correction from a merely attractive editorial possibility.

[^j01-open-center]: **Receptiveness and firmness are functions as well as line qualities.** Li Chun distinguishes what ruler and minister take as their underlying constitution from how they exercise it. The ruler’s 虛中, “open center,” is receptiveness rather than intellectual emptiness or lack of principle. The minister’s firmness at the center gives effective expression to subordinate compliance. This explains why a line can be formally misplaced by the ordinary yin–yang position rule yet still receive a favorable statement. Positional parity is not a sufficient substitute for interpreting the situation.

[^j01-meng-force]: **The correction must not become the offense.** Cheng Yi’s examples contrast what he regards as warranted punitive action with campaigns he condemns as excessive. The accounts of the Miao, the Three Overseers, and imperial warfare belong to his historical and moral argument. His own distinction is that correcting wrongdoing must not become unrestrained violence. The translation preserves those judgments as his, while keeping the oracle’s opposition between warding off a raider and becoming one; it does not recast the passage as advice to employ coercion in contemporary education.

[^j01-meng-pairs]: **Comparison is not technical correspondence.** Xiang Anshi compares first with top, second with fifth, and third with fourth to bring out complementary or contrasting meanings. These pairs are not the ordinary correspondence scheme of first–fourth, second–fifth, and third–sixth. His procedure concerns reading the structure of the discussion: beginning against end, receptive learner against accommodating teacher, and misguided proximity against isolating distance. Substituting the technical correspondence pairs would alter his explanation.

[^j01-waiting-need]: **Waiting and what is needed.** The source plays on 需 as waiting and on what living things require for nourishment. Food and drink therefore belong to the hexagram’s account of patient development, not an unrelated theme inserted into a text otherwise concerned only with danger. The editors later insist that the fifth line’s peaceful nourishment expresses a meaning broader than the avoidance of hazards. “Waiting” is retained as the title, while the introductory commentary makes the connection with need explicit.

[^j01-fu]: **Sincerity with substance.** 孚 is rendered “sincerity,” with “trustworthiness” in its explicit glosses. The commentators associate it with the solid middle line of Kan and with something real held within. It does not mean merely wishing strongly for an outcome or professing confidence without grounds. Their distinction between inward sincerity and the correctness of the undertaking is also retained: an earnest heart does not by itself make every project right or every result assured.

[^j01-raiders-error]: **王 corrected to 至.** The base prints the third line as 致寇王. W reads 致寇至, bringing about the arrival of raiders, which agrees with the ensuing commentaries. The translation adopts 至. This is a character correction, not a reinterpretation that introduces a “raider king.” The source’s progressive images of outskirts, sand, and mud remain intact.

[^j01-pit]: **The same image, different accounts of entering and leaving.** Zhu Xi takes 穴 as dangerous entrapment: the fourth can emerge, while the top has already fallen in. Cheng takes it as a creature’s resting place: the fourth is displaced from its dwelling, while the top finally reaches a secure stopping place. Zhu explicitly disputes Cheng’s explanation. “Pit” preserves a common concrete image in the oracle; “dwelling” and “place of security” appear where Cheng develops his own reading. These opposite interpretations are not silently reconciled.

[^j01-generation-reading]: **A retained source annotation, an adopted older reading, and a misattribution.** Zheng Weiyue’s passage in the base reads 必俟百年 and adds the annotation, “In the original, 俟 was written 世.” W has 必世百年. The English follows 世 and translates “after a generation or a hundred years,” recognizing the paired allusions to [*Analects* 13.11–12][A13]: a hundred years of good government and a generation before benevolence is realized. The base annotation is thus preserved here rather than lost. Both the base and W attribute “Xu is the Way of food and drink” to the *Appended Statements*; the passage is actually in the *Sequence of the Hexagrams*, as Cheng’s introduction correctly indicates. The attribution is retained in the body and corrected bibliographically in this note.

[^j01-forcing-growth]: **Neither fixing the result nor forcing growth.** 正助 compresses the discussion in [*Mencius*, “Gongsun Chou,” Part One][M2A], of sustained cultivation without fixing upon the result or trying to help growth by force. The associated story concerns a man who pulls up his seedlings to make them grow. The editors’ contrast is not between effort and complete inactivity, but between patient cultivation and artificial acceleration. Their account of transformation over generations belongs to the same argument about allowing work to mature.

[^j01-unbidden]: **Uninvited, not slow.** In 不速之客, 速 means summoning or inviting. The three guests arrive without being invited; they are not guests who arrive late. Hu Bingwen further notes that the three lower yang lines do not all stand in formal correspondence with the top, which supports the image of an unexpected arrival. The literal three is retained because the commentary connects it with Qian’s three lines; the passage need not be turned into a forecast of three identifiable visitors in every consultation.

[^j01-xu-final-reading]: **A retained wording in the general discussion.** Jiang Tisheng’s opening has 容忍 in the base and 容厚 in W. The English retains the base’s sense of bearing patiently with the situation rather than adopting W’s emphasis on accommodating generosity. Both fit the surrounding argument, so the variation is recorded without treating either intelligible reading as an obvious typographical error.

[^j01-contention]: **Dispute and litigation.** 訟 includes arguing a case before an authority, but the commentaries also extend it to rivalry, accusation, and a contentious disposition. “Contention” accommodates this range; “dispute,” “case,” and “adjudication” are used where the context becomes specifically juridical. 中 likewise supports both moderation or centeredness and stopping midway. The English does not force all the commentators into one reading. Their account of when to stop belongs to this interpretive tradition, not to a procedural rule for handling a present-day legal case.

[^j01-song-transformation]: **A structural derivation, not an instruction for casting.** In the derivation from Withdrawal 33 to Contention 6, the firm third line comes down to the second place, while the yielding second line takes the third. This produces the lower Kan beneath the unchanged upper Qian. Zhu uses the movement to explain firmness arriving at the lower center. It is a hexagram-transformation interpretation, not a claim that every consultation yielding Contention must first have produced Withdrawal or must be read as an actual two-line change from it.

[^j01-song-readings]: **Four corrections from the parallel text.** In Cheng’s explanation of the Judgment, the base’s 利見於六人 is translated with W’s 利見於大人, seeing a great person. In Zhu’s explanation of First Six, 或其象占 is read with W’s 故其象占, “hence this image and prognostication.” Yang Jian’s 卜有言 under the same line is corrected to W’s 小有言, “a little argument.” In Yu Yan’s explanation of Nine in the Second Place, 或不克訟 is read with W’s 故不克訟, “therefore cannot prevail.” The correction of conjunctions is distinguished from the more substantial disagreements about whether a dispute cannot succeed or ought not be pursued.

[^j01-peacemakers]: **The great person need not resolve every dispute in the same way.** Xiang Anshi supplies four models: declining to contend, reconciling the parties, changing their dispositions, and clarifying the matter. Zhong You is the disciple also known as Zilu. The point of the list is the variety of beneficial interventions, not the identification of a single official procedure. Accordingly, “seeing a great person” has not been narrowed in translation to hiring a lawyer, obtaining a judge, or consulting a spiritual adviser.

[^j01-three-hundred]: **A modest refuge or a protected community.** Wang Bi, Cheng Yi, and Zhu Xi emphasize the smallness of the settlement: withdrawal must not become the occupation of a new stronghold. Xun Shuang, Xiang Anshi, and Yu Yan emphasize the people spared harm when the dispute stops. The editors allow the latter interpretation alongside the former. The English oracle leaves the households visible rather than collapsing them into the abstract counsel “be humble.” The commentaries’ numerical image is not converted into a universal quantitative rule.

[^j01-former-virtue]: **Sustenance, proper station, and credit.** 食 concerns enjoying the benefits or revenues attached to an established position, interpreted through the virtue that warrants it. “Former virtue” is not merely a memory of past good behavior. The later 無成 repeats the issue raised by Kun’s third line: does it mean accomplishing nothing, or not claiming achievement for oneself? Zhu’s account here discourages going forth into service; the other commentators largely distinguish fulfilling service from competing for its credit. The editors explicitly resist making this passage’s meaning diverge too far from Kun’s closely parallel wording.

[^j01-mandate]: **命 is explained through right principle.** The oracle retains “mandate,” while both principal commentaries gloss it as 正理, right principle. Returning to it means changing an angry or contentious disposition and accepting the appropriate order of action. It is not simply resignation to whatever happens, nor an instruction that every command from another person is right. Cheng’s quotations concerning defiance of commands develop his explanation of the word; they do not remove his repeated appeal to centeredness and correctness.

[^j01-belt]: **The visible reward of an insecure victory.** 鞶帶 is a ceremonial belt associated here with the robes and insignia of an official commission. Being stripped of it three times before the morning ends gives an emphatic image of honors that cannot be retained. Zhu and Cheng both treat the apparent victory as unstable, not as an exception that cancels the warning against pursuing contention to the utmost. The literal morning and three removals are preserved, without converting them into a timetable for a modern consultation.

[^j01-seasoned-elder]: **丈人 is not “father-in-law” here.** Zhu Xi explains the term through maturity and experience; Cheng Yi through dignity, authority, and the confidence of the multitude. Cheng expressly denies that long-established high rank is necessary. “Seasoned elder” preserves the association with seniority while allowing the commentary to explain why someone raised from obscurity can fulfill the role. Neither advanced age alone nor hereditary position is presented as sufficient.

[^j01-commanders]: **Names and the compressed Rangju anecdote.** The Marquis of Huaiyin is [Han Xin][S92]. Cheng’s account of Sima Rangju says that he requested Zhuang Jia “as a commander” (為將), and that wording is retained in the body. The fuller account in [*Records of the Historian*, juan 64][S64], instead describes Rangju requesting a respected royal favorite to oversee the army (監軍), after which Zhuang Jia is appointed. Cheng compresses the story to explain how authority becomes credible. The translation records the difference rather than silently replacing his wording with that of the biographical source.

[^j01-army-discipline]: **A successful outcome can still be condemned.** Zhu Xi takes 否臧 as “not good,” here rendered “without sound order.” Cheng’s reading is stronger: without righteous purpose or proper discipline, even victory is a form of misfortune. He also distinguishes the legitimacy of raising the army from the commands and restraints that govern its conduct. “Discipline” therefore does not merely mean efficiency. The two principal explanations are preserved rather than made into one unqualified promise that a well-drilled army will win.

[^j01-army-center]: **Being in the army and attaining the center.** 在師中吉 can place the commander among the troops while also describing the balance with which he exercises authority. The commentaries explicitly connect the second line’s central position with neither excessive independence nor inadequate command. Cheng treats three royal commissions as fullness of favor, not as a specification of three named ranks. The English preserves the numerical wording while leaving its interpretation to the explanations that follow.

[^j01-delegation-duty]: **Independent command remains delegated responsibility.** 閫外, literally beyond the threshold, marks the sphere of military command in Cheng’s account. The commander’s discretion does not abolish the relation to the ruler who entrusted the task and supplied the army. Cheng then extends the distinction between fulfilling a duty and claiming extraordinary entitlement through the Duke of Zhou and Master Zeng. His analogy does not say that their achievements were small; it says that achievement within a role is not, by itself, a warrant to overstep that role.

[^j01-shi-readings]: **A correction without making W the authority in every case.** Hu Bingwen’s explanation of the second line has 以師而中 in the base; W has 在師而中, being centered in the army, which the English follows in context. Elsewhere W contains such apparent errors as 文人 for 丈人, 无吉 where the argument requires 无咎, and 敵專 where the base has 敢專. The base’s intelligible readings are retained there. Selective comparison is not a presumption that every difference in W improves the text.

[^j01-corpses-commanders]: **輿尸: a major interpretive fork.** Zhu Xi reads 輿 as carrying in a cart and 尸 as corpses: a defeated army returns with its dead. Cheng reads 輿 as a multitude and 尸 as presiding or mastering: divided command brings defeat. The oracle follows Zhu’s concrete image, while Cheng and the commentators who follow him explicitly say “many masters.” Their shared warning about military failure does not make the two lexical readings identical. The bodily severity of the first image is retained, not softened into a generic setback.

[^j01-left-camp]: **Leftward encampment and withdrawal.** 左次 is translated literally in the oracle. Both principal commentaries explain it as withdrawing to an encampment and preserving the army intact. Wu Cheng’s description of right as forward and left as rearward belongs to his account of a military diagram and its conventions. It is not turned into an instruction to retreat toward a particular compass direction. The interpretive emphasis is on an appropriate withdrawal, not on treating all retreat as either failure or virtue.

[^j01-field-game]: **An intrusion into cultivated ground.** 禽 here includes wild animals, not only birds, and 田 is treated by the commentators as cultivated fields whose crops are being damaged. Zhu takes 言 as a particle and 執 as capturing the intruder. Cheng takes 執言 as advancing the words of a declared charge. The English oracle follows Zhu, while Cheng’s explanation preserves his different reading. Likewise, “elder son” and “younger sons” remain visible images, not silently replaced by modern management titles. The commentators apply them to mature, unified command and its disruption, rather than requiring actual brothers to lead the troops.

[^j01-army-ethics]: **The source’s historical categories and restraints.** Man, Yi, and Xia preserve the names used in Cheng’s formula 蠻夷猾夏 rather than assigning its judgments to any present-day population. His account distinguishes attacks that harm the people and cannot be resolved through conciliation from campaigns that actively seek a pretext for conquest. The comparison with animals entering the crops carries that distinction. These are Cheng’s categories and examples, translated as part of his argument; they are not the translator’s classification of peoples or an endorsement of a contemporary military policy.

[^j01-elder-gloss]: **An additional gloss in W, absent from the base.** At Six in the Fifth Place, W includes a passage attributed to Hu Bingwen that the selected YiXueWang text does not contain: 胡氏炳文曰長子即彖所謂丈人也自衆尊之則曰丈人自君稱之則曰長子皆長老之稱. Translated in full: “The elder son is the seasoned elder spoken of in the Judgment. When the multitude honors him, he is called a seasoned elder; when the ruler names him, he is called the elder son. Both are designations for a senior, mature person.” It is supplied here as a parallel-edition addition, not silently inserted into the translation of the base or counted as one of its 443 blocks.

[^j01-states-estates]: **Two levels of territorial authority.** 開國 and 承家 concern the establishment of a lord’s state and the receipt of a great officer’s estate. 國 and 家 here are not simply a modern nation-state and a private nuclear household. The “altars of Soil and Grain” signify the public institutions entrusted to a territorial ruler. Zhao Rumei’s 軍, 師, and 旅 are rendered army, division, and brigade as relative levels of organization, not as claims of equivalence with modern unit sizes. His 周官 is the work also called the *Rites of Zhou*.

[^j01-postwar-use]: **A revision by Zhu Xi, and another disagreement after it.** Zhu’s *Classified Conversations* explicitly revises his earlier explanation: people who earned rewards cannot simply be excluded from them, but need not be retained for counsel and planning. He says this interpretation has not yet been incorporated into the *Original Meaning*. That statement belongs to Zhu’s own reported discussion, not to this translation’s revision history. The imperial editors offer a further reading: choose the right people from the outset, and appoint the worthy after disorder has ended. Cheng’s compressed judgment concerning Ying Bu and Peng Yue is retained as his explanation, not expanded into a comprehensive historical verdict.
