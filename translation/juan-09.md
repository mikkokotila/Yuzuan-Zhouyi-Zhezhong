**Original Meaning.** The Judgment is the statement attached by King Wen. “Upper” means the upper part of the Classic. “Commentary” means the words through which Confucius explains the Classic. All subsequent uses of “Commentary” follow this convention.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:004 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** The *Commentary on the Judgments* is Confucius's explanation of King Wen's intention. First it explains a hexagram's name, then its statement. In explaining the name, it draws variously upon the hexagram's images, virtues, and structure. Sometimes it uses these together; sometimes only one or two. In each case, however, greatest weight belongs to the meaning of the Commentary's opening phrase. In Zhun, for example, “firmness and yieldingness first interact, and difficulty arises”; in Meng, “beneath the mountain lies danger”: these give the primary meaning. The manner of explaining the statements is still more varied. Sometimes it discusses their principle directly from the hexagram's name; sometimes it draws variously upon images, virtues, and structure. For the statement arises from the name. Viewed from King Wen's original text, discussing its principle through the name is the proper approach. Yet since the name itself is rooted in the figure, the statement cannot be separated from the figure either. Drawing upon its different aspects serves two purposes: to unfold everything contained in the name and reveal the source of the statement's meaning; and to establish precedents for judging good fortune and misfortune in the two trigrams and six lines, showing that the statement's meaning embraces them all. Only the eight hexagrams Qian, Kun, Kan, Li, Zhen, Gen, Xun, and Dui have no explanation of their names. King Wen did not alter the names inherited from Fuxi; their virtues and images had long been transmitted and required no explanation. Kan alone adds the word “repeated,” drawing upon the doubling of its trigram, and this is therefore specially explained. The explanations of their statements also draw variously upon virtues, images, and line positions. Thus, in explaining Qian's “origination, success, benefit, steadfastness,” references to Heaven concern its image, and references to the fifth nine concern its line position. Earth in the explanation of Kun, water in Kan, and thunder in Zhen likewise concern their images. Firm centeredness in Kan, yielding centeredness in Li, Gen's “the upper and lower positions confront their counterparts and do not join,” Xun's firm penetration and yielding compliance, and Dui's firm center and yielding exterior all concern line positions. Understand Qian first, and the other hexagrams can be understood in turn.[^j09-method]

## 1. Qian 乾

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:005 -->

**Qian 乾**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:005 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:006 -->

### Great indeed is Qian's originating power! The myriad things draw upon it for their beginning; thus it comprehends Heaven.[^j09-four-virtues]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:006 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:007 -->

**Original Meaning.** This explains Qian's meaning exclusively through the Way of Heaven. It further distinguishes origination, success, benefit, and steadfastness as four virtues in order to elucidate it. This first passage begins by explaining origination. “Great indeed” is an exclamation. *Yuan* means great, and it means beginning. Qian's origination is the great beginning of Heaven's virtue, upon which the birth of all things depends. It is also first among the four virtues and runs through Heaven's virtue from beginning to end. Hence “it comprehends Heaven.”[^j09-qian-readings]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:007 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:008 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:008 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:009 -->

**The Changes of the Nine Schools:** Qian is pure yang, the image of Heaven. By observing Qian's beginning, one knows Heaven's virtue. Heaven alone is great; hence “great indeed.” Origination is the beginning of vital energy.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:009 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:010 -->

**Recorded Conversations of Master Zhu.** Qian's origination is simply Heaven's nature. They are not two different things.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:010 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:011 -->

**He also said:** Origination is the first emergence of activity, already containing the principles of success, benefit, and steadfastness. When these become success, benefit, and steadfastness, that too is only origination at work. This is why origination embraces all four virtues. Distinguished from one another, origination and success are sincerity's opening forth; benefit and steadfastness are sincerity's return. Their substance and function each have their place. In terms of function, origination governs; in terms of substance, steadfastness governs.[^j09-substance-sincerity]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:011 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:012 -->

**He also said:** Origination is the first discernible stirring of Heaven and Earth's bringing things to life. Origination is the impulse toward life. In success this impulse grows; in benefit it advances toward fulfillment; in steadfastness it reaches completion. Benevolence means just this. Benevolence is fundamentally the impulse toward life, and that impulse is the heart of compassion. When something wounds this life, the compassionate heart comes forth. Shame and aversion are likewise benevolence expressing itself through rightness; yielding and deference are benevolence expressing itself through ritual; discerning right and wrong is benevolence expressing itself through wisdom. How could someone without benevolence still possess rightness, ritual, or wisdom?[^j09-life-and-benevolence]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:012 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:013 -->

**Cai Qing:** Between Heaven and Earth, whatever is great serves as a beginning, and whatever begins is great of itself. At life's first emergence, spontaneous principle is like this; after life has emerged, the proper order is also like this. The Judgment's *yuan* is glossed simply as “great” because its original wording contains no meaning of “beginning.” Here, however, the phrase “the myriad things draw upon it for their beginning” also brings that meaning into play. Indeed, the greatness of Qian's origination is itself visible in the myriad things drawing upon it to begin.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:013 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:014 -->

### Clouds move and rain is bestowed; the many kinds of beings flow into form.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:014 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:015 -->

**Original Meaning.** This explains Qian's success.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:015 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:016 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:016 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:017 -->

**Yang Wanli:** The Commentary names origination, benefit, and steadfastness, but does not expressly name success. This is because the moving clouds and bestowed rain are the free passage of vital energy, while the many kinds of beings flowing into form are the free unfolding of form.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:017 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:018 -->

**Yu Yan:** Earlier it said “the myriad things”; here it says “the many kinds of beings.” Are “myriad” and “kinds” the same or different? Origination is the beginning of receiving vital energy, before distinctions can be made, so the collective term “myriad” is used. With success, movement flows forth and forms appear. Large and small, high and low, all have their distinctions. Hence the particular term “kinds.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:018 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:019 -->

### Greatly illuminating ending and beginning, the six positions each come to fulfillment in their time. Riding the six dragons in their proper times, one drives through Heaven.[^j09-six-dragons]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:019 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:020 -->

**Original Meaning.** Beginning is origination; ending means steadfastness. Without an ending there is no beginning; without steadfastness there is no means of origination. This says that when the sage greatly illuminates the ending and beginning of Qian's Way, he sees that each of the hexagram's six positions comes to fulfillment in its time. Riding these six yang, he practices the Way of Heaven. This is the sage's origination and success.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:020 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:021 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:021 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:022 -->

**Recorded Conversations of Master Zhu.** The ending and beginning of Qian's Way are the four virtues. The beginning is origination; the ending is steadfastness. Without an ending there is no means of beginning; without steadfastness there is no means of origination. The six lines are established on this basis alone. “Fulfilled in their time” means that each is fulfilled in its own time: hidden, appearing, flying, and leaping all follow their times. Yet all are the flowing activity of the four virtues.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:022 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:023 -->

**He also said:** The six dragons are simply the six lines; “dragon” is only a metaphor. Understand the meanings of these six lines—hidden, appearing, flying, leaping—and act according to the time: that is riding the six dragons; that is driving through Heaven. The sage is Heaven, and Heaven is the sage.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:023 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:024 -->

**Cai Qing:** “Riding” arises from the word “dragons.” “Driving” has the same sense as driving a carriage.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:024 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:025 -->

### Qian's Way changes and transforms. Each thing attains its proper nature and allotment, preserving and uniting the Great Harmony. Thus there are benefit and steadfastness.[^j09-nature-allotment]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:025 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:026 -->

**Original Meaning.** Change is transformation's gradual advance; transformation is change brought to completion. What things receive is their nature; what Heaven bestows is their allotment. The Great Harmony is the vital energy in which yin and yang meet and unite in balanced harmony. “Each attains its proper nature” concerns what is received at life's beginning; “preserving and uniting” concerns its wholeness after life has begun. This says that Qian's Way changes and transforms, benefiting everything without exception. All things receive their respective natures and allotments and thereby maintain their wholeness. In this way it explains benefit and steadfastness.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:026 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:027 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:027 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:028 -->

**Recorded Conversations of Master Zhu.** “Each attains its proper nature and allotment”: although these were already determined during origination and success, bodily form had not yet been completed. Only here is it completed. For example, only when the grains have hardened into full kernels do we speak of their nature and allotment being properly established.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:028 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:029 -->

**He also said:** “Qian's Way changes and transforms; each thing attains its proper nature and allotment”—throughout, there is only one principle. This principle is everywhere complete and undivided. One grain of millet grows into a shoot; the shoot flowers; the flower sets seed, becoming millet once more and returning to the original form. One ear bears a hundred grains, each grain whole and complete. Sow those hundred grains, and each again produces a hundred. Generation goes on without end. At first it was only this one grain branching out. Each and every thing has its principle, yet throughout there is only one principle.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:029 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:030 -->

**He also said:** Preserving and uniting the Great Harmony is preserving and uniting this principle of life. Heaven and Earth's intimate intermingling is their preservation and union of the principle by which things live. Creative transformation never ceases. Once the myriad things have been transformed and born, each preserves and unites its own principle of life. Without that preservation and union, there would be no things.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:030 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:031 -->

**He also said:** Preserving and uniting the Great Harmony applies alike to Heaven, Earth, and the myriad things. Heaven and Earth are the myriad things on a large scale; the myriad things are Heaven and Earth on a small scale.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:031 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:032 -->

**He also said:** Benevolence stands first among the four virtues, while wisdom can accomplish both beginning and ending, just as origination stands foremost among the four virtues of Qian. Yet origination is not born from origination: it is born from steadfastness. In Heaven and Earth's transformations, without contraction and gathering there can be no opening and dispersal. The meeting of benevolence and wisdom is the pivot of the myriad transformations. This principle revolves without exhaustion, fitting together without a gap. Without steadfastness there is no means of origination.[^j09-returning-cycle]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:032 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:033 -->

**Xiang Anshi:** Traced to its root and spoken of as a whole, it is called Qian's origination. Followed through the utmost of its changes and transformations, it is called Qian's Way. It begins in Qian's origination and ends in the Great Harmony. The myriad things emerge from origination and return into origination. This is why origination is great.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:033 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:034 -->

**Hu Bingwen:** In terms of the distinction between the two vital energies, change is the myriad things issuing from the generative pivot: origination and success. Transformation is their entering back into that pivot: benefit and steadfastness. In terms of the circulation of a single vital energy, change is the gradual process and transformation its completion. First the text speaks of the many kinds flowing into form, then of each attaining its proper nature and allotment. A thing possessing this form possesses this nature; all are bestowed by Heaven. “Each attains its proper nature” concerns receiving one's allotment: this is how nature is determined and does not alter. “Preserving and uniting” concerns maintaining one's nature: this is also how the bestowed life continues to circulate without cease. For the Great Harmony is the balanced vital energy in which yin and yang meet and unite, and within it is already present the principle of drawing upon Qian's origination for a beginning.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:034 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:035 -->

**Xue Xuan:** “Great indeed is Qian's originating power! The myriad things draw upon it for their beginning” is sincerity's source, the substance of the Way, the reason the myriad differences have one root. “Qian's Way changes and transforms; each thing attains its proper nature and allotment” is sincerity thereby established, the function of the Way, the reason one root has myriad differences. Yet whenever substance and function are spoken of, they must not be divided into two separate things.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:035 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:036 -->

**Cai Qing:** Although attaining proper nature and preserving harmony together constitute Qian's benefit and steadfastness, distinguished more finely, each attaining its proper nature is benefit; preserving and uniting is steadfastness. The *Original Meaning* on the *Words of the Text* says: “Benefit is bringing living things toward fulfillment: each obtains what suits it, without mutual injury.” Is that not precisely each thing attaining its proper nature and allotment? “Steadfastness is bringing living things to completion: real principle is fully present, sufficient wherever it is.” Is that not precisely preserving and uniting the Great Harmony?

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:036 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:037 -->

**Lin Xiyuan:** Each attaining its proper nature and allotment is benefit; preserving and uniting the Great Harmony is steadfastness. What earlier drew its beginning from origination and flowed into form through success now manifests its respective pattern and image and completes its bodily form. Its nature and allotment are thus properly established. Then it gathers ever more closely and becomes ever more firm. The impulse toward life is concentrated and stored without leakage; the generative pivot is held within and no longer visible outside. Thus the Great Harmony is preserved and united.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:037 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:038 -->

### Standing foremost above the multitude of beings, he brings peace to all the states.[^j09-sage-and-heaven]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:038 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:039 -->

**Original Meaning.** The sage above, standing high above other beings, is like the changing and transforming of Qian's Way. The many states each finding their proper place and all resting in peace are like the myriad things each attaining their proper nature and allotment and preserving the Great Harmony. This speaks of the sage's benefit and steadfastness. Considered as a whole, origination is things first coming to life; success is their free and flourishing growth; benefit is their movement toward fruit; steadfastness is the fruit's completion. Once the fruit is complete, its connection with root and stalk falls away, and it can be planted and live again. This is why the four virtues revolve without a first point. Yet throughout the four, life-giving energy flows without the slightest interruption. This is why origination embraces all four virtues and comprehends Heaven. In applying this to the sage, Confucius's intention was, it seems, to take the hexagram as a prognostication of the sage obtaining Heaven's position, practicing Heaven's Way, and bringing about great peace. Although some meanings in his wording differ from King Wen's original, readers who seek each author's intention can let both interpretations stand without contradiction. The same applies to Kun.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:039 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:040 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The statement beneath a hexagram is its Judgment. The Master then explains it, and his explanation too is called the Judgment. The Judgment states the meaning of a whole hexagram; thus, when someone of understanding examines its words, more than half the work of reflection is accomplished. “Great indeed is Qian's originating power” praises the greatness of the Way through which Qian's origination begins the myriad things. Origination among the four virtues is like benevolence among the five constant virtues. Spoken of as one particular aspect, it is one matter; spoken of comprehensively, it embraces all four. “The myriad things draw upon it for their beginning; thus it comprehends Heaven” speaks of origination. Qian's origination names Heaven's Way as a whole. Heaven's Way begins the myriad things; they draw their beginning from Heaven. “Clouds move and rain is bestowed; the many kinds of beings flow into form” speaks of success. Heaven's Way circulates, bringing forth and nourishing all things. Greatly illuminate the ending and beginning of Heaven's Way, and one sees each of the hexagram's six positions fulfilled in its time. The hexagram's beginning and ending are Heaven's Way's ending and beginning. Riding the times of these six lines is Heaven's movement. “Driving through Heaven” means answering to that movement. Qian's Way changes and transforms, bringing forth and nourishing all things: large and small, high and low, each according to its kind, each attaining its proper nature and allotment. What Heaven bestows is the allotment; what things receive is their nature. In “preserving and uniting the Great Harmony; thus there are benefit and steadfastness,” preserving means constant continuance, and uniting means constant harmony. Preserving and uniting the Great Harmony is what gives benefit and steadfastness. The enduring, unceasing Way of Heaven and Earth is this preservation and union. Heaven is the ancestor of all things; the king is the ancestral head of the many states. Qian's Way stands foremost above beings, and all their kinds find passage. The ruler's Way presides honorably in Heaven's position, and the four seas follow. When a king embodies Heaven's Way, all the states rest in peace.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:040 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:041 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:041 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:042 -->

**Recorded Conversations of Master Zhu.** In “Great indeed is Qian's originating power! The myriad things draw upon it for their beginning” and “Uttermost indeed is Kun's originating power! The myriad things draw upon it for their birth,” origination is precisely the benevolence that brings things to life. Drawing upon something for a beginning is receiving its vital energy; drawing upon it for birth is completing one's form. At success there is manifestation; at benefit, gathering and consolidation; at steadfastness, inward concentration. Once concentration leaves no outward trace, life must arise again. At the zi period around midnight, the thing is present but not yet moving. At yin and mao it comes to life; at si and wu it becomes manifest; at shen and you it gathers; at hai, zi, and chou it becomes substantial. Reaching yin again, it comes to life once more. This simply keeps revolving. A year has its year's circuit, a month its month's circuit, a day its day's circuit, and a period its period's circuit. Even within the slightest breath there are these four stages, turning in this way. He also said: Origination, success, benefit, and steadfastness have no break between them. After steadfastness comes origination again. Immediately before today's zi period stands yesterday's hai period. Some things are born in summer, autumn, or winter because only then do they respond to life-giving energy. Each has its own small cycle of origination, success, benefit, and steadfastness.[^j09-calendar-cycle]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:042 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:043 -->

**Lin Xiyuan:** Qian as drawn by Fuxi embraces a wide range of meanings, not merely Heaven's Way. King Wen's attached words, “great success; benefit in steadfastness,” were simply a divinatory statement, originally without any further intention. When the Master elucidated the *Changes*, he explained Qian specifically through Heaven's Way. He also divided the words into four virtues to bring out Qian's meaning. His explanation of Qian through Heaven's Way is not displayed elsewhere: it is displayed precisely in this distinction of origination, success, benefit, and steadfastness as four virtues.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:043 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:044 -->

**He also said:** The original intention of these words in the hexagram statement is the same as in other hexagrams. Only when our Master distinguished them as four virtues did later accounts of Heaven's Way follow this interpretation. This is why the Master became the ancestral teacher of the Way and its virtue. Likewise, the word “benevolence” first appears in the *Documents* simply in the sense of loving others. Only the Master began speaking of it as the heart's virtue. By establishing his teaching upon this, he first brought the Way of benevolence into practice in the world.[^j09-historical-claims]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:044 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:045 -->

**He also said:** Benefit is living things advancing toward fulfillment; steadfastness is living things attaining completion. How do fulfillment and completion differ? On the *Analects*' “do not remonstrate over affairs already underway,” the annotation explains that “underway” means an affair not yet complete whose momentum can no longer be stopped. Thus fulfillment is the tendency now moving toward completion, whereas steadfastness is completion itself. Hence the saying: benefit moves toward fruit; steadfastness is the fruit's completion.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:045 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:046 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Qian means vigor. The Judgment says only that the Way of utmost vigor brings great free passage and benefits from correct firmness: a prognostication concerning human affairs. When the Master composed the *Commentary on the Judgments*, he unfolded its meaning through the hexagram's image and positions. In terms of image, no image of Qian is greater than Heaven; in terms of position, none is more honored than the fifth. In Heaven's origination and success, nothing equals origination in bringing the virtues of all four seasons together in one season; when beneficence flows to all things, that is success. In the ruler's origination and success, the fifth nine brings the virtues of all six positions together in one position: this too is Heaven's origination. Beneficence flowing to all the people is likewise Heaven's success. Speaking of six positions and then of six dragons brings the meaning to bear upon “the flying dragon in Heaven.” The ending and beginning of the four virtues are embodied in the six lines. Yet the fifth nine alone possesses the virtues of all the lines and occupies the position in Heaven. It is as though, riding six dragons along Heaven's road, it could move the clouds and bestow rain, just as Heaven moves clouds and bestows rain. In Heaven's benefit and steadfastness, all things come to fulfillment, their natures and allotments properly established and the Great Harmony pervading them: this is the condition of benefit and steadfastness. In the ruler's benefit and steadfastness, the fifth nine governs the hexagram, while the other five yang above and below share its virtue. Like a great ruler above, enabling all people to realize the principle of their nature and allotment and rest nourished within the Great Harmony's transforming influence, this too is Heaven's benefit and steadfastness. Speaking of the multitude of beings and the many states further brings the meaning to bear upon “it is beneficial to see the great person.” In terms of what virtue and position command, it says “the multitude of beings”; in terms of what achievement and transforming influence reach, it says “the many states.” Standing foremost makes one visible to all beings; bringing them all to peace attains the highest government. Qian's meaning embraces everything. The Master takes its greatest aspects and therefore unfolds it fully through Heaven's Way and the ruler's Way.

## 2. Kun 坤

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:047 -->

### Uttermost indeed is Kun's originating power! The myriad things draw upon it for their birth; thus it compliantly receives Heaven.[^j09-kun-origination]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:047 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:048 -->

**Original Meaning.** This explains Kun's meaning through Earth's Way, beginning with origination. “Uttermost” means reaching the limit, its force somewhat gentler than “great.” Beginning is the beginning of vital energy; birth is the beginning of form. Compliantly receiving Heaven's bestowal is Earth's Way.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:048 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:049 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:049 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:050 -->

**Lü Dalin:** Qian's substance is great. Kun models itself upon Qian's pattern, stopping only when it has reached Qian's greatness. Thus Qian's origination is called “great indeed,” and Kun's “uttermost indeed.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:050 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:051 -->

**Recorded Conversations of Master Zhu.** Drawing upon Qian for a beginning is immediately drawing upon Kun for birth; not the briefest instant separates them. The myriad things draw upon Qian to begin and possess vital energy; they draw upon Kun to be born and possess form. The arrival of vital energy and the birth it brings are precisely Kun's origination.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:051 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:052 -->

**Cai Qing:** Had the text said only, “Uttermost indeed is Kun's originating power! The myriad things draw upon it for their birth,” it might seem to rival “Great indeed is Qian's originating power! <!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:106 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:107 -->

**Original Meaning.** This explains the hexagram statement through its structure. The second nine opens others' ignorance through a Way that can bring understanding, while also attaining the mean appropriate to its time. The matters specified below all concern proceeding through that Way and meeting what is fitting. “Their purposes respond”: the second is firm and clear, the fifth yielding and obscure. Thus the second does not seek the fifth, but the fifth seeks the second: their purposes respond of themselves. “Through firm centeredness”: being firm yet centered, it can instruct while keeping due limits. As for profanation, when divination is repeated two or three times, the questioner certainly profanes it, but the one who continues answering profanes it too. Nurturing correctness in the unformed is the work of becoming a sage. This explains the benefit of steadfastness.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4637:107 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4637:108 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Beneath the mountain lies danger. Danger within is no place to remain; a halt outside leaves no way forward. Not knowing what to do gives the meaning of obscurity and ignorance. Meng can attain success by proceeding through the Way of opening understanding. This Way is the mean appropriate to the time. Time means obtaining a response from the ruler; the mean means occupying the center. Attaining the mean is attaining the right time. “It is not I who seek the inexperienced youth; the inexperienced youth seeks me: their purposes respond.” The second, a firm and clear worthy, is below; the fifth, an inexperienced youth, is above. It is not the second seeking the fifth: the fifth's purpose responds to the second. How could a worthy below push himself forward to solicit the ruler? If he did, there would be no prospect of the ruler trusting and employing him. The ancients waited for rulers to show full respect and complete the proper courtesies before going to them. This was not a wish to exalt themselves. Unless the ruler honored virtue and delighted in the Way to this degree, they could accomplish nothing together. The first divination means coming with sincere, undivided purpose to resolve one's ignorance. One should then answer and enlighten through firm centeredness. A second and third attempt mean troublesome repetition. If the intention in consulting repeatedly cannot remain sincere and single, it becomes irreverent, and one should not answer. An answer would not be faithfully received, but would merely multiply the profanation. Hence it is called profaning Meng: questioner and respondent alike become importunate and irreverent. The Judgment speaks of benefit in steadfastness. The Commentary develops this meaning to show that it is not merely a warning to the second line, but the very Way of nurturing the unformed. What has not yet unfolded is called Meng. To nurture correctness in this pure, undivided, still-unfolded condition is the work of becoming a sage. To prohibit faults only after they have emerged meets resistance difficult to overcome. Nurturing correctness while still unformed is the highest goodness of learning. Among Meng's six lines, the two yang govern instruction; the four yin are all in the condition of being instructed.

## Translator's Endnotes

The base is [Chinese Juan Nine](../source/juan-09.md). “Parallel text” below means the *Siku Quanshu* transcription at [Wikisource, fixed revision 759592](https://zh.wikisource.org/w/index.php?title=御纂周易折中_(四庫全書本)/卷09&oldid=759592), consulted on September 26, 2026. This is selective comparison between transcriptions, not systematic collation against historical leaves. Agreement between the two does not establish independent textual authority. The Chinese source remains unchanged. Corrections adopted in the English, historical conjectures retained as the commentators' proposals, and unresolved readings are distinguished below. Supplementary passages absent from the base are confined to these endnotes. “Editorial Judgment” always denotes the imperial compilers, never the translator. Source-block references such as `4637:130` identify the corresponding hidden alignment marker.

[^j09-layer]: **A new textual layer.** This juan begins the separate treatment of the *Tuan zhuan*, the Commentary on the Judgments, whose upper part concerns hexagrams 1–30. Its canonical passages appear as headings; the explanations beneath them belong to later commentators. They are not new hexagram or line oracles. The introductory attribution of the Judgments to King Wen and the Commentary to Confucius is the compilation's traditional attribution, retained without making a new historical determination about authorship. The original page's navigation is translated and retained; added section headings organize the same thirty divisions without becoming source text.

[^j09-method]: **Four kinds of explanation.** 卦象 concerns the figure's images, such as Heaven, thunder, water, or a physical arrangement; 卦德 its characteristic powers or virtues, such as vigor and compliance; 卦體 its structure, including line positions and relations; 卦變 accounts of transformation. These categories overlap but are not interchangeable. Lines are numbered upward from the bottom; nine and six designate yang and yin, not ninth and sixth positions. Formal correctness normally places yang in odd positions and yin in even ones, while centeredness concerns the second and fifth. The compilation repeatedly distinguishes these formal criteria from the ethical or situational correctness of an action.

[^j09-four-virtues]: **One formula, two interpretations.** The translation continues Juan One's distinction between the divinatory formula “great success; benefit in steadfastness” and its interpretation as four virtues: origination, success, benefit, and steadfastness. Zhu explicitly says that the Commentary distinguishes four virtues when explaining Qian through Heaven's Way, whereas Zhun and later hexagrams often follow the wording's divinatory intention. “Origination” therefore does not silently replace every 元, nor does “steadfastness” imply that persistence is always right. The distinction is part of this compilation's argument, not a translator's reconstruction imposed upon it.

[^j09-qian-readings]: **Two transcription corrections.** At `4637:007`, the base reads 乾元大德之大始. The parallel has 乾元天徳之大始: Qian's origination is the great beginning of **Heaven's** virtue. The English follows 天 rather than 大. At `4637:018`, 放特謂之品 is read with the parallel as 故特謂之品, “hence the particular term ‘kinds.’” These corrections do not change the archived Chinese. The explanation of origination as both great and beginning remains the commentator's own.

[^j09-substance-sincerity]: **Sincerity, substance, and function.** 誠 here encompasses truthfulness and reality without falsity, not merely feeling something intensely. 體 and 用 name substance or constitution and its activity or function; they do not invariably divide physical matter from an immaterial power. The comments explicitly resist treating them as two independent things. 元亨誠之通 and 利貞誠之復 describe opening forth and return within the same reality. They are not additional instructions for casting or interpreting changing lines.

[^j09-life-and-benevolence]: **Benevolence as life-giving.** Zhu connects the originating impulse with 仁, benevolence, and the compassionate heart, then places shame and aversion, yielding and deference, and discrimination of right and wrong within its expressions as 義, 禮, and 智: rightness, ritual, and wisdom. The argument is that benevolence can be named as one virtue or understood as animating all four. Cheng later invokes the five constant virtues, a different enumeration. The English does not flatten these into a single list of four psychological traits.

[^j09-six-dragons]: **Times rather than six separate vehicles.** The six dragons correspond to the six yang lines and their different conditions. Zhu expressly calls the dragon an image, while the compilers relate the comprehensive power of the six positions to the fifth nine's honored position. “Driving through Heaven” keeps 御's image of driving or governing a course. It does not mean manipulating the sky or overriding Heaven. The subject, made explicit as the sage in the explanations, need not be supplied identically in every preceding canonical sentence.

[^j09-nature-allotment]: **性, 命, and Great Harmony.** The commentarial distinction is exact: what a thing receives is its nature; what Heaven bestows is its allotment. 命 here therefore concerns bestowed being and its governing order, not simply an individual's predicted fate. 大和 or 太和 is rendered Great Harmony, the balanced conjunction of yin and yang. Zhu's initial explanation and the later grain analogy locate the establishment of nature at somewhat different stages of manifestation; the translation retains their formulations instead of silently resolving the apparent difference.

[^j09-returning-cycle]: **An ending that enables a beginning.** The repeated assertion that origination comes from steadfastness explains the generative cycle: concentration and completion enable renewed emergence. It does not rank stagnation above movement. The paired discussion of benevolence and wisdom likewise concerns their roles in beginning and completion, not a claim that one ethical virtue can dispense with the others. “Generative pivot” translates 機 where the text concerns the initiation and turning of transformation.

[^j09-sage-and-heaven]: **A change of reference within Qian.** Zhu reads the clauses on clouds, rain, natures, and allotments cosmologically, and the clauses on the six dragons and the many states through the sage. The compilers emphasize the fifth line as the ruler embodying the whole figure. Cheng presents a related correspondence between Heaven's generative rule and the king's political rule. The English preserves these transitions rather than treating the whole passage as either natural description or personal spiritual instruction alone.

[^j09-calendar-cycle]: **The branch sequence.** Zi, yin, mao, si, wu, shen, you, hai, and chou are names in the twelve-branch cycle. Zhu uses corresponding cycles of year, month, day, and smaller intervals to illustrate generation, manifestation, gathering, and consolidation. These examples are not an instruction to assign a fixed clock hour or calendar date to every divination. The discussion's primary claim is that the fourfold process recurs at different scales. Its calendar imagery remains distinct from the numerical interpretations of seven days and eight months later in the juan.

[^j09-historical-claims]: **Historical claims remain attributed.** Lin Xiyuan's remarks about the early use of 仁 in the *Documents* and Confucius's role in establishing the heart's virtue are translated as his account of the tradition. The translation does not independently certify those claims as a complete history of the term. The same applies to assertions elsewhere that a particular formulation first appears with one sage or that every earlier scholar held a given view. Such claims remain within their named speaker's voice.

[^j09-kun-origination]: **“Great” and “uttermost.”** 大哉 and 至哉 are deliberately distinguished. Both praise generative power, but the commentators treat Kun's 至 as responsive completion rather than independent precedence. They likewise distinguish 始, the beginning of vital energy, from 生, the birth of formed beings. Zhu insists that these are not two events separated by an interval. The translation keeps the conceptual distinction without manufacturing a temporal gap.

[^j09-mare-and-vigor]: **Compliance is not immobility.** The mare is both female and a vigorous earth-traveling animal. Cheng and Zhu explicitly argue that Kun could not match Qian if it remained inactive while Qian proceeded. The image therefore combines compliance with effective movement. Its gendered and hierarchical associations belong to the source and have not been replaced by contemporary personality types. “Compliant vigor” should not be mistaken for either inert submission or an independent initiative that the commentators assign to Qian.

[^j09-kun-syntax]: **Rhyme and the noble person's course.** 君子攸行 can be linked backward to yielding compliance, benefit, and steadfastness, or forward to losing the way when leading and attaining it when following. Zhu's recorded discussion acknowledges the forward sense while accounting for its placement by rhyme; Xiong Liangfu refuses to let rhyme determine the grouping. Both constructions remain visible. The canonical heading follows the selected base's division rather than silently rearranging its sentences.

[^j09-kun-companions]: **Two explanations of the final blessing.** Zhu explains northeastward loss followed by a return southwestward. Cheng and several collected commentators instead explain leaving one's yin companions to follow yang. These are not simply two phrasings of the same itinerary. The English retains southwest and northeast as directions and lets each commentary develop its own account. He Kai further distinguishes gathering companions to follow from withdrawing companions to make way; that variation is also preserved.

[^j09-kun-half]: **A traditional asymmetry.** Zhu's statement that Kun falls short of Qian “by half” expresses his asymmetrical account of encompassing and being encompassed. It is not offered here as a numerical measurement of human abilities or as the translator's assessment of women. The surrounding discussion nevertheless credits Kun with indispensable generative activity. Both the asymmetry and that indispensable contribution remain in the text.

[^j09-kun-boundless]: **Three kinds of boundlessness.** Cheng distinguishes Qian's unceasing movement, Earth's inexhaustibility, and the horse's vigorous travel. Kong additionally distinguishes spatial breadth from temporal duration. “Boundless” is retained throughout, but the explanations prevent its occurrences from collapsing into one abstract infinity. In Cheng's concluding account, 本 and 用 concern the underlying constitution and its activity; they should not be read as two unrelated forces. At `4637:084`, the base’s 放云資生 is read with the parallel as 故云資生, “hence ‘draw upon it for their birth.’”

[^j09-zhun-trigrams]: **First interaction and arising difficulty.** Zhu assigns “first interact” to Zhen and “difficulty arises” to Kan. Cheng initially treats the interaction of the two images more comprehensively. The recorded conversation expressly separates explanation through trigrams, their virtues, and their images. The English keeps these explanatory levels distinct rather than turning them into a single literal account of hexagram production.

[^j09-zhun-virtues]: **The oracle formula after Qian and Kun.** Zhu marks this as the point at which the Commentary's explanation of 元亨利貞 follows King Wen's divinatory intention: great success, with benefit in correctness. This limits the Four Virtues reading developed at the beginning of the juan. It does not imply that every later occurrence of these characters must have one mechanically fixed English equivalent.

[^j09-zhun-storm]: **Cloud, rain, and the unsettled world.** The commentators distinguish cloud and thunder in Zhun from thunder and rain in Release. The transition from gathering clouds to rain becomes an account of obstruction turning into passage. 建侯 can mean establishing a ruler or establishing regional lords and assistants; those differences are preserved in the individual explanations. “Heaven's work” renders 天造 while allowing the commentarial gloss of Heaven's or the time's movement.

[^j09-zhun-readings]: **Corrections and punctuation.** At `4637:098`, the base's 冥味 is read with the parallel as 冥昧, obscurity. At `4637:099`, 難生 is read continuously as “difficulty arises,” not according to the base's misleading comma after 難, which might suggest that difficulty means birth. At `4637:103`, the base's 醞釀姻媼 is corrected to the parallel's 醞釀絪縕, brewing and intimate intermingling. The last correction restores a description of gathering vital energy, not a reference to marriage or an elderly woman.

[^j09-meng-instruction]: **Opening ignorance and respecting inquiry.** 蒙 encompasses an unformed or inexperienced condition, not simply stupidity. 筮 remains divination, even where the commentators extend the exchange into instruction and political counsel. Their warning concerns the sincerity and readiness of the exchange: the teacher or diviner who keeps answering may also profane it. “The work of becoming a sage” renders 作聖之功, avoiding the suggestion that childhood itself is already complete sagehood.

[^j09-meng-single-work]: **One educational process.** Lin expressly says that opening ignorance and nurturing the unformed are not two separate undertakings. One names the process in relation to earlier ignorance, the other in relation to later sagehood. This also explains his reading of 利貞 as governing the preceding instruction, not introducing a new practice after enlightenment. The translation does not substitute an esoteric initiation for this ethical and educational account.

[^j09-xu-patience]: **Waiting as a strength.** Xu's waiting is not incapacity. Wang Shenzi and Cai Qing locate patient restraint in inward firmness: one can recognize danger without being driven into it. “Firm and vigorous” therefore does not invariably mean immediate forward action. Conversely, the later statement that going brings accomplishment prevents waiting from becoming permanent withdrawal.

[^j09-xu-correct-center]: **Correctness beyond the positional rule.** Cheng includes the second line in 正中 even though a yang second line does not occupy an ordinarily correct odd-numbered place. He is discussing centered, ethically fitting conduct, not simply repeating the formal rule of placement. This distinction recurs particularly clearly in Li's fifth line at the end of the juan. The apparent discrepancy is not corrected into a different line number.

[^j09-song-danger]: **A conflict or one person's disposition.** Mao Pu distinguishes “firmness above, danger below,” which may describe the two parties, from “dangerous yet vigorous,” which may describe one person's character. 險 thus moves between hazardous circumstances and a dangerous disposition. Translating all its occurrences as either external peril or deliberate deceit would suppress part of this discussion.

[^j09-song-text]: **A consequential correction in the canonical passage.** At `4637:130`, the base reads 則來而得中也. The parallel reads 剛來而得中也: “firmness comes and attains the center.” The translation adopts 剛, also required by the following commentaries' discussion of the second nine. It does not retain a misleading “thus comes” or supply an unexplained subject. The warning against carrying contention to its end concerns exhausting a dispute, not a prohibition on completing all undertakings.

[^j09-coming-convention]: **Coming from another kind.** Kong gives both spatial and relational explanations of 來. Movement into a lower trigram can be called coming, but yang approaching a yin line may also be called coming even when that movement is upward. The word is therefore not a universal instruction to move downward. Fusi is Wang Bi's courtesy name. Kong's account is particularly important to the compilation's recurring resistance to treating every “coming” as proof of derivation from another six-line figure.

[^j09-song-supplement]: **Supplementary editorial passage absent from the base.** After Cai Qing's final gloss on Song, the parallel includes the following entire paragraph, translated here rather than inserted among the aligned source blocks: **Editorial Judgment:** “Whenever the Commentary on the Judgments speaks of firmness and yieldingness going and coming, rising and descending, these are all imagined figures. Earlier scholars built a profusion of hexagram-transformation theories on this. Yet consider ‘the small goes, the great comes’ and ‘the great goes, the small comes’ under Peace and Obstruction: these are King Wen's words. From which hexagram, in fact, do they go, and from which do they come? They merely present such images. Following Wang's commentary and Kong's subcommentary in treating them as imagined figures therefore comes close to the meaning.” 虛象 here means images used to convey a relation, not worthless inventions. The passage anticipates the explicit disputes under Following and Adornment.

[^j09-army-harm]: **毒, harm, and the medicinal analogy.** 毒 is rendered “afflict” in the canonical passage, following Zhu's explicit gloss 害, harm. Both principal commentaries acknowledge that raising an army damages resources and injures people before arguing when it can be justified. Hu Bingwen's potent-drug analogy stresses exceptional necessity, not the harmlessness of righteous intentions. These are historical ethical arguments, not a validation of any present-day war or a recommendation to use toxic remedies. The “seasoned elder” mentioned by Zhu recalls the Army's original Judgment in Juan One.

[^j09-bi-surplus]: **An alleged interpolation remains visible.** The source prints 比吉也 as a separate passage. Zhu initially suspects all three characters; his recorded conversation later proposes removing only 也 and reading “Holding Together: good fortune.” Cheng and Kong explain the existing words. The translation preserves the passage and all three treatments. It would be inaccurate to present one commentator's preferred deletion as an uncontested correction to the selected edition.

[^j09-bi-divination]: **Consultation and the qualities it tests.** The quoted oracle follows the earlier translation's “consult the oracle again,” while Cheng develops 原筮 as examining and determining the Way of association. 元永貞 likewise receives a moral and political interpretation: fundamental goodness or origination, endurance, and steadfast correctness. The different explanations remain attributed. They do not establish a general permission to repeat an unwelcome divination until a desired answer is obtained.

[^j09-response-types]: **More than one kind of response.** Formal line correspondence normally pairs positions one and four, two and five, and three and six, with complementary yin and yang. Holding Together's response instead includes the many yin around a single governing yang; Small Restraint reverses those polarities. The Army's response concerns the ruler's support for the commander. The translation therefore does not mechanically expand every 應 into “proper corresponding line.”

[^j09-small-restraint]: **The small restraining and restraint that is small.** Hu Yuan expressly gives both meanings: one yin restrains the five yang, and its restraint can only be slight. Cheng distinguishes the figure's formation from its capacities for success. The cloud-and-rain discussion then interprets what has not yet been fully restrained or released. These distinctions continue the separate readings preserved in Juan Two rather than reducing 畜 to only physical obstruction.

[^j09-xiaoxu-punctuation]: **A split phrase and a changed character.** At `4637:168`, 陽性上進下復乾體 is read as “yang's nature advances upward; below, moreover, is Qian's body.” The base's comma after 復 disrupts this construction; no character needs changing. At `4637:175`, the base's 非不住 is corrected to the parallel's 非不往, “not that there is no onward movement.” The correction matters because Xiang distinguishes an unfinished process from a permanently impossible one.

[^j09-lu-treading]: **The line and the trigram.** Wang Shenzi explains yieldingness treading upon firmness through the third six among yang lines; Hu Bingwen insists upon Dui as a whole in relation to Qian. The compilers retain both. Their “treading” is a structural and ethical relation, not necessarily a literal person walking upward through six locations. The tiger remains the canonical image rather than being replaced by a modern label such as “a powerful institution.”

[^j09-lu-conditions]: **A conditional explanation of an affirmative sentence.** Cheng reads the fifth's freedom from affliction conditionally: **if** it is free of defects, its conduct shines. He names decisive treading as a possible defect, recalling the fifth line's warning in Juan Two. The canonical praise and the line's danger are thus not flattened into a uniform favorable verdict. 疚 can indicate affliction, distress, or a defect according to the explanation.

[^j09-tai-levels]: **Three interpretive levels in Peace.** Xiang distinguishes mutual interaction without preference for either cosmic force, a preference for what occupies the interior, and finally a moral preference for yang's growth. The same figures therefore support different kinds of statement. It is misleading to infer that every mention of yin anywhere in the book means evil or every mention of yang means virtue. Qiu further distinguishes communion of energies or hearts from erasure of spatial or social distinctions.

[^j09-tai-transformation]: **Diminishing a way, not eliminating people.** Wang Yinglin's quoted explanation explicitly denies that the decline of the petty person's Way requires eliminating petty people. It instead means transforming them into noble people. Qiao's medical analogy serves the same point in political terms. These passages qualify other, more exclusionary accounts elsewhere in the compilation; the different emphases are retained rather than reconciled into one systematic doctrine.

[^j09-pi-human-way]: **A world without a true state.** 天下无邦 is not translated as a claim that all territorial states literally vanish. Cheng explains it as loss of the relationship that makes a state fulfill its purpose: government above and allegiance below no longer communicate. “Not the human Way” continues the translation of 否之匪人 in Juan Two; the following Commentary supplies its relational and political explanation.

[^j09-pi-yinyang]: **The scope of a generalization.** Wu Qi's claim that only four hexagrams speak of yin and yang concerns the wording and explanatory emphasis under discussion, not every occurrence of those words in the entire compilation. It should not be turned into a claim that the other sixty figures lack yin and yang. The comment is retained as his generalization; the surrounding explanations themselves use the terms far more widely.

[^j09-tongren-public]: **One correspondent or all five yang.** Zhu and Cheng identify Qian with the fifth nine. The compilers object that confining the passage to the second and fifth would not adequately express fellowship in the open country. They take Qian as a collective designation for the five yang. The disagreement affects the breadth of fellowship, not merely the labeling of a line, and is left explicit.

[^j09-tongren-text]: **辦 and the repeated heading.** At `4637:213`, the base's 非柔之所能辨 is read with the parallel as 非柔之所能辦, “not something yieldingness can accomplish,” rather than discriminate. The following 同人曰 is retained as “Fellowship says.” Zhu and Cheng call it superfluous, whereas Kong treats it as a regular introduction to the hexagram's words. No deletion has been made on their behalf.

[^j09-dayou-great]: **The great and the worthy.** 大 can name the yang lines, the scope of possession, or the worthiness of those gathered around the ruler. “Great Possession” is therefore not reduced to material wealth. Yang Wanli and Xiang contrast Fellowship's “I unite with others” with Great Possession's “others unite with me.” The direction of association is essential to their explanation.

[^j09-dayou-yuan]: **元亨 and 大亨 are not identical graphs.** Both may be readable as “great success,” but Cheng distinguishes them expressly. He treats 元 as great goodness where the formula is not completed by 利貞, and calls 大亨 in Pushing Upward a textual error. This is Cheng's proposed correction, not one adopted into the translated canonical text here. His claim that goodness precedes evil is likewise an argument within his account, not an independently established chronology of every event. Transliteration is retained where needed to make the graphic distinction legible in English.

[^j09-dayou-name]: **Name versus capacity.** Cheng denies that the name Great Possession alone guarantees great success; the compilers counter that the virtues and structure unpack what the name already contains. Both agree that the figure's favorable meaning involves honoring the worthy. Their methodological disagreement remains intact and connects directly with the introductory editorial account of names and statements.

[^j09-modesty-descent]: **濟 and 際.** Cheng proposes reading 下濟 as 下際, descent into intercourse or contact, rather than descent to aid. The canonical text retains 濟, while his proposed reading appears explicitly in the commentary. The two explanations of Heaven's descent and Earth's ascent also depend on the figure's two trigrams, not on an assertion that the physical sky and ground exchange places.

[^j09-modesty-fullness]: **Fullness and self-importance.** 盈 denotes physical fullness and, in human application, self-satisfaction or overreaching. The translation keeps “fullness” so that celestial, geographical, spiritual, and human comparisons remain linked. Zhu reads 尊 and 卑 as contrasting honored and lowly positions, while Cheng describes modesty itself as outwardly lowly yet exalted in virtue. Their syntactic disagreement is preserved in the collected discussion.

[^j09-modesty-reading]: **Harm, not blessing-and-harm.** At `4637:249`, the base has 盈滿者福害之. The parallel has 盈滿者禍害之: fullness receives misfortune and harm. The English follows 禍, preserving the contrast with 謙損者福祐之, modest self-diminution receiving blessing and assistance. This is an attested transcription correction, not an attempt to make every maxim in the chapter uniformly consoling.

[^j09-modesty-taiji]: **No thing to claim as one's own.** Zhu's remark that there is originally no thing in the Supreme Ultimate introduces the question of possessing achievements as private merits. It should not be isolated as a simple denial that things exist. The explanation immediately appeals to Heaven and Earth generating beings without claiming a benefit for themselves. The source's metaphysical expression is retained rather than replaced by a modern therapeutic instruction about self-esteem.

[^j09-yu-response]: **The many yin respond.** At `4637:259`, the base's 四為群陰以應 is read with the parallel as 四為群陰所應: the fourth is **responded to by** the many yin. The passive construction matters because this line supplies the firmness whose purpose can proceed. The commentary also distinguishes movement that follows principle from movement to which the multitude willingly responds.

[^j09-yu-syntax]: **The work done by conjunctions.** The editors give different weights to 而, 以, and 而以: coordinate qualities, a governing first quality, or emphasis on the second. English cannot reproduce the same particles mechanically, so the explanation names their functions. The quoted phrases should be read with their accompanying account, not as a universal rule that “and” always has one force in Classical Chinese.

[^j09-time-meaning-use]: **Eleven or twelve, and why “time” matters.** Cheng lists eleven hexagrams under the formulas praising time, the meaning of a time, or its use. Xiang lists twelve by including Following. Both readings occur in the parallel text. Cheng's later discussion of Following explicitly distinguishes 隨時之義, the meaning of following the time, from 隨之時義, the meaning of Following's time; this helps explain the differing classification. Neither enumeration has been silently corrected. 時 includes the fitting conditions of a situation, not only an hour, season, or date. These distinctions are relevant to application but do not create a free license to disregard the text whenever circumstances are inconvenient.

[^j09-sui-transformation]: **A disagreement about derivation.** Zhu invokes hexagram transformation; Cheng describes exchanged Qian and Kun lines. Wang Zongchuan objects to deriving Following from Obstruction and Decay from Peace, arguing that the relationships already arise when trigrams are doubled. The compilers expressly endorse that objection. These statements are preserved even though they do not compose a single consistent theory. A reader should not assume the compilation has silently adopted every derivation it quotes.

[^j09-sui-time-text]: **Two changes reported in Wang Su's text.** The base's canonical text reads 天下隨時, “the world follows the time,” and 隨時之義, “the meaning of following the time.” Zhu reports Wang Su's 天下隨之, “the world follows it,” and 隨之時義, “the meaning of Following's time,” and prefers both. Cheng defends the transmitted time-centered reading. The translation leaves the base's wording in the canonical passages and translates the proposed alternatives separately. This preserves the substantial dispute rather than silently choosing Zhu's text for all voices.

[^j09-gu-cause-cure]: **The cause of decay or its remedy?** Zhu and Yu Yan explain deference and stopping as the conditions producing decay; Cheng makes ordered relations and resting in compliance part of its cure. The text openly disputes which reading is right. “Deference” here can become drifting accommodation, but Xun is not inherently condemned everywhere in the book. Its value depends on the combination and the particular interpretation.

[^j09-gu-attribution]: **The uncertain 集氏.** The selected base and parallel both attribute the short gloss to 集氏. “Master Ji” preserves that reading without assigning a personal name. It is not silently changed to another commentator with a more familiar surname. The same attribution was retained and noted in the earlier translation. Guishan in the following paragraph is the appellation under which Yang Shi is discussed.

[^j09-gu-jia]: **Repair requires action.** Jia is the first of the ten celestial stems; geng, discussed by comparison with Penetration, marks another point in the cycle and is associated here with alteration. The comments distinguish beginning anew from correcting an existing course. Yang Wanli explicitly denies that disorder cures itself. Thus Heaven's movement from ending to beginning is not presented uniformly as a reason to wait passively for repair.

[^j09-lin-supplement]: **Supplementary Zhu Xi gloss.** Between “joyful and compliant; firm at the center, with a response” and the editorial paragraph, the parallel has a short *Original Meaning* gloss absent from the base: 又以卦德卦體言卦之善. Its complete translation is: **Original Meaning:** “This further states the hexagram's goodness through its virtues and structure.” It is supplied here only. The long editorial paragraph immediately following it **is** present in the base and is translated in the main text; it is not a supplementary passage.

[^j09-lin-approach]: **Approach and beneficent oversight.** The compilers reject explaining 臨 only as bearing down upon petty people. They combine the growth of yang with harmonious response, common purpose, and beneficence reaching the world. “Approach” remains the established hexagram name, while overseeing or presiding appears where needed to preserve the political application. Neither sense should erase the other.

[^j09-lin-eight-months]: **Calendar and number are competing accounts.** Cheng counts inclusively from Return in the zi month to Retreat in the wei month. Lu and the compilers instead relate eight to young yin and month to a yin image, contrasting seven as young yang and day as a yang image. The editors explicitly warn against forcing every numbered interval into a hexagram-calendar correlation. These are historical interpretations, not verified schedules of future events. The discussion extends, rather than replaces, the alternatives already noted in Juan Three.

[^j09-guan-seeing]: **Seeing and being seen.** 觀 means viewing, but also an exemplar presented for others to view. The first passage and Cheng's explanation chiefly concern the ruler as a visible example; later clauses concern observing Heaven and being transformed through what one beholds. “Exemplar” makes the active political role readable without deleting the shared root of sight and contemplation. Cultivated clarity, 文明, does not mean modern technological civilization.

[^j09-guan-ritual]: **Washing, libation, and offering.** Zhu Zhen carefully distinguishes 盥, washing the hands, from 祼, the libation, and 薦, presenting offerings. 尸 is the ancestral personator in the rite, not a corpse. These distinctions are necessary to follow his sequence and the argument about undispersed sincerity. The translation does not merge all three acts into a generic sacrifice. The ritual explanation continues the discussion under Viewing in Juan Three.

[^j09-numinous-way]: **神道 is broader than a “way of spirits.”** “Numinous Way” preserves the text's sense of subtle, efficacious operation not reducible to visible coercion. Some comments explicitly discuss spirits, while others explain seasonal regularity, sincerity, and unforced transformation. Translating the entire passage as either supernatural manipulation or a wholly secular technique would suppress part of its range. The English attributes these claims to their respective speakers rather than presenting them as tested causal laws.

[^j09-guan-reading]: **Great transformation, not unchanged influence.** At `4637:350`, the base reads 不變之感. The parallel reads 丕變之感, the response or influence of great transformation. The English follows 丕, consistent with the passage's account of transforming others. This is a textual correction, not an interpretive substitution of change for a deliberate doctrine of changelessness.

[^j09-shihe-justice]: **Adjudication and differing uses of firmness.** 獄 means a legal case or the judicial process here, not merely a prison. Cheng distinguishes the yielding or firm constitution of a line from the mode of action indicated by its place. Zhu Zhen and Zhao Rumei particularly emphasize the ruler's compassion. These are distinct historical accounts of judging and punishment, not a single modern theory of criminal justice. The bodily act of biting remains explicit before its political application.

[^j09-shihe-pardon]: **三宥, not 三又.** At `4637:362`, the base has 三又而後制刑. The parallel reads 三宥而後制刑, invoking consideration of leniency before punishment. The translation follows 宥 and renders the threefold consideration as “the three grounds for leniency.” The distinction between 宥, mitigation, and 赦, pardon, is explicit in [Fang Hui’s *Continued Examination of Ancient and Modern Matters*, “The Official of Inquiry”](https://www.shidianguji.com/book/SK1805b/chapter/1l9o3vhttqux6), which quotes the *Offices of Zhou*. No modern legal procedure is implied. The correction is consequential: 三又 by itself obscures the act of lenient deliberation that supports Zhao's argument for yielding virtue.

[^j09-bi-pattern]: **文 is not ornament alone.** In this discussion 文 includes visible pattern, cultivated expression, and the patterned order of ritual. 質 is the underlying substance or material on which pattern depends. “Adornment” remains the hexagram's name, while “pattern” lets the argument move between the natural and human orders without reducing ritual to decoration. The initial “success” is retained even though Zhu suspects it is superfluous.

[^j09-bi-interweaving]: **A possible missing phrase is not inserted.** Zhu reports the proposal that 剛柔交錯, firmness and yieldingness interweaving, originally preceded 天文也. He finds it plausible, not certain. The translated canonical passage therefore retains the base without inserting those four characters. His report appears in the commentary. This is distinct from a transcription correction attested in the parallel edition.

[^j09-bi-derivation]: **One hexagram can become sixty-three others.** Su Shi's point is methodological: the possibility of transforming Peace into Adornment does not uniquely establish that derivation, since other transformations are possible. He instead grounds the language of coming and going in Qian and Kun's generation of the six children. This rejects a particular account of derivation, not all relationships among figures or all uses of changing lines.

[^j09-human-pattern]: **Restraint completes expression.** “Cultivated clarity with restraint” preserves the connection of Li and Gen and the claim that each relation has its proper share or limit. It does not mean suppressing all expression. The comments on ruler and minister, family, and friendship explain how visible ritual and maintained distinctions together constitute human pattern. The source's social assumptions remain visible rather than being silently replaced by modern egalitarian ones.

[^j09-bi-middle-line]: **A tension retained in Cheng's explanation.** At `4637:377`, Cheng speaks of apportioning Qian's middle line to adorn Gen's top. His later account at `4637:385` insists that the lower Li derives from changing Qian's middle line and the upper Gen from changing Kun's top line, not from one line moving upward within a completed hexagram. Both formulations occur in the parallel and are retained. The translation does not invent a corrected line number or harmonize the accounts by deleting the difficult phrase.

[^j09-bi-final-reading]: **A simple transcription correction.** At `4637:385`, 大下成其禮俗 is read with the parallel as 天下成其禮俗, bringing the world's rituals and customs to completion. The same long paragraph distinguishes several ways in which a figure's meaning is formed: images, line structure, use, shape, and growth or decline. “Formation” in these accounts should not always be mistaken for a chronological claim about when a physical implement or a hexagram was first invented.

[^j09-bo-changing]: **Changing rather than openly deciding.** Chen Youwen contrasts 變, gradual undermining, with 決, open decisive removal. His moral interpretation is expressly attached to the different wording of Splitting Apart and Breakthrough. The following injunction to observe the image and stop is therefore not a general instruction never to oppose wrongdoing; it is the conduct he and Cheng associate with this particular time of declining support.

[^j09-fu-seven]: **Seven days read as seven months.** Hou Xingguo interprets the interval through the cycle from yin's rise to yang's return and cites the *Odes* to defend calling months days. This is his argument, not a silent change of 七日 into 七月 in the canonical text. The compilers' earlier discussion under Approach favors a different numerical explanation and criticizes over-reliance on monthly correspondences. Both are preserved. See also Return's endnotes in Juan Four.

[^j09-fu-heart]: **Heaven and Earth's heart.** 心 is retained as heart, with both affective and understanding functions, rather than being consistently narrowed to either emotion or intellect. Here the commentators explain it through the impulse to generate life and the reemergence of the human original heart. The question does not require imagining Heaven and Earth as a human-shaped organism. Yu Yan also warns against simply identifying this heart with either stillness or movement.

[^j09-fu-shao-poem]: **The poem's unmanifest beginning.** Shao's eight-line poem is presented with line divisions marked by slashes inside its source-aligned paragraph. “Dark wine” 玄酒 is the ritual name for plain water (Kong Yingda’s explanation of the *Record of Rites*, quoted in the [Ministry of Education’s historical dictionary](https://dict.revised.moe.edu.tw/dictView.jsp?ID=112121&la=0&powerMode=0)), fitting the image of an as-yet-unelaborated beginning. The “great sound” likewise points to what is barely manifest. Baoxi is the name used here for Fuxi. The base's 包羲 is retained; the parallel's 包義 is not adopted. The poem's symbolic time should not be read as an independently verified physical account of a solstice.

[^j09-fu-readings]: **Two repairs in the heart discussion.** At `4637:413`, 大地之心 is read with the parallel as 天地之心, Heaven and Earth's heart. At `4637:419`, 動力天地之心 is read as 動為天地之心, “movement is Heaven and Earth's heart.” The latter restores the second of Yu Yan's paired rejections: neither simply equating the heart with stillness nor simply equating it with movement is sufficient. A missing copula must not become a new doctrine of “motive force.”

[^j09-fu-stillness]: **Different reference points, not simply opposing schools.** Yichuan is Cheng Yi; Lianxi is Zhou Dunyi. Zhu distinguishes the turning back or gathering associated with benefit and steadfastness from the first renewed movement associated with origination. He prefers Cheng's account for this hexagram while saying that the underlying principle in the two explanations is the same. The translation preserves both his preference and his qualification.

[^j09-fu-heart-learning]: **Refinement, singleness, and the center.** 精 and 一 are rendered refined attention and single-mindedness in their practical context: discerning a first wrong movement and not repeating it. 道心 and 人心 are the heart of the Way and the precarious human heart; they are not presented as two anatomical organs. The editors' attribution of this teaching to transmission from Yao to Shun belongs to their historical account. The heart's “heavenly ruler” is a metaphor for its governing function.

[^j09-wuwang-going]: **Going wrongly or going beyond the right?** Zhu understands the final warning as going under the stated condition of incorrectness. Cheng instead takes further going beyond an already correct, unfalsified condition as entry into falsity. Hu Bingwen expressly contrasts these readings. The canonical passage's compressed “such a going” leaves the explanations to identify the referent; it does not erase the disagreement. “Heaven's mandate” is also explained here as Heaven's Way, not only a ruler's political legitimacy.

[^j09-wuwang-within]: **An image of origin is not an external imposition.** Zhao and the editors distinguish a firm line's coming from outer Qian from the human possession of principle. They explicitly deny that heavenly principle is something imposed or melted into a person from outside. The translation retains “comes from outside” for the diagram and the qualification for human application. Without both, the passage's analogy would be misleading.

[^j09-daxu-three]: **Accumulation, nourishment, restraint.** Zheng's three meanings of 畜 govern the whole section: storing virtue, supporting worthy people, and checking vigorous force. The established title Great Restraint is therefore only one aspect of the word. “Earnest substance” renders 篤實, the firm grounding that makes accumulated learning and virtue effective, rather than mere quantity of information or wealth.

[^j09-daxu-supplement]: **Supplementary Zhu Xi gloss.** After the canonical passage at `4637:436`, the parallel includes a gloss absent from the base: 以卦變卦體釋卦辭. Its complete translation is: **Original Meaning:** “This explains the hexagram statement through transformation and structure.” It is supplied here, not inserted before Cheng's commentary in the aligned main text.

[^j09-daxu-reading]: **Action responding to Heaven.** At the end of Cheng's explanation in `4637:447`, the base reads 所以能應乎天, while the parallel has 所行能應乎天. The English follows the latter, “the action can answer Heaven,” which continues the immediately preceding account of acting in response to Qian. This is a correction supported by the parallel, not an attempt to turn all metaphysical language into action-oriented advice.

[^j09-yi-nourishment]: **What one feeds and how one sustains oneself.** 所養 can concern the people one nourishes and the Way of nourishing them; 自養 turns the inquiry toward one's own sustenance and cultivation. The later comments distinguish nourishing the greater moral capacities from giving priority to appetite and bodily maintenance. They do not require translating every reference to food into a spiritual metaphor. The mouth remains part of the text's concrete image.

[^j09-yi-reading]: **Ancient observation, not a new divination rule.** At `4637:455`, the base's 占之觀人 is corrected to the parallel's 古之觀人, “in antiquity, observers of people.” This prevents an accidental claim that the paragraph introduces a special divinatory procedure. The argument concerns judging people through what they nourish and what they themselves cultivate.

[^j09-yi-worthy]: **Institutional nourishment.** Zhao's point is that a ruler cannot personally supply every household. Nourishing worthy officials makes wider beneficence possible. The fifth and top lines supply the diagrammatic relation used by the editors. The base has no 集說 heading before Zhao's paragraph, so the English retains his direct attribution without silently adding a source block. The parallel does label it as collected explanation.

[^j09-daguo-mean]: **Exceeding the ordinary without exceeding principle.** The central distinction is between ordinary measure and what a particular emergency requires. Great Excess is not permission for excess as such. Zhu Zhen calls an extraordinary response proportionate to an extraordinary condition the mean appropriate to the time. Hu Bingwen adds that the proper time without the necessary ability is still insufficient. Both qualifications matter to a practitioner's reading.

[^j09-daguo-medicine]: **A historical medical analogy.** The potent medicine causing dizziness illustrates proportionality between remedy and condition. It is not present-day treatment guidance, and the translation does not endorse inducing adverse effects or using toxic substances. The relevant interpretive point is that a response excessive for a slight difficulty may be proportionate to a severe one, provided the necessary judgment and capacity are present.

[^j09-daguo-going]: **Success depends on action here.** Xiang distinguishes a statement that independently promises success and benefit from one where success follows specifically from going. 乃 carries that dependence: “and thus there is success.” This contrasts with the warnings against forward action elsewhere. The compilation requires attention to the particular statement's structure rather than a universal preference for either action or waiting.

[^j09-kan-practice]: **Repetition and becoming practiced.** 習 can signify repetition and also practice or familiarity. The canonical phrase is translated “Repeated Kan,” while Kong and the editors develop the need to become practiced in difficult conditions. The two senses are related but not identical. This is not an additional chapter on casting the same hexagram repeatedly.

[^j09-kan-fullness]: **Not yet full or not overflowing?** Cheng reads 不盈 as the pit not yet filled, after which water can emerge. Zhu's recorded explanation distinguishes level fullness from overflowing; Yu emphasizes continued flow, while Liang combines filling with timely advance. The English lets each speaker use the sense his explanation requires. Rendering every occurrence uniformly as “empty” or uniformly as “overflowing” would conceal the disagreement.

[^j09-kan-punctuation]: **Both clauses concern water.** Hu Bingwen's 水字當讀 is read as an instruction to pause after 水. Thus both “flows without overflowing” and “passes through danger without losing constancy” predicate qualities of water. The base's punctuation before the quotation can obscure this instruction. No character change is required. Constancy and trustworthiness translate the physical and ethical uses of 信 without suggesting that water literally makes a verbal promise.

[^j09-kan-barriers]: **Danger encountered and defenses employed.** 險 covers dangerous terrain and the obstacles that protect a place from intrusion. “Barriers” is used when the passage discusses Heaven's height, Earth's difficult terrain, and deliberate defenses. The editors expressly distinguish 習險, practiced engagement with difficulty, from 用險, employing protective difficulty. Yu Yan further limits such use to its fitting occasion rather than making it a permanent mode of rule.

[^j09-kan-reading]: **The main forms of defense.** At `4637:503`, the base's 大瑞 is corrected to the parallel's 大端, the principal forms or major categories. This restores Cheng's distinction between physical defenses and the social boundaries he subsequently includes. The same historical hierarchy of rank, insignia, and permitted conduct remains visible; it is not silently converted into contemporary civic institutions.

[^j09-kan-allusions]: **Four examples of strategic access.** Wang Yinglin lists places whose capture, fortification, loss, or crossing changes a state's security: Xiayang and Guo, Hulao and Zheng, the lands west of the river and Wei, and Daxian and Yan. They are kept as named historical allusions, not collapsed into one campaign. The comment's immediate function is to show why control of a barrier matters. This translation has not undertaken a separate historical verification of all four campaigns or their chronology.

[^j09-li-clinging]: **Attachment and brightness.** Li is explained through 麗, clinging or attachment, rather than by treating “fire” as an adequate translation in every sentence. Sun and moon, growing plants, flame, and the relation of spirit to form provide different examples. Xiang and the editors then disagree with an account that makes the relation yin clinging to yang. Those directions are preserved, even where other parts of the compilation use a different structural explanation.

[^j09-li-essence-spirit]: **Repunctuating union and separation.** At `4637:513`, the base's stops divide 其合也氣聚而形成於有 and 其分也氣散而神泯於無. The English restores the balanced construction: when they unite, energy gathers and form comes into being; when they separate, energy disperses and spirit fades into nonbeing. This is repunctuation, not a replacement of characters. 精, 神, 氣, and 形 are rendered essence, spirit, vital energy, and form within the historical account; they are not claimed as modern anatomical or physiological categories.

[^j09-li-cow]: **Nurturing compliance.** The cow remains the concrete image, while the commentators explain it through the nourishment of utmost compliance. Its relation to centered correctness is essential: the passage does not praise compliance without regard to what one follows. The gloss continues the treatment of Li in Juan Four and should not be read as a literal instruction to acquire or raise livestock in every consultation.

[^j09-li-position]: **A deliberately nonmechanical judgment.** Cheng is asked directly how a yin fifth line can be correct. He answers that Li emphasizes what it clings to: the fifth position is correct and central, and the yielding line clinging to it attains correctness. This is his explicit interpretation, not a typographical error in the line number. Hu Bingwen nevertheless gives greater weight to the formally correct second six. The juan therefore ends by reinforcing that structural rules require contextual judgment rather than mechanical application.
