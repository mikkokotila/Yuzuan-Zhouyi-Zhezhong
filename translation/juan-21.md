## Supplementary Discussions to the Introduction · 啓蒙附論

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4718:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4718:002 -->

Zhu Xi wrote the Introduction because those who explained the Changes through images and numbers so often pursued groundless subtleties and scattered interpretations without evidence. Yet the Changes was indeed made through images and numbers; these could not simply be passed over without examination. Moreover, in his day, those who discussed the Chart and Writing, hexagram strokes, and stalk numbers devised novel doctrines to overturn established methods, making their own ingenuity their teacher and disparaging earlier worthies. Zhu Xi therefore set out this work for students: anyone wishing to understand how the Changes was made could find an entrance here. We now gather several aspects of what the Chart and Writing, hexagram strokes, and stalk numbers contain—marvels of interweaving and transformation that can bring out meanings Zhu Xi left undeveloped. For each we provide a diagram or table and attach an explanation. Our purpose is to show that the Chart and Writing are Heaven and Earth’s patterned expression, and that establishing the hexagrams and generating the stalk method are the work of sages endowed with spiritual insight. Here the myriad principles have their root, and the myriad methods their beginning. Such things are assuredly beyond the intervention of human effort and private cleverness. The world’s restless fabrications and imitations, its petty doubts and disputes, may then be stilled.[^j21-voice]

When the Great Commentary speaks of the River Chart, it says 1 and 2, 3 and 4, 5 and 6, 7 and 8, 9 and 10: the numbers accompany one another in pairs. When the Elder Dai’s Record of Rites speaks of the Luo Writing, it says 2–9–4, 7–5–3, 6–1–8: they accompany one another in threes. Thus, tracing the River Chart to its beginning, with 1 there is at once 2; with 3 there is at once 4; at 5 one reaches the center. With 6 there is at once 7; with 8 there is at once 9; at 10 one again reaches the center. They are distributed in sequence to form the five positions. Tracing the Luo Writing to its beginning, with 1–2–3 there is at once 4–5–6; with 4–5–6 there is at once 7–8–9. They are set out in layers to form the four directions. If one considers yang moving and yin still, the numbers begin above: the River Chart’s 1 and 2 originally lie above, 3 and 4 to the right, 6 and 7 below, and 8 and 9 to the left; the Luo Writing’s 1–2–3, 4–5–6, and 7–8–9 originally descend from top to bottom. The yang numbers then move and exchange places, while the yin numbers remain still: the positions of the River Chart and Luo Writing are formed. If one considers yang still and yin moving, the numbers begin below: the River Chart’s 1 and 2 originally lie below, 3 and 4 to the left, 6 and 7 above, and 8 and 9 to the right; the Luo Writing’s 1–2–3, 4–5–6, and 7–8–9 originally ascend from bottom to top. The yang numbers remain still, while the yin numbers move and exchange places: once again the positions of the River Chart and Luo Writing are formed. Accompaniment in pairs is like Heaven implying Earth, a ruler implying ministers, or a husband implying a wife. Accompaniment in threes is like Heaven and Earth implying human beings, ruler and ministers implying the people, or parents implying children. Yang moving and yin still is like Qian ruling and Kun storing, the ruler commanding and ministers following, the husband acting and the wife complying: this proceeds from above downward and speaks of functioning. Yang still and yin moving is like Qian presiding and Kun laboring, the ruler at ease and ministers at work, the father dwelling peacefully while wife and children attend to their duties: this proceeds from within outward and speaks of structure. Sharing one root and accompanying one another, they accomplish the work of unity; movement and stillness sustain one another and spread the transformations of generation and completion. The subtleties of creative transformation and human affairs are exhausted here. The deepest meanings of the Earlier Heaven and Later Heaven diagrams all issue from this.[^j21-structure-function]

The Luo Writing, with its three-by-three accumulation of numbers, is the source of numerical arrangements: arrays of four and beyond all take it as their model. Why? Three is Heaven’s number, so its image is round, as in the preceding diagrams. The numbers in the four directions and the four corners either move or remain still—the central number is fixed—and each arrangement produces 15 along every row and column. Four is Earth’s number, so its image is square, as in the following diagrams. The numbers at the center and corners, and those at the four sides, likewise either move or remain still; each arrangement produces 34 along every row and column. Five-by-five and the succeeding arrays have their root in the three-by-three diagram; six-by-six and the succeeding arrays have their root in the four-by-four diagram. Yet the four-by-four diagram itself has its root in the three-by-three. Thus the Luo Writing’s being the source of numbers is an unalterable conclusion. We append the four-by-four diagrams below to illustrate the correspondence. The others are contained in mathematics and are not reproduced in full.[^j21-magic-squares]

The Great Commentary says, “Heaven: 1; Earth: 2. Heaven: 3; Earth: 4. Heaven: 5; Earth: 6. Heaven: 7; Earth: 8. Heaven: 9; Earth: 10.” Heaven’s and Earth’s numbers proceed from fewer to more, and from more return again to fewer: this is the source of addition and subtraction. It also says, “Give Heaven three and Earth two, and upon these establish the numbers.” Heaven’s numbers proceed by threes, Earth’s by twos: this is the source of multiplication and division. Thus the River Chart takes 1 and 2 as the beginning of numerical structure; the Luo Writing takes 3 and 2 as the beginning of numerical functioning. The Luo Writing’s functioning begins with threes and twos because these are its root. Yet in truth all the numbers circulate and serve as one another’s root; none fails to contain the methods of multiplication and division, and all in turn have addition and subtraction as their foundation. We have now derived from the Luo Writing four methods of addition and subtraction, sixteen of multiplication and division, five of forming square areas, and four of right-triangle calculation. The sixteen Luo methods of multiplication and division can be reduced to eight. Why? Five is the central number of both the River and Luo diagrams; numbers beyond it are generated through it. Five plus 1 is 6, and 6 minus 5 is 1: 6 and 1 therefore share a root. Five plus 2 is 7, and 7 minus 5 is 2: 7 and 2 likewise share a root. The same holds for 3 and 8, and 4 and 9. When 3 and 8 multiply odd and even numbers leftward, both yield adjoining odd or even numbers: from this one can understand that 8 is equivalent to 3. When 2 and 7 multiply odd and even numbers rightward, both yield adjoining odd or even numbers: one can understand that 7 is equivalent to 2. The apparent exception is multiplication of odd numbers by 2, which yields an even number two positions away. What is obtained shares a root with the adjoining odd position, so it is as though the adjoining position were obtained. For example, 2 × 9 = 18; 8 and 3 share a root, so obtaining 8 is like obtaining the adjoining 3. The other cases follow this pattern. When 1 and 6 multiply, both yield the odd or even number of the original position: one can understand that 6 is equivalent to 1. The apparent exception is multiplication of odd numbers by 6, which yields an adjoining even number. The result shares a root with the original number, so it is as though the original position were obtained. Thus 6 × 7 = 42; 7 and 2 share a root, so obtaining 2 is like obtaining the original 7. The other cases follow this pattern. When 4 and 9 multiply, both yield the odd or even number opposite: one can understand that 9 is equivalent to 4. The apparent exception is multiplication of odd numbers by 4, which yields an even number two positions away. The result shares a root with the opposite position, so it is as though the opposite were obtained. Thus 4 × 9 = 36; 6 and 1 share a root, so obtaining 6 is like obtaining the opposite 1. The other cases follow this pattern. Why do some cases yield only a number sharing the same root? Whether an odd or an even number multiplies an even number, the results are even and coincide: 3 × 4 = 12, while 8 × 4 = 32. An odd number multiplying an odd number yields an odd result. An even number multiplying that odd number cannot yield the same odd result, and so yields only the even number sharing its root. Thus 3 × 3 = 9, while 8 × 3 = 24; 9 and 4 share a root, so obtaining 4 is like obtaining 9. Hence 1/6, 2/7, 3/8, and 4/9 are paired within the four directions in the River Chart, but adjoining across cardinal and corner positions in the Luo Writing: their numbers arise from the central 5 and share a root.[^j21-same-root]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4718:103 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4718:104 -->

Numbers have partners and opposites. Partner numbers arise through 5; opposite numbers complete 10. The pairs 1/6, 2/7, 3/8, and 4/9 are partners: their differences are all 5. The pairs 1/9, 2/8, 3/7, and 4/6 are opposites: their sums are all 10. In the River Chart, partners occupy the same direction and opposites adjoin. In the Luo Writing, partners adjoin and opposites face one another. Among partners, 6 follows 1, 7 follows 2, 8 follows 3, and 9 follows 4, as in the sixteen preceding multiplication-and-division methods. Among opposites, 9 follows 1, 8 follows 2, 7 follows 3, and 6 follows 4, as in the five square-area methods below. Whenever partner numbers multiply the same number, the results must agree: with an even multiplicand the numbers coincide; with an odd multiplicand they share a root. If each partner is multiplied by itself, the results must themselves be partners—for example, 3 × 3 gives 9 and 8 × 8 gives 64. When opposite numbers multiply the same number, the results must be opposites: 3 × 3 gives 9 and 7 × 3 gives 21. If each opposite is multiplied by itself, the results must coincide: 1 × 1 gives 1, and 9 × 9 also gives 81; 2 × 2 gives 4, and 8 × 8 also gives 64. Thus, in squaring partners, if one gives its own number, the other also gives its own—1 gives 1 and 6 gives 6. If one gives its opposite, the other also gives its opposite—4 gives 6 and 9 gives 1. If one gives its neighbor, the other likewise gives its neighbor—3 gives 9 and 8 gives 4; 2 gives 4 and 7 gives 9. In squaring opposites, if one gives its own number, the other gives its opposite—1 gives 1 and 9 also gives 1; 6 gives 6 and 4 also gives 6. If one gives its neighbor, the other likewise gives its neighbor—3 gives 9 and 7 also gives 9; 2 gives 4 and 8 also gives 4. All converge upon 1,6,4,9 and are brought into accord. Hence the self-products involved in extracting square roots are confined to 1,6,4,9. This is why, in the Luo Writing, 1,6,4,9 occupy the upper and lower positions as the warp, while 2,7,3,8 occupy the left and right as the weft.[^j21-square-last-digits]

Later Heaven determines the division from the strokes: those governed by yang are yang trigrams; those governed by yin are yin trigrams. <!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4718:158 -->

“How are its patterns and images natural?” The reply: “Fire’s heat and brightness plainly make it yang. The marsh is water’s accumulated moisture, driven by yang qi to nourish the myriad things; it too is yang. Water’s darkness, coldness, and severity plainly make it yin. The mountain is an elevation of Earth’s form, one substance with Earth; it too is yin. Thus the Earlier Heaven trigrams give the primary images of yin and yang. When they change to Later Heaven, fire and marsh follow wind and become yin, while water and mountain follow thunder and become yang. This has a reason. Yin and yang qi never bring anything to completion without joining; yet there is a distinction between what first stimulates and what afterward responds. What begins with yang and encounters yin belongs to yang; what begins with yin and encounters yang belongs to yin. When yang qi below is about to emerge and meets yin pressing upon it, it rouses itself as thunder. When yang qi within is about to disperse and meets yin enclosing it, it gathers under constraint as rain. When yang qi rises straight upward and meets yin supporting it, it comes to rest as mountain. These all begin with yang and encounter yin; hence they are yang trigrams. When yin is within, yang qi must enter and disperse it: one sees this in wind ceasing only when the overcast has cleared. When yin is in the middle, yang qi must cling to it and disperse it: one sees this in fire going out only when the fuel is consumed. When yin is outside, yang qi must spread and disperse it: one sees this in the marsh drying only when its moisture is exhausted. These all begin with yin and encounter yang; hence they are yin trigrams. Taken together, only Qian’s pure yang and Kun’s pure yin cannot change classification. Thunder is the beginning of yang’s movement, wind the beginning of yin’s arising: these too cannot change. Fire warms and the marsh diffuses, so in functioning they are yang. Yet fire is rooted in yin’s dryness, the marsh in yin’s moisture, so in structure they are yin. Water cools and the mountain congeals, so in functioning they are yin. Yet water is rooted in yang’s exhalation and flows, the mountain in yang’s upthrust and rises, so in structure they are yang. Earlier Heaven’s images disclose functioning; Later Heaven’s investigate the root. It is just as benevolence is yang in bringing forth life, yet may also be yin in its gentleness; rightness is yin in gathering and restraining, yet may also be yang in its firmness and decisiveness. Yin and yang are fundamentally one qi, rooted in each other. Thus these principles proceed together without contradiction.”[^j21-natural-explanations]

## Later Heaven: Heaven, Earth, Water, and Fire as Structure and Functioning

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4718:160 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4718:161 -->

![Heaven, Earth, water, and fire in the Later Heaven arrangement](../assets/eee-learning/b8dcc0fa249c-chimon23.png) *Heaven, Earth, water, and fire in the Later Heaven arrangement.*

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4718:161 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4718:162 -->

What makes creative transformation what it is is simply Heaven, Earth, water, and fire. Although the Changes has eight trigrams, in truth there are only four. Why? Wind is Heaven’s qi breathing forth and joining Earth below. Mountain is Earth’s form rising and joining Heaven above. Thunder is fire constrained within Earth, striking and surging forth. Marsh is water gathered upon Earth, spreading and moistening. Daoists speak of Heaven, Earth, sun, and moon; Buddhists of earth, water, fire, and wind; Westerners of water, fire, earth, and air. One can see that creative transformation never departs from these four things. Thus Earlier Heaven makes south and north the warp, occupied by Heaven and Earth as structure; east and west the weft, occupied by water and fire as functioning. Later Heaven takes Heaven and Earth as structure and places them at the four corners; water and fire as functioning and places them at the four cardinal positions. Thunder is fire first emerging, and so moves in spring; when fire spreads its qi, it rules in summer. Marsh is water not yet gathered back, and so disperses in autumn; when water returns to its root, it rules in winter. Water and fire are Heaven and Earth’s functioning; they therefore occupy the cardinal positions and govern the seasons. Heaven’s qi first gives signs in the northwest, then reaches the southeast and joins Earth below: this is the Changes’ “Beneath Heaven there is wind”—Coming to Meet. Thus Qian and Xun face each other as Heaven’s guiding framework. Earth’s work labors in the southwest, then reaches the northeast and joins Heaven above: this is the Changes’ “Heaven within the mountain”—Great Restraint. Thus Kun and Gen face each other as Earth’s ordering framework. Heaven and Earth are the structure of water and fire; they therefore occupy the corners and turn the pivots. Heaven, Earth, water, and fire, structure and functioning rooted in each other, generate and complete the myriad things: this is the subtlety of Earlier and Later Heaven. Considered through the strokes, Zhen is Li: one yin closing it above makes Zhen. Dui is Kan: one yang spreading below makes Dui. Xun is Qian: one yin moving below makes Xun. Gen is Kun: one yang stretching across above makes Gen. Hence the sixty-four hexagrams begin with Qian and Kun, reach Kan and Li in the middle, and end with After Completion and Before Completion. From this one knows that the Way of creative transformation is exhausted by Heaven, Earth, water, and fire.[^j21-four-things]

When yin responds to yang, water generates wind: wind is the mutual response of marsh and thunder through their upper and lower parts. Thus Dui exchanges its top and bottom strokes with Zhen and becomes Xun. Wind is originally Heaven’s qi; joining mountain and entering below it, it meets Earth beneath. Thus Xun exchanges its upper two lines with Gen’s lower two and becomes Kun. Mountain is originally Earth’s substance; joining wind and emerging above it, it meets Heaven above. Thus Gen exchanges its lower two lines with Xun’s upper two and becomes Qian.

Someone asks, “Is there evidence for this in the classics?” The reply: “In the Changes, Heaven and fire make Fellowship: Heaven takes fire as its functioning. Water and Earth make Holding Together: Earth takes water as its functioning. Li is fire and also lightning. The Changes says, ‘Thunder and lightning join and are manifest,’ and also, ‘Thunder and lightning arrive together’: thunder and fire are one qi. A marsh with water makes Limitation; a marsh without water makes Oppression: marsh and water are one thing. The Rites of Zhou says, ‘West of the sun there is much yin.’ The west is a gathering of mountains and therefore has much cloud and thunder; places close to mountain ranges are still like this. It also says, ‘East of the sun there is much wind.’ The east is a gathering of waters and therefore has many winds and gales; coastal places are still like this. Zhuang Zhou says, ‘The Great Clod exhales; its name is wind’: wind joins with Earth’s qi. The Rites speaks of climbing mountains to sacrifice and ascending to present accomplishment to Heaven: mountain joins with Heaven’s qi. Heaven, Earth, water, and fire are simply one yin and one yang. In their dispositions they exchange and communicate; in their forms they change without fixity. Thus Earlier Heaven exchanges and transforms to become Later Heaven: each finds its position and works its subtle transformation, each follows its kind and returns to its root. How could this be accidental?”[^j21-scriptural-evidence]

In the Later Heaven diagram, the Eight Trigrams interchange yin and yang through their upper and lower strokes: this is the Miscellaneous Hexagrams’ root. Why? In Later Heaven, some trigrams transform by following their own kind, others by obtaining their opposite. Qian occupies the northwest and the three yang trigrams follow it; Kun occupies the southwest and the three yin trigrams follow it: these follow their own kind. Qian faces Xun, Kan faces Li, Gen faces Kun, and Zhen faces Dui: these obtain their opposites. This is the successive order of the three yang trigrams. Xun becomes Li when a yang is removed above and a yang generated below. Li becomes Dui when a yang is removed above and a yang generated below. Dui returns to Xun when a yin is removed above and a yin generated below. This is the successive order of the three yin trigrams.

Li becomes Kan when a yang is removed below and a yin generated above. Kun becomes Gen when a yin is removed below and a yang generated above. Dui becomes Zhen when a yang is removed below and a yin generated above. This is the order in which the four yin trigrams become the four yang trigrams opposite them.

Among the four yang trigrams, Gen is youngest, Kan middle, Zhen eldest, Qian old. Transformation proceeds from younger to older. Kan becomes Li when a yin is removed below and a yang generated above. Zhen becomes Kun when a yang is removed below and a yin generated above. Qian becomes Dui when a yang is removed below and a yin generated above. The four yin trigrams have arisen from yang, and therefore transform again into the four yang trigrams opposite them.

These two alone obtain Heaven’s and Earth’s central numbers; the others do not. Three-by-three is the Luo Writing’s number, and seven-by-seven the stalk number. The Luo Writing’s number has 5 at the center, while its four sides form four right-triangle number relationships, with the central 5 as hypotenuse. The stalk number has 25 at the center, while its four sides contain four right-triangle areas, with 25 as the area on the hypotenuse. Thus the hexagram number eight agrees with the River Chart’s four: both have their root in leaving 5 and 10 vacant. The stalk number seven agrees with the Luo Writing’s three: both have their root in employing the central 5. Such is the interweaving of the sage’s work arising from his heart with Heaven and Earth’s natural pattern.[^j21-three-by-three]

## The Great Expansion: Anticipating the Sun by Reckoning with Stalks

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4718:232 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4718:233 -->

The histories say that the Yellow Emperor anticipated the sun by reckoning with counters. These counters were, it seems, the numinous yarrow stalks. He developed the stalk numbers to observe the sun and moon; hence “anticipating the sun by reckoning with stalks.”[^j21-calendar-scope]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4718:233 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4718:234 -->

In later ages, those discussing hexagram strokes often matched them with calendrical methods, yet Confucius never spoke of this. Only of the Great Expansion’s numbers did he say “to image the four seasons,” “to image intercalation,” and “corresponding to the days of the year.” Thus the stalk numbers and calendrical methods are manifestly inward and outward aspects of one another. Yet some correspondences resemble one another in principle, while others agree closely in calculation. Those resembling one another in principle are what Confucius sets out in the Great Commentary. Arrange forty-nine counters as a square: considered through the right-triangle numbers, the single remainder lies in the middle as the center; considered through square extraction, it lies at the edge as the corner. Because it lies at the center, dividing in two images Heaven and Earth, while hanging one images the human being as Heaven and Earth’s heart. Because it lies at the corner, dividing in two images the two qi, while hanging one images intercalation as the year’s remainder. The Great Commentary’s “hang one to image the Three” refers to this single remaining stalk; its “return the remainder to the fingers to image intercalation” likewise refers to this stalk. Yet at the first division in two, hanging it merely images the solar surplus. Only after each counting, when the remaining stalks are lodged and the hung stalk is brought together with them, do they jointly image intercalation: solar surplus and lunar shortfall combined as one. This is a broad resemblance in principle, and is Confucius’s explanation. As for calculation, after the Yellow Emperor’s method was lost, the Tang monk Yixing first named a calendar Great Expansion and used stalk numbers to establish its calculations of the year’s fraction and intercalary remainder. Yet examination of the calendrical treatise in the History of the Tang shows that his method did not agree closely enough. We now take Confucius’s words as the governing principle, combine Yixing’s numbers with Kangjie’s reasoning, and follow the system of Zhuanxu and the Zhou Gnomon, to outline a method by which a thousand years may be reckoned without leaving one’s seat. The table below makes it clear.[^j21-hung-stalk]

Reckoning from the counted and lodged stalks of the first processing round, the forty-eight counted stalks correspond to the integral number of forty-eight quarters; the one hung stalk corresponds to the solar surplus of 5¼ days. The twelve lodged stalks correspond to the actual measure of twelve lunations; the one hung stalk corresponds to the lunar shortfall of ten days and 827 parts. In the quarter-day calendar, each day has 940 parts. Thus a year’s solar surplus is five days and 235 parts, while its lunar shortfall—including the solar surplus in the total—is ten days and 827 parts. Each quarter is seven days and 470 parts; seven tenths of it under the solar divisor gives five days and 235 parts. Each lunation is twenty-nine days and 499 parts; seven nineteenth-parts under the lunar divisor gives ten days and 827 parts. The moon moves each day through twelve degrees and seven nineteenth-parts of a degree; hence nineteen is taken as the divisor. The solar and lunar divisors differ, yet the remainder’s numerator is seven in both. Hence, in the Han scholars’ hexagram-qi system, each hexagram governs six days with seven parts remaining. Here “six days and seven parts” uses eighty parts to the day. Multiply the year’s 365¼ days by four and divide by three: the result is 487, called the annual counter-number. Six days under the eighty-part divisor give 480 parts, with seven further parts: the root of the annual counter-number. Sixty hexagrams govern 360 days; their accumulated fractional remainders are 420 parts. Dividing by the day’s divisor gives 5¼ days. This is the source of the method by which calendars, ancient and modern, contain seven intercalary months within one cycle.[^j21-nineteen][^j21-sixty-hexagrams]

But how do the stalk numbers show that the one hung stalk is the reckoning of a remainder of seven? This too is known from the method that generates the stalk system. Eight times eight, the hexagram number, is the number of structure; seven times seven, the stalk number, is the number of functioning. The stalks employ seven, and the one hung stalk is functioning within functioning: its fractional numerator therefore also stops at seven. All this combines Yixing’s calendar and Kangjie’s explanation. Yet Yixing takes the quarter as an actual lunar quarter, shorter than 7½ days, and the hung stalk as the actual intercalary remainder, exceeding one quarter. We therefore take the quarter as the regular measure of 7½ days, and the hung stalk as the surplus fraction of 5¼ days. Only after the remainders have been lodged does the hung stalk returned with them correspond to ten days and 827 parts, becoming the year’s actual intercalary remainder. This seems to accord more closely with the Great Commentary’s sequence.

Thus the Canon of Yao, when first establishing the four seasons, speaks of the equal day, the longest day, and the shortest day: this takes the sun as master. At the end it says, “By intercalary months, fix the four seasons and complete the year”: this takes the moon as master. The 360 regular stalks correspond to one annual cycle’s days: the sun makes one circuit of Heaven to complete the year, and therefore governs them. ## Diagram of Qian’s and Kun’s Stalk Totals

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4718:258 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4718:259 -->

![The Qian and Kun stalk totals: horizon and twilight](../assets/eee-learning/c225bf9aed65-chimon36.png) *The Qian and Kun stalk totals: horizon and twilight.* Divide the degrees of the celestial circuit in two at the horizon, giving 180 degrees to each half. At sunrise and sunset, dawn and dusk each contribute eighteen degrees of dim light, thirty-six in all. Add these to the 180 degrees of daylight: the total is 216, Qian’s stalk number. Subtract them from the 180 degrees assigned to the night watches: 144 remain, Kun’s stalk number.[^j21-twilight]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4718:259 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4718:260 -->

The Great Commentary says, “Qian’s and Kun’s stalks together make 360, corresponding to the days of the year.” The two halves of 180 are therefore the structural numbers of cold and heat, day and night proceeding together. Yet yang generates and yin kills; yang is bright and yin dark. Thus yang has abundance and yin deficiency, yang fullness and yin emptiness. Reason from the equal division of day and night, and their natural numbers are as shown. For the year’s seasons of cold and heat, Shao’s explanation says that things open at the end of Yin: this likewise begins eighteen days early. They close at the beginning of Xu: this likewise ends eighteen days late. Hence, of the 11,520 that number the myriad things, 6,912 follow yang and 4,608 follow yin. When the generative qi remains abundant, the year is fruitful; when the good remain numerous, the age is well governed. The principle of waning and waxing, fullness and emptiness, is no more than this.

## Diagram of Transformations by Doubling

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4718:261 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4718:262 -->

![Successive doubling and the triangle of coefficients](../assets/eee-learning/8279b807aa13-chimon37.png) *Successive doubling and the triangle of coefficients.*[^j21-binomial]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4718:262 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4718:263 -->

This diagram uses the method of doubling. For example, the second row’s two ones generate the 2 at the center of the third row; with the two outer ones, the total is 4: two is doubled to four. The third row’s 1 and 2 each generate a central 3 in the fourth row; with the two outer ones, the total is 8: four is doubled to eight. The rest follow this pattern. In mathematics this is called finding the intermediate coefficients for root extraction. The left-hand 1 is the main square or cube portion, the right-hand 1 the corner portion, and the numbers between them the intermediate coefficients. The third row is for the square, the fourth for the cube, and the fifth, sixth, and seventh for the fourth, fifth, and sixth powers. This also resembles the principle of forming hexagrams. Why? Yang is large and yin small: yang resembles the main portion, yin the corner portion, occupying the two ends. Their joining generates the two intermediate images, just as the main and corner portions of a square join to generate two side rectangles, each as long as the main portion and as wide as the corner portion. Multiplying again generates the six intermediate trigrams, just as the main and corner portions of a cube join to generate six intermediate pieces: three flat pieces rooted in the main portion, with the corner portion’s thickness, and three elongated pieces rooted in the corner portion, with the main portion’s length.[^j21-cubic-correction]

Thus, however indefinitely the powers multiply in root extraction, the calculation always rests on the main and corner portions. However indefinitely the figures multiply in forming hexagrams, their structure always rests on yin and yang. At the beginning of hexagram formation there is simply one yin and one yang, repeatedly added to one another. When the completed figures are analyzed, from one stroke through six only pure yin and pure yang remain unchanged. At the Four Images, the intermediate combination of one yin and one yang occurs twice. At the Eight Trigrams, one yin and two yang occur three times, and one yang and two yin three times. At four strokes, one yin and three yang occur four times, one yang and three yin four times, and two yin and two yang six times. At five strokes, one yin and four yang occur five times, one yang and four yin five times, two yin and three yang ten times, and two yang and three yin ten times. When the six strokes are complete, one yin and five yang occur six times, one yang and five yin six times, two yin and four yang fifteen times, two yang and four yin fifteen times, and three yin and three yang twenty times.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4718:263 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4718:264 -->

Zhu Xi’s diagram of hexagram transformation is established from this. Its doubling method is the same as that of drawing the hexagrams; the numerical counts of the interwoven combinations are what hexagram transformation employs.

The Zhou Rites’ east/west statements and the Zhuangzi’s “Great Clod” are appeals made by the source; the English does not substitute a modern explanation. “Ascending to present accomplishment to Heaven” renders 升中於天, a ritual expression, not a literal ascent into the sky.

[^j21-earth-directions]: 丑 and 未 are Chou and Wei, the second and eighth Earthly Branches. Here they mark the northeast and southwest seasonal/positional transitions associated with Gen and Kun. 四季 can name the closing earth-associated periods of the four seasons, not merely four unrelated calendar quarters. The text’s pairing of trigrams with five phases is retained even though the compilers questioned five-phase derivations elsewhere.

[^j21-three-sons]: In the Earlier Heaven/Luo explanation the base reads 乾生二陽, “Qian generates two yang.” The parallel transcription reads 乾生三陽. The English adopts three: Zhen, Kan, and Gen are all explicitly named. These are the three male trigrams, not three yang lines in each figure.

[^j21-luo-exchange]: The preliminary linear assignments are 9 Qian, 8 Zhen, 7 Kan, 6 Gen and 1 Kun, 2 Xun, 3 Li, 4 Dui for Earlier Heaven; 9 Li, 8 Gen, 7 Dui, 6 Qian and 1 Kan, 2 Kun, 3 Zhen, 4 Xun for Later Heaven. The following paragraph exchanges the 2 and 8 positions. Thus the text is describing a construction of the received arrangements, not assigning both 2 and 8 simultaneously to the same position.

[^j21-reversal-counts]: Reversal means turning the entire six-line figure upside down. The eight reversal-invariant hexagrams are 1,2,27,28,29,30,61,62. Of the other 56, twelve have two component trigrams that retain their individual forms under reversal, twelve have two that change form, and thirty-two have one of each. The trigrams exchange upper/lower places even when their forms remain unchanged. “Opposite” in the Earlier Heaven pairing is not the same operation as reversal.

[^j21-order-not-history]: “Root of the Sequence” and “root of the Miscellaneous Hexagrams” state structural explanations offered by the compilers. The combinatorial counts and individual shifts can be checked independently, but do not by themselves prove a historical derivation of either Wing. The base’s 一一陽 in the next discussion is a duplicated 一; the parallel transcription has 一陽.

[^j21-shift]: In the sixteen short instructions, “remove below” removes the lowest line, shifts the remaining two down, and generates a new top line; “remove above” does the converse. This is a shift-and-append operation, not merely changing the polarity of one line in place. The Chinese trigram signs are retained alongside their English names.

[^j21-overlap]: 互卦 here concerns overlapping three-line components drawn from four consecutive lines. There are sixteen such four-line configurations. The compilers identify these with the root of the Miscellaneous Hexagrams; that identification is their interpretive claim, not the title’s ordinary lexical definition. The original repeated demonstrations are retained in full. The ensuing same-polarity/opposite-polarity distinctions use the Later Heaven family classification. In each of the two classes, “eight” counts distinct resulting hexagrams; the prose repeats them from the perspectives of different constituent trigrams.

[^j21-circle-ratio]: The circle calculation assumes circumference/diameter = 22/7, a traditional approximation, not an exact value of π. On that assumption, a diameter of 7 gives circumference 22 and a circumscribing square perimeter of 28; both the perimeter ratio and square-to-circle area ratio reduce to 14:11. The perimeter sum of 50 therefore depends on this approximation. “Only” concerns the source’s privileged small construction and cannot be taken as a general uniqueness theorem for circles and squares.

[^j21-seven-fifty]: The triangle identity 3² + 4² = 5² is exact, and 9 + 16 + 25 = 50. The source’s statement that only 3 and 4 yield an integral hypotenuse is not true as an unrestricted claim: scaled triples alone provide counterexamples. Its intended setting is the selected elementary construction. “Extracting the square” of 50 here takes the largest integral square, 49 = 7², leaving 1; it does not assert that √50 is exactly 7. The 7-by-7 figure contains eight triangles of area 6 and a central unit, so 8 × 6 + 1 = 49.

[^j21-old-yang-repair]: The base’s 一六五互乘 is corrected from the parallel reading 一六互乘: the two cross-products are 1 × 6 and 6 × 1, totaling 12, not a multiplication involving 5. Together with the unit corner they make 13, while the 6-by-6 square makes 36.

[^j21-repeated-caption]: The base places “Old yin’s numbers agree with the right-triangle method; the complete square is forty-nine” immediately before repeating the heading and area statement. This repeated source caption is retained and labeled, rather than silently deleted.

[^j21-old-yin-areas]: Here 積六 is the triangle’s area, 3 × 4 / 2 = 6, whereas “hypotenuse 5, area 25” means the square on the hypotenuse. Four triangles contribute 24; the central square contributes 25. That central square itself contains four more such triangles and a unit square, since (4 − 3)² = 1. These area distinctions are essential to following the argument.

[^j21-three-by-three]: The base’s 三二者《洛書》之數 is corrected to the parallel 三三者: three-by-three is the Luo Writing’s number. The following relation of eight to four is explained by leaving the central 5 and 10 out of the River Chart, leaving eight numbers in four directional pairs. “Five and ten” must not be read as the integer fifty there.

[^j21-calendar-scope]: The calculations use a stipulated four-quarter-day year of 365¼ days and a mean lunation of 29 + 499/940 days. They are exact relations within that historical model, not a claim to present-day astronomical accuracy. 策 can mean stalks or calculating counters; the compilers identify the Yellow Emperor’s “reckoning with counters” with numinous yarrow stalks. Their reconstruction of the lost method is expressly their own.

[^j21-hung-stalk]: The source distinguishes the one stalk permanently left unused from the one hung during a processing round. It further assigns more than one symbolic role to the latter. In the calendar analogy, the “twelve lodged stalks” combines the alternative normalized first-round remainders 4 and 8; it is not a claim that a single first round physically yields twelve. The explanations retain their theoretical, not merely instructional, character.

[^j21-decimal]: 四十八零七分 is 48.7: 36,525 / 25 = 1,461, and 1,461 / 30 = 48.7. It is not 48 + 7/30 or 48.07. “Solar number” and “lunar number” are rescaled quantities in this construction. Four parts per annual day give 1,461 quarter-day units; dividing by the nominal thirty-day month yields 48.7 quarter-month units.

[^j21-quarters]: A quarter here is the schematic quarter of a thirty-day month, 7½ days, not the actual variable interval between consecutive lunar phases. Forty-eight such quarters make 360 days; seven tenths of a quarter is 5¼ days. This qualification is part of the compilers’ later criticism of Yixing and is retained there.

[^j21-lunation]: Twelve lunations of 29 + 499/940 days total 354 + 348/940 days. Subtracting this from 365¼ gives 10 + 827/940 days. This text calls that entire solar–lunar difference the lunar shortfall, explicitly including the solar surplus above 360; the deficit relative to 360 alone is smaller. Seven nineteenth-parts of one stipulated lunation give exactly the same 10 + 827/940 days.

[^j21-nineteen]: The base punctuates 十，九 in the lunar-motion parenthesis; the parallel transcription also breaks the words incorrectly, leaving 九 outside the parenthesis. Historical page image 2225 reads 以十九為法: “nineteen is taken as the divisor.” The English restores that reading. The same note corrects the base’s 六門 to 六日 in “six days and seven parts,” supported by the image and parallel transcription.

[^j21-sixty-hexagrams]: The calendrical example uses sixty assigned hexagrams, not all sixty-four: 60 × (6 + 7/80) = 365¼. The factor 80 is the day’s stated denominator. The preceding arithmetic 365¼ × 4 / 3 = 487 and 60 × 7/80 = 5¼ is also exact. These equalities do not establish an empirical causal connection between stalk outcomes and celestial motion.

[^j21-epoch]: 章 is a nineteen-year calendar cycle. Its 228 months here are the twelve regular months per year, 19 × 12, before adding seven intercalary months; there are 235 actual lunations in the full stipulated cycle. The numbers 216 + 144 = 360 and 78 + 150 = 228 are exact stalk totals. “Each combined total is sixty” refers to opposite line types, 36 + 24 and 28 + 32; their corresponding full set-aside totals are 13 + 25 and 17 + 21, each 38.

[^j21-intercalation-limit]: The thirty-two-month and thirty-two-year statements are schematic, not exact consequences of the nineteen-year/seven-intercalation model. At thirty days per month and twelve double-hours per day, 32 × 30 × 12 = 11,520 double-hours; at a schematic 360 days per year, 32 × 360 = 11,520 days. The exact nineteen-year model gives a mean interval of 228/7 regular months per intercalation, not precisely 32. 辰 here is rendered double-hour to make the first total intelligible.

[^j21-twilight]: The diagram labels the horizon (地平線) and eighteen-degree twilight portions at both ends. It assigns 180 + 36 = 216 to light and 180 − 36 = 144 to darkness. This is a schematic correspondence, not a universal duration of day, twilight, or growing season: a solar altitude angle cannot in general be identified with an equal duration or seasonal interval without further geometric assumptions. The eighteen-day analogy is the source’s attribution to Shao Yong. Yin (寅) and Xu (戌) here are the third and eleventh Earthly Branches; the branch name Yin is not the yin of 陰陽.

[^j21-binomial]: The last diagram contains rows 1; 1,1; 1,2,1; 1,3,3,1; 1,4,6,4,1; 1,5,10,10,5,1; 1,6,15,20,15,6,1. Each interior entry is the sum of the two above; the row totals are 1,2,4,8,16,32,64. 開方求廉率 concerns the coefficients used in traditional root extraction. 方 is the large square/cube portion, 隅 the small corner portion, and 廉 the intermediate rectangular/prismatic pieces. The traditional labels 三乘方, 四乘方, 五乘方 here denote fourth, fifth, and sixth powers, not modern third, fourth, and fifth powers.

[^j21-cubic-correction]: The base reads 二長廉, “two elongated pieces”; historical page image 2237 also has 二. The parallel Siku transcription reads 三長㢘, “three elongated pieces,” which the English adopts. Three flat pieces and three elongated pieces give the six intermediate pieces just stated, as in (a + b)³ = a³ + 3a²b + 3ab² + b³. This is a reading attested in the parallel transcription and supported by the geometry, but contradicted by the inspected image—not a facsimile-confirmed correction. The base’s 伺則 is corrected to 何則 (“why?”), supported by page image 2236 and the parallel transcription.
