## Guiding Principles, Part One

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4538:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4538:002 -->

**Source description:** On the origins and transmission of the Changes.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4538:002 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4538:003 -->

**Rites of Zhou.** The Grand Diviner administers the methods of the Three Changes: the first is Linking Mountains, the second Returning to the Storehouse, and the third the Zhou Changes. Each has eight fundamental trigrams and sixty-four differentiated hexagrams.[^p-transmission]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4538:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4538:004 -->

**Lu Deming.** When Fuxi ruled the world, he looked upward at the patterns of Heaven and downward at the configurations of Earth. He observed the markings of birds and beasts and what was appropriate to the land, taking images nearby from his own body and farther away from other things. He first drew the eight trigrams, then doubled them to make sixty-four hexagrams. Imprisoned at Youli, King Wen composed the hexagram statements; the Duke of Zhou composed the line statements. Confucius composed the Ten Wings: the Judgments, Images, Wenyan, Appended Statements, Discussion of the Trigrams, Sequence of the Hexagrams, and Miscellaneous Hexagrams. Ban Gu says that Confucius loved the Changes late in life and read it until the leather bindings broke three times, composing a commentary upon it. That “commentary” is the Ten Wings. Shang Qu of Lu, styled Zimu, received the Changes from Confucius and taught it to Qiao Bi of Lu, styled Ziyong. Ziyong taught Han Bi of Jiangdong (馯臂), styled Zigong; Zigong taught Zhou Chou of Yan, styled Zijia; Zijia taught Sun Yu of Dongwu, styled Zicheng; and Zicheng taught Tian He of Qi, styled Zizhuang. When Qin burned the books, the Changes alone was not prohibited because it was a work of divination. Its transmission therefore continued unbroken. **Source parenthesis:** The Sui History says that when Qin burned the books, the Zhou Changes alone survived through its use in divination. Only three chapters of the Discussion of the Trigrams were lost; a woman of Henei later recovered them.[^p-three-lost]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4538:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4538:005 -->

**Lu Deming, continued.** When the Han arose, Tian He moved from Qi to Duling with the Tian clan and was called Master Tian of Du. He taught Wang Tong of Dongwu, styled Zizhong, Zhou Wangsun of Luoyang, Ding Kuan of Liang, and Master Fu of Qi; all wrote commentaries on the Changes. Early Han learning in the Changes had its source in Master Tian. Tong taught Yang He of Zichuan. Kuan taught Tian Wangsun of Dang, in the same commandery. Wangsun taught Shi Chou, Meng Xi, and Liangqiu He; from this arose the Shi, Meng, and Liangqiu schools.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4538:005 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4538:006 -->

**Lu Deming, continued.** Shi Chou transmitted the Changes to Zhang Yu and Lu Bo of Langya. Yu taught Peng Xuan of Huaiyang and Dai Chong of Pei. Bo taught Tun Moru of Taishan (屯莫如) and Bing Dan of Langya. In the Later Han, Liu Kun received the Shi school’s Changes from Dai Bin of Pei. His son was Yi.[^p-liu-yi]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4538:006 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4538:007 -->

**Lu Deming, continued.** Meng Xi’s father, Meng Qing, was accomplished in the Rites and the Spring and Autumn Annals. Finding the ritual classics numerous and the Annals complex, Meng Qing sent Xi to receive the Changes from Tian Wangsun. Xi wrote an exposition by chapters and sentences and taught Bai Guang of his own commandery and Zhai Mu of Pei. In the Later Han, Wa Dan, Huayang Hong (觟陽鴻), and Ren An all transmitted the Meng school’s Changes.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4538:007 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4538:008 -->

**Lu Deming, continued.** Liangqiu He first received the Changes from Jing Fang, a Grand Palace Grandee, then studied with Tian Wangsun. He transmitted it to his son Lin, who taught Wulu Chongzong and Wang Jun of Langya. Chongzong taught Shisun Zhang of Pingling, Deng Pengzu of Pei, and Heng Xian of Qi. In the Later Han, Fan Sheng transmitted the Liangqiu Changes to Yang Zheng of Jingzhao. Zhang Xing of Yingchuan also taught that tradition; his registered disciples numbered nearly ten thousand, and his son Fang continued his work.[^p-two-jings]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4538:008 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4538:009 -->

**Lu Deming, continued.** Jing Fang received the Changes from Jiao Yanshou of Liang. Yanshou said he had once questioned Meng Xi about the Changes, so Fang regarded Yanshou’s teaching as the Meng school. Zhai Mu and Master Bai refused to recognize it, saying it was not. Fang wrote an exposition by chapters and sentences, distinguished particularly by its treatment of portents and calamities. He taught Duan Jia of Donghai, Yao Ping of Hedong, and Cheng Hong of Henan, all of whom served as court gentlemen or academicians. The Jing school consequently had many adherents in the Former Han. Dai Feng, Sun Qi, and Wei Man all transmitted it in the Later Han.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4538:009 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4538:010 -->

**Lu Deming, continued.** Fei Zhi transmitted the Changes to Wang Huang of Langya, establishing the Fei school. Its basis was a text in ancient characters, called the ancient-script Changes. It had no exposition by chapters and sentences, but explained the Upper and Lower Classics solely through the Judgments, Images, Appended Statements, and Wenyan. When Liu Xiang supervised the collation of books under Emperor Cheng of Han, he examined the interpretations of the Changes and concluded that the various schools all descended from Tian He, Yang Shuyuan, and General Ding, and agreed broadly in their main meanings; only the Jing school differed. Xiang also collated the ancient-script Classic of Changes in the palace against the Shi, Meng, and Liangqiu texts, which sometimes omitted “no blame” or “regret vanishes.” Only the Fei text agreed with the ancient-script copy.[^p-ancient-script]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4538:010 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4538:011 -->

**Lu Deming, continued.** Master Fan’s History of the Later Han says that Chen Yuan of Jingzhao, Ma Rong of Fufeng, Zheng Zhong of Henan, Zheng Kangcheng of Beihai, and Xun Shuang of Yingchuan all transmitted the Fei school’s Changes. Gao Xiang of Pei studied the Changes at the same time as Fei Zhi. His teaching likewise had no exposition by chapters and sentences, but concentrated on yin, yang, and portents, claiming descent from General Ding. When it reached Xiang, he taught his son Kang and Mujiang Yong of Lanling, forming the Gao school. The early Han established an academician for the Yang school’s Changes. Emperor Xuan added the Shi, Meng, and Liangqiu schools; Emperor Yuan added the Jing school. The Fei and Gao schools received no official establishment and circulated among the people. In the Later Han, the Fei school flourished and the Gao school consequently declined.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4538:011 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4538:012 -->

**Lu Deming, continued.** During the Yongjia upheaval, the Shi and Liangqiu traditions were lost, and the Meng, Jing, and Fei traditions no longer had transmitters. Only the commentaries of Zheng Kangcheng and Wang Fusi circulated, and Wang’s was particularly esteemed. Wang did not annotate the Appended Statements and the works following them; received practice completed his edition with Han Kangbo’s annotations.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4538:012 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4538:013 -->

**Kong Yingda.** The Appended Statements says, “The River brought forth the Chart, and the Luo brought forth the Writing; the sages took them as models.” Kong Anguo, Ma Rong, Wang Su, Yao Xin, and others therefore all say that Fuxi obtained the River Chart and made the Changes. Yet even after receiving the Chart, Fuxi still had to observe above and examine below, comparing and correcting his understanding before drawing the figures. When he first drew the eight trigrams, the images of the myriad things were already within them. This is what the Appended Statements means by “The eight trigrams stand in their ranks; the images are within them.” Although the images of all things were present, the principles by which things change and adapt were not yet complete. He therefore doubled the eight trigrams. Each hexagram had six lines, and the doubling produced sixty-four hexagrams. This is what the Appended Statements means by “By doubling them, the lines are within them.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4538:013 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4538:014 -->

**Kong Yingda, continued.** Scholars disagree about who doubled the trigrams; there are four accounts. Wang Fusi and others attribute it to Fuxi, Zheng Kangcheng’s followers to Shennong, Sun Sheng to Yu of Xia, and the Grand Historian Sima Qian and others to King Wen. The claims for Yu and King Wen collapse without refutation: examination of the Appended Statements shows that in Shennong’s time there were already references to “taking the idea from Increase” and “from Biting Through.” Yet the attribution to Shennong is also unsatisfactory. We follow Fusi in holding that Fuxi, having drawn the eight trigrams, himself doubled them into sixty-four hexagrams. This reaches the actual situation. The intention behind doubling is fully explained in the Discussion of the Trigrams and need not be set out here.[^p-three-four]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4538:014 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4538:015 -->

**Kong Yingda, continued.** In Fuxi’s time the Way was still plain and unadorned; drawing figures and doubling their lines sufficed to establish a model. Later ages grew superficial and corrupt, and virtue fell short of antiquity. The line images no longer sufficed for instruction, so appended words were composed to make them clear.[^p-appended-word]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4538:015 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4538:016 -->

**Kong Yingda, continued.** The Grand Diviner in the Rites of Zhou has three Changes: Linking Mountains, Returning to the Storehouse, and the Zhou Changes. Du Zichun attributes Linking Mountains to Fuxi and Returning to the Storehouse to the Yellow Emperor. Zheng Kangcheng’s Praise of the Changes and Discourse on the Changes say that the Xia called theirs Linking Mountains, the Yin Returning to the Storehouse, and the Zhou the Zhou Changes. Zheng further explains: Linking Mountains images mountains giving forth clouds in an unbroken succession; Returning to the Storehouse means that everything returns to be stored within it; Zhou Changes means that the Way of change is all-pervading, with nothing left outside it. Yet Zheng cites no textual evidence for this explanation. Earlier scholars used it to develop theories of cultural refinement and native substance, all elaborate and useless; we do not adopt them. According to the Genealogical Records and other books, Shennong was also called Lianshan, or Lieshan, and the Yellow Emperor was also called Guizang. Since Linking Mountains and Returning to the Storehouse were names of ruling houses, “Zhou” in Zhou Changes likewise derives from the place-name in the region south of Mount Qi. It is the Zhou of the Mao Odes’ “The plain of Zhou is rich and broad.” When King Wen developed the Changes, he was at Youli: Zhou’s virtue had not yet risen to power, and it was still the Yin age. The title “Zhou” therefore distinguishes it from Yin. Because this was the Changes elaborated by King Wen, it was called the Zhou Changes, just as the Documents of Zhou and Rites of Zhou bear that designation to distinguish them from other dynasties.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4538:016 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4538:017 -->

**Kong Yingda, continued.** There are two accounts of the words appended to the Zhou Changes. One holds that both hexagram and line statements were composed by King Wen. Its evidence is the Appended Statements: “Did the Changes arise in middle antiquity? Did its maker know sorrow and trouble?” And again: “Did the Changes arise at the end of Yin, when Zhou’s virtue flourished? Was it during the affair of King Wen and Zhou?” Thus the Grand Historian says that King Wen, imprisoned, elaborated the Changes: this is the meaning of “Did its maker know sorrow and trouble?” Zheng’s school follows this account.

The second observes that many line statements refer to events after King Wen. At Pushing Upward’s six in the fourth place, “The king presents offerings at Mount Qi”: only after King Wu conquered Yin was King Wen posthumously entitled king. Had King Wen composed the line statement, it should not have said this. At Darkening of the Light’s six in the fifth place, “Jizi’s darkening of the light”: Jizi was imprisoned and enslaved only after King Wu’s military demonstration. King Wen should not have spoken of this beforehand. Moreover, when Han Xuanzi visited Lu, the Zuo Tradition says that he saw the Images of the Changes and declared, “Now I understand the Duke of Zhou’s virtue.” The Duke too suffered slander and could therefore be described as knowing sorrow and trouble. Examining these accounts, we assign the hexagram statements to King Wen and the line statements to the Duke of Zhou. Ma Rong, Lu Ji, and others agree, and we follow them. The Duke of Zhou is not separately counted in the expression “three sages” because the father’s work includes that of the son. The line statements presumably also express King Wen’s original intention; hence only King Wen is named.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4538:017 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4538:018 -->

**Kong Yingda, continued.** Earlier scholars have no alternative account to the attribution of the Ten Wings—the Judgments, Images, and the rest—to Confucius. There are, however, several ways of counting ten. Since King Wen’s Classic of Changes was originally divided into Upper and Lower Parts, their domains are distinct; the Judgments and Images explaining the hexagrams should accordingly follow that division. One enumeration therefore counts: Upper Judgments, first; Lower Judgments, second; Upper Images, third; Lower Images, fourth; Upper Appended Statements, fifth; Lower Appended Statements, sixth; Wenyan, seventh; Discussion of the Trigrams, eighth; Sequence of the Hexagrams, ninth; Miscellaneous Hexagrams, tenth. Zheng’s school follows this enumeration, as do we.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4538:018 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4538:019 -->

**Chao Shuozhi.** The Han Bibliographical Treatise lists the Classic of Changes in twelve parts, in the Shi, Meng, and Liangqiu traditions. Yan Shigu explains: the Upper and Lower Classics together with the Ten Wings make twelve. Thus the Judgments, Images, Wenyan, and Appended Statements first became attached to the hexagrams and lines in their Han transmission. This accords with earlier scholars’ statement that Fei Zhi explained the lines solely by reference to the Judgments, Images, and Wenyan: the intermingling of those works with the hexagrams began with the Fei school. Initially, Fei’s teaching was not established in the official schools and circulated only among the people. By the end of the Han, Chen Yuan, Zheng Kangcheng, and their followers studied Fei’s tradition, and the ancient twelve-part Changes disappeared.[^p-layout-history]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4538:019 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4538:020 -->

**Chao Shuozhi, continued.** Kong Yingda further says that Fusi considered the Images explanations of the classic and thought they should be placed close to it; he separated each line’s Image statement and attached it to that line. Thus, when Fei’s school first disturbed the ancient arrangement, it was still like the present Qian, where Judgments and Images are attached after the hexagram. The ancient classic began to be altered by Fei and ended in complete disorder under Wang Bi. What a pity! Why did later scholars go on imitating them? Du Yu divided the Zuo Tradition among the entries of the Annals; Song Zhong, Fan Wang, and others scattered the Praises and Assessments of the Supreme Mystery under its eighty-one Heads. These are plain parallels. Examining the original arrangement, one finds it like the prefatory accounts to the ancient-script Documents, to Sima Qian and Ban Gu’s histories, or to Yang Xiong’s Model Sayings. The current popular edition of Model Sayings places each prefatory account at the head of its chapter, unlike the official-school edition: the general process is evident. In the Tang, Li Dingzuo also placed passages of the Sequence at the heads of the hexagrams, again imitating young Wang’s error. Liu Mu says that only Qian’s Small Images are not attached to the line statements, out of respect for the ruler. Shi Shoudao likewise says that Confucius put the Judgments and Great Images before the six lines and the Small Images beneath the individual lines, but placed all of Qian’s afterward as a gesture of deference. Alas! What more can one ask of other people?

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4538:020 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4538:021 -->

**Zhu Xi, in conversation.** A disciple asked: “When Fuxi first drew the eight trigrams, did King Wen later double them into sixty-four hexagrams, or had Fuxi already drawn them? Looking at the Earlier Heaven diagram, one sees that once there are eight trigrams there are sixty-four hexagrams. I suspect that Fuxi had already drawn them.” He replied: “The Rites of Zhou says that the Three Changes each have eight fundamental figures and sixty-four differentiated ones. That shows they were not drawn gradually by King Wen.” The disciple asked further: “Were the names of the sixty-four hexagrams also present in Fuxi’s time, or were they established by King Wen?” He replied: “That cannot be determined.” Zishan asked: “From the account of the thirteen hexagrams, it seems they already existed under Fuxi.” He replied: “When that account says ‘presumably taking the idea from Clinging’ or ‘from Increase,’ it means that knotted cords made into nets have Clinging’s image—not that people first observed Clinging and only then made nets.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4538:021 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4538:022 -->

**Zhu Xi, continued.** The ancient-script Zhou Changes, in twelve parts of classic and commentary, was established by Lü Zuqian of Donglai, styled Bogong. Its one part of Pronunciations and Glosses was taken down by his disciple Wang Shensou of Jinhua. I have always held that the Classic of Changes was originally made for divination, giving instruction and warning through good and ill fortune. Its language is therefore concise, but its compass exceedingly broad. In composing his commentaries, the Master likewise brought out only one aspect at a time to show the governing examples. Once scholars divided the classic and interspersed the commentaries, however, readers followed the words immediately before them in seeking meaning. Often, before giving their minds to the whole classic, they seized on one aspect of a commentary as a settled interpretation. Each hexagram and line consequently came to represent only one affair. The use of the Changes was thereby restricted, unable to reach the world’s manifold circumstances. This has troubled me. Repeated reading of Bogong’s work brought fresh understanding—not merely because its chapter arrangement approaches antiquity.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4538:022 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4538:023 -->

**Lü Zuqian.** When the Han arose, six schools expounded the Changes. Only the Fei school transmitted the ancient-script Changes, and it was not established in the official schools. Liu Xiang compared the ancient-script palace text with the Shi, Meng, and Liangqiu copies, which sometimes lacked “no blame” or “regret vanishes”; only Fei’s text agreed with it. It was therefore truly the surviving book of Confucius. In the Eastern Han, Ma Rong and Zheng Kangcheng studied the Fei school, and its text first became widely current. The Wang Bi Changes now established in the official schools, although its interpretation follows Zhuangzi and Laozi, is in its underlying text Zheng’s book. Among the Han schools, Fei’s was closest to antiquity and most rejected; after a thousand years it alone stands intact. Is that not Heaven’s doing? Since Kangcheng and Fusi joined the Judgments, Images, and Wenyan to the classic, readers no longer saw the old arrangement. Recently Master Chao of Songshan compiled the Ancient Zhou Changes, intending to restore it. Yet readers found some of his deletions, additions, separations, and combinations unsettling. I have respectfully used Chao’s book, compared historical accounts, and reestablished twelve parts, deciding their titles and divisions entirely by ancient precedent.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4538:023 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4538:024 -->

**Lü Zuqian, continued.** King Wen’s words beneath each hexagram are called its Judgment; Confucius merely set out their meaning, and therefore entitled his work Judgments. Were King Wen’s hexagram statements not called Judgments, why would Confucius say, “Those with understanding need only observe the Judgment statements, and more than half their thinking is done”? Words beneath the lines are called Images. Since many line statements concern events after King Wen, the various accounts attribute them to the Duke of Zhou. The Great Image is the drawn figure itself: by observing its strokes one sees Heaven, Earth, water, fire, thunder, wind, mountain, and lake. Great Image statements include “Heaven’s movement is vigorous; thus the noble person strengthens himself without ceasing.” The Small Images explain the Duke of Zhou’s words, as in “‘Hidden dragon. Do not act’: yang is below.” These are all commentaries on the images. The classic was composed by King Wen and the Duke of Zhou; the commentaries by Confucius. In his Essential Purport of the Six Classics, Sima Tan quotes “The world returns to one destination by different paths, reaching one end through a hundred deliberations,” calling it the Great Commentary on the Changes. Ban Gu says that Confucius studied the Changes late in life, read it until the leather bindings broke three times, and wrote its commentaries: these are the Ten Wings. In the Former Han, the six classics and their commentaries all circulated separately. Only when Later Han scholars wrote annotations were classic and commentary joined. The Duke of Gaogui District of Wei asked Academician Chunyu Jun: “The Judgments and Images do not adjoin the classic, yet the annotated edition joins them. Why?” Jun replied: “Zheng Kangcheng joined them to make the text easier for students to consult. Confucius did not join them because he feared that his words would be confused with King Wen’s.” Thus, before Zheng annotated the classics, the Judgments and Images did not adjoin the text. After Zheng joined them, “The Judgment says” and “The Image says” were added to distinguish them, throughout the hexagrams.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4538:024 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4538:025 -->

**Shui Yuquan.** In the renxu year of Yuanfeng, Duke Lü of Ji first had the ancient twelve-part Zhou Changes engraved at the official school of Chengdu. In the xinsi year of Jianzhong Jingguo, Master Chao Jingyu combined it into eight parts, entitled it Ancient Zhou Changes, and kept a fair copy at home. In the xinwei year of Shaoxing, Li Wenjian of Xunyan said that northern learning had distinct lines of transmission; he followed Lü in the work’s title and Chao in its chapter divisions, and had it engraved again. In the renyin year of Chunxi, Zhu Wengong of Xin'an brought forward Lü Chenggong of Donglai’s Ancient-Script Zhou Changes: Classic, Commentaries, Pronunciations, and Glosses, yet said that editing the ancient Changes began with Master Chao. Had those two gentlemen perhaps not seen Duke Lü’s Sichuan edition? Nevertheless, Lü Chenggong criticized Chao’s combining the Upper and Lower Classics, while Zhu Wengong’s Original Meaning agrees in its divisions with Duke Lü’s edition.[^p-calendar-repair]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4538:025 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4538:026 -->

**Wang Yinglin.** The Explanation of the Text of the Discussion of the Trigrams quotes Xun Shuang’s Collected Explanations of Nine Schools and preserves thirty-one additional trigram images. The Sui and Tang bibliographies list that work in ten juan. Only the prefatory catalogue of the Explanation of the Text names the nine scholars, saying that its compiler is unknown and that Xun Shuang is named because he is its principal authority. Its preface lists Xun Shuang, Jing Fang, Ma Rong, Zheng Kangcheng, Song Zhong, Yu Fan, Lu Ji, Yao Xin, and Zhai Zixuan as authors of explanations of the Changes. The annotations also cite Masters Zhang and Zhu, whose identities are unknown. Xun Yue’s Annals of Han says that Ma Rong’s Explanation of the Changes introduced quite a few unusual views, whereas Shuang’s Commentary based itself on line images, supporting and corresponding relations, and the transformations of yin and yang, explaining the classic through the Ten Wings. Scholars of the Changes in Yan and Yu consequently transmitted Xun’s learning. His explanations can now be found in Li Dingzuo’s Collected Explanations.

## Guiding Principles, Part Two

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4539:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4539:002 -->

**Source description:** On the subtle riches of the Way of the Changes and the interpretive principles of the classic and commentaries.[^p-heading-repair]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4539:002 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4539:003 -->

**Sima Qian.** The Changes begins from what is hidden and proceeds to what is manifest; the Spring and Autumn Annals infers the hidden from what is visible.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4539:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4539:004 -->

**Ban Gu.** Among the writings of the Six Arts, Music harmonizes the spirit; the Odes rectify speech; the Rites clarify proper forms; the Documents broaden what one hears; and the Spring and Autumn Annals adjudicates affairs. These five express the Way of the Five Constants, completing one another through mutual dependence; the Changes is their source. Hence the saying, “If the Changes could no longer be seen, Qian and Kun would perhaps almost cease.” This means that it shares the beginnings and endings of Heaven and Earth.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4539:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4539:005 -->

**Wang Bi.** What is a Judgment? It discusses the constitution of an entire hexagram and makes clear the governing element from which it arises. Though six lines interweave, one may be brought forward to illuminate them; though firm and yielding ride upon one another, a governing element may be established to settle their order. Seek them from what unifies them: however numerous the things, one knows they may be governed by holding to the one. View them from their root: however extensive the meanings, one knows they may be gathered under a single name. Thus, once a hexagram’s name is brought forward, its meaning has a governing focus. “Observe its Judgment statement, and more than half the thinking is done.” When a hexagram has five yang lines and one yin, that yin governs it; with five yin and one yang, that yang governs it. Yin seeks yang, and yang seeks yin. If there is only one yang, how could the five yin not return to it together? If there is only one yin, how could the five yang not follow it together? Thus even a yin line, lowly though it is, governs a hexagram when it occupies the position of the fewest. Sometimes the lines are set aside and the two trigram bodies brought forward, because the hexagram’s constitution does not derive from a particular line. Complexity without fear of disorder, change without fear of confusion; breadth preserved through concision, multiplicity brought to completion through simplicity—is this not the work of the Judgment?

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4539:005 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4539:006 -->

**Wang Bi, continued.** What is a line? It speaks of change. What is change? What genuine feeling and artifice bring about. Therefore “genuine feeling and artifice act upon one another”; the far and near pursue one another, love and hatred contend, contraction and extension impel one another. Without the utmost capacity for change, what could take part in this? Thus the hexagram preserves the situation, and the lines show its changes.[^p-time-position]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4539:006 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4539:007 -->

**Wang Bi, continued.** A hexagram is a situation; its lines are the changes appropriate to that situation. Times bring Obstruction or Peace, so conduct calls for going forth or withdrawing. Hexagrams have lesser and greater elements, so their words speak of danger or ease. The rule appropriate to one time may be reversed in its application; what is fortunate at one time may become unfortunate at another. Thus hexagrams stand in reversed opposition, and the lines likewise change. Follow their names to observe good and ill fortune; take up their situations to observe movement and stillness. The changes within one constitution become visible in this way. Correspondence images a shared purpose; position images where a line stands. Supporting and riding above image compliance or opposition; distance and nearness, danger or ease; inner and outer, retirement or public activity; first and top, beginning and ending. Therefore, to observe change, attend to correspondence; to examine safety and danger, attend to position; to distinguish compliance and opposition, attend to supporting and riding; to understand public activity and retirement, attend to outer and inner. Near and far, ending and beginning, each has its appropriate conjunction. In avoiding danger, distance is valued; in meeting the moment, nearness. Holding Together and Return favor being first; Qian and Great Strength dislike taking the lead. Good and ill fortune have their times, which must not be transgressed. Movement and stillness have their fitting occasions, which must not be exceeded. When one violates what the time forbids, the offense need not be great; when one misses what is appropriate, the error need not be deep. Observe the lines and ponder their changes: the possibilities of change are exhausted thereby.

**Wang Bi, continued.** Images bring forth meaning; words make images clear. Nothing fully expresses meaning like images, and nothing fully expresses images like words. Meaning is fully expressed through images, and images become manifest through words. Words therefore serve to clarify images: once the image is grasped, forget the words. Images serve to preserve meaning: once the meaning is grasped, forget the images. One who clings to words has not grasped the image; one who clings to images has not grasped the meaning. If images arise from meaning, yet one clings to the image, what one retains is no longer the image intended. If words arise from images, yet one clings to the words, what one retains is no longer the words intended. **Wang Bi, continued.** The Images contain no statement that the first or top line gains or loses its position. The Appended Statements discusses only the third and fifth, and the second and fourth, as having the same function but different positions; it too omits first and top. Why? Only Qian’s Wenyan says of the top nine, “Honored but without position,” and Waiting’s top six says, “Although not in the appropriate position.” If the top were a yin position, Waiting’s top six could not be called inappropriately placed. If it were a yang position, Qian’s top nine could not be called honored but without position. Whether occupied by yin or yang, it is described as lacking position; nor is the first described in terms of appropriate or inappropriate position. Thus first and top are an affair’s beginning and ending, with no fixed yin or yang stations. Qian’s first is therefore called hidden, and what lies beyond the fifth is called without position. No one occupying a station is called hidden, nor someone possessing a station called without one. Examination of all the hexagrams bears this out. The first and top have no fixed yin–yang stations: this is clear.

A position is the place in a hierarchy of honor and lowliness, a dwelling awaiting the use of ability. A line is what undertakes a station’s responsibilities and answers that hierarchy. Positions have rank; lines have yin and yang. The honored is yang’s place, the lowly yin’s. Hence the honored is called a yang position, and the lowly a yin position. Excluding first and top, the third and fifth stand above within their respective trigram bodies: how could they not be called yang positions? The second and fourth stand below within their respective bodies: how could they not be called yin positions? First and top are a constitution’s beginning and ending, the earlier and later stages of affairs. Their stations have no constant assignment, their affairs no constant location; they cannot be fixed as yin or yang. Rank has a constant order, but beginning and ending have no constant occupant. Yet an affair cannot lack beginning and ending, and a hexagram cannot lack six lines. Though first and top lack inherent yin–yang stations, they are the places of beginning and ending. Speaking comprehensively, wherever a line stands is its position. Since six lines complete a hexagram, one must nevertheless speak of “the six positions taking shape in their time.”[^p-extreme-positions]

**Kong Yingda.** “Changes” is the general name for transformation, a particular designation for alteration. Since Heaven and Earth opened, yin and yang have circulated, cold and heat have alternated, sun and moon have risen in turn. Every kind germinates; every form is nourished. Thus, when the sages first drew the eight trigrams, they established firm and yielding strokes to image those energies and arranged them in three places to image the Three Powers. The name Changes derives from transformation. As to why the Changes was made to hand down instruction, Confucius says: “In high antiquity, people were not yet differentiated and the kinds of things not yet distinguished. By drawing the trigrams and establishing the five energies, it set up the conduct of the Five Constants. Modeling Qian and Kun and following yin and yang, it rectified the obligations of ruler and minister, father and son, husband and wife. Assessing the time and devising what suited it, Fuxi made nets for hunting and fishing to supply the people’s needs. Then the people became ordered: rulers and parents were honored, ministers and children compliant, and all living things harmonious, each secure in its own nature.” This was the original intention in making the Changes and handing down its teaching.[^p-three-meanings]

**Master Shao Yong.** Heaven changes and people model themselves upon it: hence origination, success, benefit, and steadfastness are the changes of the Changes. **Cheng Yi, Preface to the Commentary.** Changes means transformation: changing with the time in order to follow the Way. As a book it is vast and complete. It enables us to accord with the principles of nature and destiny, comprehend the workings of hidden and manifest, exhaust the dispositions of affairs and things, and see the Way of opening understanding and accomplishing tasks. The sages’ concern for later generations may truly be called complete. Although antiquity is far away, the surviving classic remains. Yet earlier scholars lost its meaning while transmitting its words, and later students recite its words while forgetting their savor. Since Qin, its true transmission has largely ceased. Born a thousand years afterward, I grieve that this cultural inheritance has become obscured and wish later people to follow the stream back to its source. That is why I have written this commentary. “The Changes contains four ways of the sages: in speaking, esteem its words; in acting, its changes; in making implements, its images; in divining, its prognostications.” The principles of fortune and misfortune, decline and growth, and the paths of advance, withdrawal, survival, and loss are fully present in the words. By developing the words and examining the hexagrams, one may understand change; images and prognostications are included within it. “At rest, the noble person observes its images and dwells upon its words; in action, observes its changes and dwells upon its prognostications.” Some grasp the words without reaching their meaning, but no one fails to grasp the words and yet comprehends the meaning. Principle is most subtle; image most manifest. Substance and function have one source; the manifest and subtle admit no gap. Observe their meeting and interconnection in order to enact their norms and rites, and nothing is absent from the words. Therefore those who learn well must begin their search for the words’ meaning with what is near at hand. To make light of what is near is not to understand words. What I transmit is the words. To attain their meaning through the words rests with the reader.

**Zhu Xi, continued.** The Changes is the contraction and extension of yin and yang, changing with the time. Throughout antiquity and the present there are great openings and closings, and small ones. People today discuss the Changes without getting hold of anything. **Zhu Xi, continued.** When the sages first made the Changes, they observed above and examined below, seeing that everything filling the space between Heaven and Earth was nothing other than the principle of one yin and one yang. Where there is this principle, there is its image; where there is the image, its number is already present. This is not true only of the River Chart and Luo Writing; those two are simply especially ingenious and manifest instances. The sages therefore drew the hexagrams accordingly. Once the strokes were established, good and ill fortune were already within them. Yin and yang go and come, crossing and interweaving. In their times they differ in waning and growth: what grows is host, what wanes guest. In affairs they differ in appropriateness: what is appropriate is good, what is not is bad. Through these distinctions between host and guest, good and bad, good and ill fortune become visible. Hence “The eight trigrams determine good and ill fortune.” Once these are determined without error, affairs can be established by them, and great undertakings arise. Thus the sages made the Changes and taught divination to open the world’s unknowing, settle its purposes, and accomplish its affairs.

From Fuxi onward there were only these six strokes, with no written text to transmit. Only King Wen and the Duke of Zhou appended words. Hence “The sages established the hexagrams, observed the images, and appended words to make good and ill fortune clear.” Broadly speaking, the world’s paths concern only good and bad. Yet positions differ, circumstances differ, and the first indications are exceedingly subtle. Because people could not understand them, the sages used divination to make them intelligible. At rest, people could observe the images and dwell upon the words; in action, observe the changes and dwell upon the prognostications, without losing their way among right and wrong, gain and loss. This is why the Xia, Shang, and Zhou all used the book. Though their explanations differed and their words cannot all now be seen, each was administered by the Grand Diviner for divination. From Fuxi to King Wen and the Duke of Zhou, the treatment became fuller, but its divinatory use remained one. The principles for dealing with the matter in hand were already within the divination itself. Thus the method may seem rough and simple, yet the wise and foolish alike can each find a use for it.

Though images and words are fixed, they speak hypothetically of a situation and how it ought to be handled; they are not attached to one particular thing. A single hexagram or line can therefore embrace limitless affairs. In application, the Changes leaves nothing out and reaches everywhere; it depends only on how people use it. The Changes resembles a mirror: whatever comes before it can be reflected. The “hidden dragon,” for example, is simply the image of a hidden dragon. From the Son of Heaven to the common person, whoever comes before it can use it. When Confucius explains it as “dragon virtue that remains hidden,” he speaks more specifically in relation to an affair. Yet for one who knows how to read, even Confucius’s explanation remains alive and universally applicable; for one who does not, even King Wen’s and the Duke of Zhou’s words become dead. One must understand that the language is figurative and inclusive. “Figurative” means it is not caught on that particular affair; “inclusive” means it sets an image here that can encompass everything.[^p-images-open]

**Zhu Xi, continued.** The images of the Changes have sources from which they are taken and uses toward which they are developed; they are not casual allegories. Yet Han scholars, insisting on exhausting their sources, became fixed and obstructed. From Wang Bi onward, interpreters wished only to develop their uses, becoming sketchy and unsupported. Both err through partiality and an inability to leave doubtful matters open. Consider one instance. The Discussion of the Trigrams explicitly says that Qian is a horse and Kun an ox. That a horse is vigorous and an ox compliant accords with the constant principles of things. But when one demands that the hexagram answer every word, there are things one cannot understand: Difficulty at the Beginning has horses but no Qian; Clinging has an ox but no Kun; Qian’s six dragons might seem to require Zhen; Kun’s mare ought instead to indicate Qian. Han scholars could not find the answers in the Discussion of the Trigrams and consequently devised methods of overlapping bodies, changing hexagrams, the five phases, stem assignments, and manifest-and-hidden configurations, cross-checking them in hope of an accidental fit. Their explanations may be detailed, but what cannot be made coherent remains incoherent. What can be made to fit rests on forced association rather than a natural connection. Only the occasional case that fits without ingenuity seems credible; yet if it has no bearing above on the root of moral principle, nor below on instruction and warning in human affairs, why strain one’s mind and every power to find it, insisting on success?

Thus Wang Bi says, “If the meaning accords with vigor, why must Qian be present for a horse? If the line accords with compliance, why must Kun be present for an ox?” Master Cheng likewise says, “Principle has no form, so images are borrowed to reveal meaning.” In breaking the earlier scholars’ rigid, disjointed errors and opening a way for later students to dwell upon words and prognostications, these statements are excellent. Yet their intention seems to be that the Changes takes its images from no particular source, merely using comparison and evocation like the Odes, or analogies like Mencius. On that account, the Discussion of the Trigrams would have nothing to do with the Changes, and “taking nearby from the body and farther away from things” would be superfluous. Their explanations therefore seem not quite complete.

My own tentative view is that the images must have had sources, whose explanations were presumably preserved in the Grand Diviner’s office. Since these can no longer be investigated, let us leave them open. Taking the images in the words directly, and seeking their meanings so that they provide instruction and determine good and ill fortune, as Wang, Cheng, and my Original Meaning do, is sufficient. We need not pursue an image’s origin to the utmost; but neither may we simply declare it hypothetical and hasten to forget it.[^p-image-methods]

**Zhu Xi, continued.** The sages originally made the Changes so that people could divine whether a proposed action was permissible, and through this learn to do good. It is like Yan Junping’s practice: when speaking with a son, he grounded his words in filial devotion; when speaking with a minister, in loyalty. Hexagram and line statements therefore follow classes of images, standing here in readiness for someone to inquire and decide, so that the words encountered may resolve the matter in doubt. It seems as though the decision is entrusted to spiritual illumination, yet there must be a corresponding principle before there can be such words. Principle is invariably right, so the earnest counsels and warnings all depend on rightness. In this way the world’s movements are made right in their unity and do not go astray in their direction.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4539:037 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4539:038 -->

**Zhu Xi, continued.** Hexagram and line statements originally determined good and ill fortune for diviners, and thereby instructed and warned them. Only when the Judgments, Images, and Wenyan were composed were the moral principles implicit in these prognostications and admonitions developed and explained. Later people saw only the principles Confucius expounded, without tracing back to the original intention of King Wen and the Duke of Zhou. They dismissed divination as beneath discussion, and their accounts of the Changes grew remote from the realities of daily use. They generally forced associations and labored explanations, favoring one particular affair, and lost the subtle power to encompass, penetrate, unfold, and reach in every direction. Were that all the sages intended, they could simply have written another book explaining moral principles plainly for posterity. Why borrow hexagram images and compose such difficult, profound, and obscure language?

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4539:038 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4539:039 -->

**Zhu Xi, continued.** Broadly speaking, the Changes was made for divination; its words must therefore be rooted in images and numbers, not in the sages’ private invention. Its encouragements and warnings address the person who receives that hexagram or line in casting—not, conversely, the hexagram or line itself. Recent interpreters take quite another approach. Their accounts may contain moral principles, yet lack the living intention. Even great scholars have not entirely escaped this. Recently, through dwelling upon the text, I happened fortunately to reach this insight and privately rejoiced, thinking that Heaven had opened my understanding. Yet when I speak of it to others, I have not found anyone who understands it deeply.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4539:039 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4539:040 -->

**Zhu Xi, continued.** Throughout the Changes there is “good fortune in steadfastness,” never good fortune in not being steadfast; there is “benefit in steadfastness,” never benefit in its opposite. Someone casting Qian certainly receives the promise of great success, but what follows is “benefit in steadfastness”: rightness brings benefit; without rightness there is no benefit. The beginnings of the highest principle and the sages’ utmost instruction lie within this. The book is broadly established for noble people, not something petty people, robbers, and thieves can steal and put to their own use.[^p-steadfastness]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4539:040 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4539:041 -->

**Cai Yuanding.** The world’s myriad sounds arise from one closing and one opening; its myriad principles from one movement and one stillness; its myriad numbers from one odd and one even; its myriad images from one square and one circle. All arise from the two strokes of Qian and Kun.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4539:041 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4539:042 -->

**Xu Heng.** The first is the lowest position and the beginning of an affair. Occupied by yang, it has the ability to act, but there may be concern that it will not remain content within its allotted place. Occupied by yin, it poses no fear of overstepping, but there may be concern that its weakness and obscurity will prevent it from meeting the time. Generally, weakness finds completion difficult, while firmness and vigor make action easier; in many hexagrams, weakness leads to misfortune. Speaking broadly, it is easier to remain steadfast at the first position and harder at the top. At the first, many paths remain available; at the top, the position has reached its limit. Hence the first lines of the sixty-four hexagrams often escape blame, while at the top there is frequently no remedy. Such is the difference in difficulty between beginning and ending.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4539:042 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4539:043 -->

**Xu Heng, continued.** The second and fourth are both yin positions. Even correctly placed, the fourth still suffers from not being centered; how much more if it is not correctly placed! Even incorrectly placed, the second retains the excellence of centeredness; how much more if both correct and centered! The fourth is near the ruler; the second far away. Their circumstances therefore also differ. This is why the second often receives praise and the fourth often feels apprehension. The second is a central position: yin or yang occupying it both attain the center. Centeredness means neither leaning nor inclining, neither excess nor deficiency. With such a capacity, one more readily accords with the meaning of the time. Once that accord is attained, good fortune can be predicted.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4539:043 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4539:044 -->

**Xu Heng, continued.** Of the six positions, the third is especially difficult to occupy. It stands where upper and lower meet, at the boundary between inner and outer—not a place of simplicity, peace, and ease.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4539:044 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4539:045 -->

**Xu Heng, continued.** The fourth stands near the ruler, a place of much apprehension. Yieldingness there has the excellence of compliance; firmness arouses suspicion of encroachment. Yet one must also ask whether the fifth is yin or yang. Yin supporting yang finds acceptance with the ruler and a compliant configuration; yang supporting yin finds acceptance but a contrary configuration. With compliance, nothing is necessarily impermissible. In the contrary configuration, advancing upward is particularly forbidden, and misfortune and blame will surely follow. Yang supporting yang, or yin supporting yin, finds no such acceptance. Nevertheless, yang is incorrectly placed but capable, while yin is correctly placed but lacks that capacity: their circumstances differ. Capability without correctness calls for few desires; hence the fourth lines belonging to Qian often escape blame. Correctness without capability calls for a correspondence; hence the fourth lines belonging to Gen are better with a correspondence and worse without one. The fourth lines belonging to Kun alone can occupy their place with yielding compliance and escape blame even without corresponding support. This too is the meaning of following the time.[^p-fourth-trigrams]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4539:045 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4539:046 -->

**Xu Heng, continued.** The fifth is the center of the upper trigram and the ruler’s position. Among the lines, no virtue is more refined. It can stand foremost among all things and, whatever the time, bring great affairs to completion. This is why the Commentary says that the fifth often achieves results.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4539:046 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4539:047 -->

**Xu Heng, continued.** The top is an affair’s ending and a time’s extremity. The firmness or yieldingness of its capacity and whether it has a correspondence within may contribute to its meaning, but ultimately none carries as much weight as its being topmost and final. Thus, when difficulty is about to end, the words point to a way out; when an affair is accomplished, they show how it may be preserved. Where goodness needs no further encouragement, they simply pronounce good fortune. Where adverse circumstances cannot be resolved, they simply pronounce misfortune. Though the line’s qualities are poor, if improvement remains possible, it is still admonished. Though its position is extreme, if action remains possible, it is still instructed. Broadly, what accumulates from small beginnings flourishes, and what passes beyond flourishing declines. Some things cannot change; others cannot but change. Is not the Great Commentary’s “The top is easy to know” said because the affair has already taken shape?

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4539:047 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4539:048 -->

**Hu Yigui.** Although upper and lower bodies correspond, actual correspondence is between a yang line and a yin line, or a yin line and a yang line. If both are yang or both yin, they do not correspond, even though they occupy corresponding positions. Yet affairs have many changes, and action depends on the time. Some benefit from having a correspondence; others suffer from it. Some have good fortune without a correspondence; others misfortune. All this is brought about by the particular circumstances; no single rule can settle it. Holding Together’s fifth, firm and centered, receives the response of the five yin above and below; Great Possession’s fifth, yielding and centered, receives that of the five yang; Small Restraint’s fourth, yielding and appropriately placed, likewise receives the response of the five firm lines. These cannot be judged by the ordinary rule of correspondence among six lines.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4539:048 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4539:049 -->

**Hu Yigui, continued.** That the fifth is the ruler’s position throughout the sixty-four hexagrams is a broad outline of the Changes. Sometimes a line occupies it without a ruler’s meaning; sometimes another position carries that meaning. These are variations within the Changes, and one must not be held fast by the general rule.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4539:049 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4539:050 -->

**Hu Bingwen.** In hexagram prognostications, “success” is common and “great success” rare. In line prognostications, “good fortune” is common and “supreme good fortune” rare. Great success means success through great goodness; supreme good fortune means fortune through great goodness. In human conduct, goodness occurs once in a hundred instances, great goodness once in a thousand; hence yuan is esteemed. Yet let us consider the mind at its first stirring. Good and bad both arise in the slightest movements of thought. Fully develop the good at that point, and it becomes goodness in its greatest extent. If there is the slightest unwholesomeness, it is not yuan; if there is unwholesomeness for even one breath, it is not yuan.[^p-yuan]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4539:050 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4539:051 -->

**Wu Cheng.** Nowhere is time as fully expressed in its character as in the Changes. Master Cheng calls it “changing with the time in order to follow the Way.” In explaining the sixty-four Judgments, the Master singles out twelve hexagrams to disclose the governing principle, praising the greatness of their time, its meaning, or its application. Each hexagram has its time: sixty-four distinct times. Each line has its time: three hundred and eighty-four distinct times. Beginning with Qian remaining Qian and ending with Before Completion remaining Before Completion, there are four thousand and ninety-six times, each with its own encounter. Extend the pattern and develop its kinds: circumstances change by hundreds, thousands, and tens of thousands without end. Yet that by which we respond fittingly to each time is one.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4539:051 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4539:052 -->

**Xue Xuan.** The sixty-four hexagrams are simply one odd stroke and one even stroke. Because the times encountered and positions occupied differ, however, there are limitless changes of affairs. Human beings likewise have only movement and stillness; yet because times and positions differ, there are limitless principles to understand. This is what makes the Changes the Changes.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4539:052 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4539:053 -->

**Cai Qing.** Qian’s hexagram statement asks people to be like Qian; Kun’s asks them to be like Kun. With such hexagrams as Unformed Understanding and Decay, however, one must counter the condition they signify. Thus there are circumstances to follow in accordance with the time, and circumstances to restrain in accordance with the time. The Way of the Changes is simply the time. These are its two aspects, which students must examine closely.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4539:053 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4539:054 -->

**Cai Qing, continued.** In appending line statements, the Duke of Zhou sometimes drew on a line’s virtue, sometimes its position, sometimes the time of the whole hexagram or that particular line. Sometimes he also drew on the corresponding line, or on the line it supports or rides above. Sometimes supporting, riding, correspondence, time, and position are considered together; sometimes only one or two of these aspects. Sometimes one line is taken as governing all the others. Broadly, the explanations do not go beyond these considerations.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4539:054 -->

## Guiding Principles, Part Three

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4540:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4540:002 -->

**Source description:** On how to read the Changes and on the strengths and defects of its interpreters.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4540:002 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4540:003 -->

**Wang Tong.** The Changes meets sorrow and trouble with vigilance and unremitting effort. Does it not stand in awe of Heaven, pity humanity, and consider how to act at the proper time? Fan Shixuan said: “How far-reaching! When I contemplate the Way of the Changes, how difficult it seems!” He replied: “Indeed! One may simply remain vigorous throughout the day.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4540:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4540:004 -->

**Wang Tong, continued.** Liu Xuan asked about the Changes. He replied: “Even the sage pursued the Changes to the end of his life; how much more must we?” Xuan said: “When I discuss it at court, no one can match me.” He gave no answer. After Xuan withdrew, he told his disciples: “‘Silently bringing it to completion, inspiring trust without words’ rests in virtuous conduct.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4540:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4540:005 -->

**Wang Tong, continued.** Master Huang of Beishan was skilled in medicine: he attended first to sleep and food, then to needles and medicines. Master Hou of Fenyin was skilled in divination: he attended first to human affairs, then to explaining the hexagrams.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4540:005 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4540:006 -->

**Master Shao Yong.** To know the Changes, one need not quote it or expound it. Mencius’s words never explicitly discuss the Changes, yet its Way is present within them; few people see this. To be able to use the Changes is to know it. Mencius may indeed be called someone who used it well.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4540:006 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4540:007 -->

**Master Cheng.** In observing the Changes, first examine the time, then each line’s capacity. A single line often contains several meanings; the sage usually selects the weightiest and formulates its words accordingly. Sometimes, because the Changes has already spoken often of one matter, he chooses something not yet expressed. Sometimes he speaks only of the situation and not of the line’s capacity. All must be considered in their particular context. One must first examine the hexagram in order to understand the words.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4540:007 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4540:008 -->

**Master Cheng, continued.** Ancient students all received a transmission. The sages composed the classics to clarify the Way. If people today do not first understand moral principles, they cannot study the classics: they have not attained the intention of that transmission. The Appended Statements, for example, was intended to clarify the Changes. Without first seeking the meanings of the hexagrams, one cannot understand it.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4540:008 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4540:009 -->

**Master Cheng, continued.** The Changes must be silently recognized and inwardly understood. Merely exhausting the words’ meanings is wasted effort.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4540:009 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4540:010 -->

**Zhu Xi.** In reading the Changes, one must consider what it was like before hexagrams and lines were drawn. From there, see how all its hexagrams, lines, images, and numbers arise naturally, rather than being invented at will. The Odes were made in response to customs and historical changes; the Documents in response to rulers’ affairs. The Changes initially had no particular thing before it: it spoke without attachment to one. Before there were drawn hexagrams, there was one undivided Supreme Ultimate. In a person, this is the centeredness before joy, anger, sorrow, and pleasure arise. Once they issue forth, yin and yang, good and ill fortune, and every affair are present within them. One must see this principle in its encompassing openness from the utmost emptiness and stillness. Begin by fixing the explanation to one particular affair, and one becomes stuck and unable to proceed. This is the meaning of “Purity, stillness, precision, and depth are the teaching of the Changes.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4540:010 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4540:011 -->

**Zhu Xi, continued.** The classics are difficult to read, and this one especially so. Before opening it, one must already have worked at the broad outlines of images and numbers. After opening it, one finds that earlier scholars have often forced the text’s original meaning into rigid explanations. Readers are left with a cramped understanding, unable to see its original living method for opening understanding and accomplishing tasks.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4540:011 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4540:012 -->

**Zhu Xi, continued.** The Changes differs from the Odes and Documents. It speaks fully of the limitless principles of affairs throughout the world and future generations. Just one or two words express a principle. Only after experiencing many of the world’s changes can one read the Changes and recognize in each its precise, carefully judged, and upright principle. Since we have not experienced them all, how could we perceive this unless our minds were exceedingly open, clear, calm, and still?

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4540:012 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4540:013 -->

**Zhu Xi, continued.** If one reads the Changes with firm attention to its images, its savor is enduring. Reading without any grounding offers little meaning. He also said: “It is true that ‘when one grasps the principle, images and numbers are contained within it.’ Yet, following the stream upward, one must first see the precise application of images and numbers before one can explain principle without going astray. Otherwise affairs lack concrete verification, and abstract reasoning easily errs.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4540:013 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4540:014 -->

**Zhu Xi, continued.** People reading the Changes today should distinguish three levels. Read Fuxi’s Changes as though the many explanations in the Judgments, Images, and Wenyan did not yet exist. Then its original intention becomes visible: it was to be used for divination. When King Wen and the Duke of Zhou treated the sixty-four hexagrams and added Qian’s “great success; benefit in steadfastness” and Kun’s “great success; benefit in the steadfastness of a mare,” they were already expressing a further account of principle. Yet they still spoke from the position of the person divining: receiving Qian meant great success and benefit in what is right. When Confucius appended his explanations of the Changes and composed the Judgments, Images, and Wenyan, he took origination, success, benefit, and steadfastness as Qian’s four virtues.[^p-three-readings]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4540:014 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4540:015 -->

**Source summary:** The foregoing concerns reading the Changes.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4540:015 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4540:016 -->

**Kong Yingda.** When the dragon emerged from the River, the eight trigrams proclaimed their images; when the qilin was wounded in the marsh, the Ten Wings manifested their use. The work drew on several sages and passed through the three antiquities. When Qin lost the golden mirror, this cultural inheritance did not fall; when Han put the pearl bag in order, classical learning flourished anew. Among those who transmitted the Changes, the Western Capital had Ding, Meng, Jing, and Tian; the Eastern Capital had Xun, Liu, Ma, and Zheng. Broadly, they continued one another’s teachings, with none standing incomparably above the rest. Only Wang Fusi’s commentary in the Wei surpassed both ancient and modern works. Thus the scholars south of the Yangzi all transmitted his learning, while northern scholars seldom equaled them. Yet more than ten southern schools of explanatory commentary favored empty profundity in their words and fanciful, insubstantial meanings. The principles of the Changes are indeed difficult to exhaust: one may speak of mystery beyond mystery, but when it comes to handing down models and establishing standards, it addresses what exists and teaches what exists. Discussions of emptiness abiding within or without, or of the acting subject and its object, concern the Buddhist teaching, not instruction in Confucius’s school.[^p-several-sages]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4540:016 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4540:017 -->

**Master Cheng.** Master Shao Yaofu received his learning from Li Tingzhi; Tingzhi received it from Mu Bochang; Bochang received it from Master Chen Tunan, called Xiyi, of Mount Hua. Traced back, its line of transmission reaches far. Mu and Li’s words and conduct can still be known in outline. Yet the purity and vastness of Master Shao’s understanding owed much to what he attained for himself.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4540:017 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4540:018 -->

**Yin Tun.** Master Yichuan’s practice fully embodied the Changes. His commentary was simply written out from that practice. Read it thoroughly and savor it, and this becomes visible.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4540:018 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4540:019 -->

**Zhu Xi, questioned by a disciple about “corresponding to the year.”** “Placing Zhen in the east, Li in the south, Dui in the west, and Kan in the north is one account. Assigning the twelve sovereign hexagrams to the twelve earthly branches is another. When Jiao Yanshou made his method of assigning hexagram energies to days, he combined the two. He assigned the twenty-four lines of Zhen, Li, Dui, and Kan to the four seasons and the twelve sovereign hexagrams to the twelve months. He further divided forty-eight hexagrams among dukes, marquises, ministers, and grandees; from this arose the doctrine of six days and seven parts. If the eight-trigram arrangement is primary, Qian should not be sovereign of si, Kun sovereign of hai, Gen marquis for shen and you, or Xun marquis for xu and hai. If the twelve monthly hexagrams are primary, Qian should not be northwest, Kun southwest, Gen northeast, or Xun southeast in the eight-trigram arrangement. The two accounts contradict one another. Moreover, dividing forty-eight hexagrams into dukes, marquises, ministers, and grandees and attaching them to the twelve sovereign figures rests on no modeled pattern. It is simply asserted by personal opinion, without foundation. One need not wait to discuss the removal of four hexagrams and twenty-four lines to see the error. Yang Xiong’s ordering in the Supreme Mystery uses Jiao’s method entirely. Its eighty-one Heads likewise exclude Zhen, Li, Dui, and Kan and model only the remaining sixty hexagrams. Many interpreters nevertheless make some of those Heads model the four excluded figures; only recently did Xu Han correct this. The establishment of the two additional appraisals, Odd and Surplus, was likewise an addition because the seven hundred and twenty-nine appraisals fell short of the total of sixty hexagrams at six days and seven parts. One should not use Yang’s account in reverse to establish the correctness of Jiao’s.”[^p-calendar-schools]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4540:019 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4540:020 -->

**Zhu Xi, continued.** The Earlier Heaven diagram is not my teaching but Kangjie’s; not Kangjie’s but Xiyi’s; not Xiyi’s but Confucius’s. At that time the scholars had lost its transmission, while people outside their tradition passed it secretly among themselves as an art of the alchemical furnace. Xiyi and Kangjie returned it to the Changes, and only then was its meaning again made clear to the world.[^p-lineage]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4540:020 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4540:021 -->

**Zhu Xi, in conversation.** Someone said: “Yichuan’s Changes explains too much moral principle.” He replied: “Yichuan says that sages have their use and worthy people theirs; if one line represented only one affair, the three hundred and eighty-four lines could serve only three hundred and eighty-four affairs. That is excellently said. Yet in his explanations, those lines still serve only three hundred and eighty-four affairs.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4540:021 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4540:022 -->

**Zhu Xi, continued.** With the Odes and Documents, one need only examine the glosses enough to make the words intelligible, and then dwell upon the original text: its principles are in the original. However much the small-print commentary beneath it says, how could it surpass that? But Cheng’s Commentary on the Changes can stand apart from the original. In this book, Master Cheng proceeds plainly, patiently, and through every turn, leaving no meaning unexplored. These are not principles hammered and squeezed out of the text: they are spread before one with ease. Even that manner of writing is difficult to learn. One might produce other writing that is lofty, forceful, and vigorous; but how could one attain the quiet simplicity of this Commentary?

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4540:022 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4540:023 -->

**Zhu Xi, in conversation.** Asked whether the Commentary’s general treatment of the three hundred and eighty-four lines as persons limited its reach, he replied: “Yes. One must not fix them as persons; look at what the casting encounters. Some statements concern affairs, others positions. Good and ill fortune concern affairs; beginnings and endings concern times; high and low concern positions. Read according to what is being undertaken, and everything communicates. The Appended Statements says, ‘It cannot be made into a fixed rule; it follows only where change leads.’ How could one fix it as speaking of persons?”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4540:023 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4540:024 -->

**Zhu Xi, continued.** Having recently read the book closely, I think Cheng’s Commentary has already grasped a great deal. It should not, however, have explained everything as moral principle without considering divination; that leaves some of its explanations with nowhere to rest. Read it as divination and develop Cheng’s explanations from there, and their principles are unalterable.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4540:024 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4540:025 -->

**Zhu Xi, continued.** Since Qin and Han, those who study images and words have become mired in numerical techniques, missing their broad, penetrating simplicity. Those who discuss moral principles have sunk into emptiness and quiescence, failing to reach benevolence, rightness, centeredness, and correctness. If one seeks a work that establishes instruction according to the time and continues the three sages—differing in method while sharing the Way—there is only the book of Master Cheng of Yichuan.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4540:025 -->

**Zhu Xi, continued.** The Elder Su’s interpretation of the Changes depends especially on the three statements beginning “Love and hatred contend, and good and ill fortune arise.” He views the six lines as though they were people whose families had hated and killed one another for generations: this line attacks that one, this stroke overcomes another. It is quite unlike ordinary human feeling. Dongpo saw how rough and careless this was and added some Buddhism and Laozi, leaving the book with two different characters.[^p-su-and-wang]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4540:026 -->

**Wang Yinglin.** Explaining the Changes through moral principles began with Wang Bi; He Yan was not his equal. The man whose “pure conversation” helped destroy Jin was Yan, not Bi. Fan Ning was wrong to place Wang Bi and He Yan together.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4540:027 -->

**Wang Yinglin, continued.** Master Cheng says of the Changes that when its meaning is grasped, images and numbers are contained within it. Master Zhu holds that one must first see images and numbers before explaining principle; without concrete verification in affairs, abstract reasoning easily errs. I have read Yan Yanzhi’s Instructions for the Household: “Ma and Lu grasped its images and numbers, taking them from things; Xun and Wang brought forward its governing source, finding it in the mind.” He esteems Xun and Wang more highly. Li Taifa likewise says that Yixing understood numbers without knowing their meaning, and Guan Lu understood images without penetrating their principle. Since Fusi’s learning prevailed, image-and-number teaching has become obscured. Yet only when moral principles, images, and numbers are threaded together as one is understanding fully sound.

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4541:001 -->

## Principles of Interpretation

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4541:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4541:002 -->

#### Time

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4541:002 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4541:003 -->

**Imperial Compilers.** Decline and growth, fullness and emptiness, are called time: Peace, Obstruction, Splitting Apart, and Return are examples. Sometimes time is expressed through an affair, as in Contention, The Army, Biting Through, and Nourishment; sometimes through a principle, as in Treading, Modesty, Influence, and Constancy; sometimes through an image, as in The Well and The Cauldron. All four are called time.[^p-compilers]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4541:003 -->

<a id="principles-position"></a>

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4541:004 -->

#### Position

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4541:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4541:005 -->

**Imperial Compilers.** Honor and lowliness, above and below, are called position. When Wang Bi says that the middle four lines have positions but the first and top do not, he does not mean they lack yin–yang positions; he means official stations. The fifth is the ruler’s position, the fourth that of a close minister. The third, though not close, still holds an honored station. The second is less honored than the third and fourth, but corresponds directly to the fifth. These four all hold responsibility at the time, so they are said to have positions. First and top are more often discussed only as the beginning and ending of the time. In terms of position, the first is someone beginning to advance but not yet in charge, while the top is someone already retired and outside affairs; they are therefore said to lack positions. This, however, is only the general rule. In variant cases—the first lines of Difficulty at the Beginning, Peace, Return, and Approach, or the top lines of Great Possession, Viewing, Great Restraint, and Nourishment—all meet the time and exercise responsibility because they are principal lines of their hexagrams. Sometimes the fifth is not discussed as the ruler’s position, because the hexagram concerns the minister’s Way and does not extend to the ruler. Hence Master Zhu says: “The regular may be sought through analogous cases; variation cannot be measured by a fixed rule.”[^p-extreme-positions]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4541:005 -->

<a id="principles-virtue"></a>

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4541:006 -->

#### Virtue

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4541:006 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4541:007 -->

**Imperial Compilers.** Firmness, yieldingness, centeredness, correctness, and their absence are called virtue. Firmness and yieldingness each have good and bad applications. When the time calls for firmness, firmness is good; when it calls for yieldingness, yieldingness is good. Centeredness and correctness alone are never without goodness. Yet correctness is less excellent than centeredness. Thus Master Cheng says: “What is correct is not necessarily centered; what is centered is never without correctness.” The six lines are not necessarily fortunate whenever appropriately placed, whereas the centered second and fifth are especially often fortunate. This is the reason.[^p-correct-centered]

#### Correspondence and Adjacency

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4541:008 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4541:009 -->

**Imperial Compilers.** Correspondence relates lines facing one another across the lower and upper bodies; adjacency relates neighboring lines in successive positions. In the Changes, the weightiest relations are the fourth’s adjacency to the fifth and the second’s correspondence with the fifth. The fifth is the honored position; the nearby fourth supports it, while the distant second answers it. Nearby support values reverence, compliance, and care; here firmness is less good than yieldingness. Distant response values strength, resolution, and the ability to act; here yieldingness is less good than firmness. The Master says: “The second and fourth have the same function but different positions. The second often receives praise, the fourth often feels apprehension because it is near. Yieldingness as a Way does not benefit from distance; its essential aim is no blame, and its use lies in yielding centeredness.” Saying that yieldingness does not benefit from distance also shows that firmness does not benefit from nearness; conversely, yieldingness benefits from nearness and firmness from distance. This passage of the Master’s is truly a comprehensive example for the entire Changes.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4541:009 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4541:010 -->

**Imperial Compilers.** In adjacency or correspondence, only when one line is yin and the other yang do their dispositions seek and find one another. Firm answering firm or yielding answering yielding is called no correspondence. Firm beside firm or yielding beside yielding likewise lacks this disposition of mutual seeking and attainment.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4541:010 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4541:011 -->

**Imperial Compilers.** Extend this rule: sixteen hexagrams have a six in the fourth place supporting a nine in the fifth, and all are favorable.[^p-sixteen-groups] Holding Together says, “Outwardly holding to the worthy”; Small Restraint, “With sincerity, apprehension departs”; Viewing, “It benefits one to be the king’s guest”; Repeated Danger, “Presenting simplicity through the window”; The Family, “Enriching the family”; Increase, “Walking the middle way, reporting to the duke: he follows”; The Well, “The well is lined with masonry: no blame”; Gradual Progress, “Perhaps finding a level branch”; Penetration, “The hunt yields three kinds”; Dispersion, “Dispersing one’s group: supreme good fortune”; Limitation, “Ease in limitation: success”; Inner Sincerity, “The moon nearly full.” These are all favorable words.[^p-oracle-excerpts] Difficulty at the Beginning, Waiting, and Limping involve following one another amid danger and difficulty, and therefore say “Going brings good fortune,” “Coming out of the pit,” and “Coming brings connection.” After Completion involves mutual warning before disorder arises, and therefore says “Be on guard all day.” These too are favorable words.

**Imperial Compilers.** Sixteen hexagrams likewise have a nine in the fourth place supporting a six in the fifth, but these cannot all be favorable; many are unfortunate. Clinging’s “Burned, dead, cast away,” Constancy’s “No game in the field,” Advance’s great rat, The Cauldron’s overturned food, and Shock’s sinking into mud are all unfortunate lines. Great Possession’s absence of boastful display, Opposition’s solitude, Release’s loosening the great toe, The Marrying Maiden’s deferred time, The Wanderer’s unquiet heart, and Small Excess’s “Going is dangerous: be on guard” are not outright misfortune, yet are not wholly favorable. Only Enthusiasm’s fourth, the one yang to which those above and below respond; Biting Through’s fourth, the firm line governing judicial action; Abundance’s fourth, governing movement in response to clarity; Great Strength, whose strength reaches its height at the fourth; and Before Completion, whose incompletion reaches completion at the fourth, are principal lines. They therefore receive words of fortune and benefit and escape misfortune and blame.

**Imperial Compilers.** Sixteen hexagrams have a nine in the second place corresponding with a six in the fifth, and all are favorable. Unformed Understanding’s “The son can manage the household”; The Army’s “At the center of the army”; Peace’s “Attaining esteem through the middle way”; Great Possession’s “A great wagon for carrying”; Decay’s “Setting right the mother’s decay” while “attaining the middle way”; Approach’s “Influencing approach: good fortune, nothing without benefit”; Constancy’s “Regret vanishes”; Great Strength’s “Good fortune in steadfastness”; Opposition’s “Meeting the lord in the lane”; Release’s “Obtaining the yellow arrow”; Decrease’s “Not decreasing it, increasing it”; Pushing Upward’s “It benefits one to present the yue offering”; and The Cauldron’s “Having substance”—all are favorable words. Great Restraint’s “The wagon’s axle-binding comes loose” means the time calls for stopping. The Marrying Maiden’s benefit in secluded steadfastness means the time calls for keeping to one’s station. Before Completion’s “Dragging the wheel back: good fortune in steadfastness” means the time calls for waiting. None is an unfortunate statement.

**Imperial Compilers.** Sixteen hexagrams likewise have a six in the second place corresponding with a nine in the fifth, but these cannot all be favorable; some bring misfortune or humiliation. Obstruction’s “Embracing and supporting”; Fellowship’s humiliation in the clan; Following’s “Binding oneself to the little boy, losing the grown man”; Viewing’s “Peering through a crack is shameful”; and Influence’s “Influence in the calf: misfortune” are not favorable words. Difficulty at the Beginning’s hesitation and obstruction, Retreat’s binding with yellow oxhide, Limping’s repeated difficulty undertaken not for oneself, and After Completion’s lost carriage screen that must not be pursued reveal steadfast compliance in difficult times. Only Holding Together’s inward attachment; Without Falsity’s benefit in going somewhere; The Family’s “At the inner provisions: good fortune in steadfastness”; Increase’s “Enduring steadfastness brings good fortune”; Gathering’s “Being drawn brings good fortune, no blame”; Revolution’s “Only after the day has passed is there trust; going forward brings good fortune”; and Gradual Progress’s eating and drinking in ease meet a time when those above and below unite in virtue. Hence their words are all favorable. How true are the Master’s words: “Its essential aim is no blame; its use lies in yielding centeredness.”[^p-revolution-quotation]

**Imperial Compilers.** There are correspondences beyond the second and fifth, and adjacencies beyond the fourth and fifth. Their meaning, however, is less weighty than responding to or supporting the fifth.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4541:015 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4541:016 -->

**Imperial Compilers.** Among correspondences, that between fourth and first is sometimes significant, while third and top very seldom are interpreted through correspondence. Why? The fourth is a great minister’s position. A great minister serves the ruler through other people and must obtain worthy assistance from below: this gives the correspondence its meaning. The top stands outside affairs; answering downward to someone actively responsible would compromise its lofty independence. The third holds a minister’s position; reaching past the fifth to answer the top would compromise undivided allegiance. Hence the correspondence is not generally invoked. Even the favorable correspondence of fourth and first concerns only a six in the fourth place answering an initial nine. The initial nine is a worthy person of firm virtue; the fourth six has the excellence of placing itself below others. Hence Difficulty at the Beginning and Adornment’s “Seeking a marriage alliance,” Nourishment’s “The tiger gazes intently,” and Decrease’s “Make haste: there is joy” are favorable. If a nine in the fourth place answers an initial six, association below with a petty person instead becomes a burden: Great Excess’s not bending downward, Release’s “Loosen your great toe,” and The Cauldron’s “The legs break” are examples.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4541:016 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4541:017 -->

**Imperial Compilers.** Among adjacencies, that between fifth and top is sometimes significant; the others very seldom are interpreted through adjacency. Why? The fifth is the ruler’s position, unsurpassed in honor. If it can place itself below the top, it honors that line’s worthiness, and this gives the relation its meaning. Again, this interpretation applies only when a six in the fifth place meets a top nine. The top nine is a worthy person above the common world; the fifth six is a ruler inwardly open. Thus Confucius praises the fifth six and top nine of Great Possession and Great Restraint for “honoring the worthy,” and those of Nourishment and The Cauldron for “nourishing the worthy.” Their words are especially favorable. A nine in the fifth place beside a top six is instead burdened by honoring and favoring a petty person: Great Excess’s “An old woman obtains a young husband,” Influence’s “The intention is at the extremity,” Breakthrough’s amaranth, and Joy’s “Trust in what strips away” are examples. Following alone gives significance to its fifth nine’s placing itself below the top six, because the hexagram means that firmness comes and places itself beneath yieldingness. Adjacency between first and second, second and third, or third and fourth is not direct correspondence and may fall into factional partiality; its meaning is therefore less weighty.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4541:017 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4541:018 -->

**Imperial Compilers.** These are the regular examples. When a line is principal to the hexagram, however, the other lines derive good or ill fortune from their adjacency or response to it. The fifth’s role as principal line need not be explained further. Where Enthusiasm’s fourth is principal, the first cries out and the third looks upward. Where Splitting Apart’s top is principal, the third has no blame and the fifth nothing without benefit. Where Return’s first is principal, the second places itself below benevolence and the fourth returns alone. Where Breakthrough’s top is principal, the third shows strength in its cheekbones and the fifth is amaranth. Where Coming to Meet’s first is principal, the second has fish in its wrapping while the fourth has none. These too are major meanings of the Changes and cannot be judged by ordinary rules of adjacency and correspondence.

#### Principal Lines of the Hexagrams

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4541:019 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4541:020 -->

**Imperial Compilers.** What is called a hexagram’s principal line may be either its constituting line or its governing line. The constituting line is that through which the hexagram comes to be what it is. Regardless of high or low position, good or bad virtue, a line may be principal if the hexagram’s meaning arises through it. The governing line must possess good virtue and meet both time and position. It is therefore most often the fifth, though other lines are sometimes selected. When the constituting line is also the governing line, its virtue must be good and it must also meet time and position. When the constituting line cannot serve as governing line, its virtue, time, and position are out of accord. Broadly, these explanations are all present in the Master’s Commentary on the Judgments and must be examined separately in each hexagram.[^p-governing-two]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4541:020 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4541:021 -->

**Imperial Compilers.** If a hexagram’s constituting line is also its governing line, it has one principal line. If it has a constituting line and a distinct governing line, both are principal. Sometimes two lines together constitute the hexagram, and both are principal. Sometimes its constitution draws on two images, and the principal line of each image becomes principal to the hexagram. These cases too must be distinguished hexagram by hexagram.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4541:021 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4541:022 -->

- **Qian (1) · 乾.** The nine in the fifth place is principal. Qian is Heaven’s Way, and the fifth images Heaven; Qian is the ruler’s Way, and the fifth is the ruler’s position. It is also firm, vigorous, centered, and correct. With these four qualities complete, it attains the purity of Heaven’s virtue and is therefore principal. The Judgment Commentary’s “Mounting the six dragons in their time to drive through Heaven” and “Standing foremost among all things” both speak principally of the ruler’s Way.
- **Kun (2) · 坤.** The six in the second place is principal. Kun is Earth’s Way, and the second images Earth; Kun is the minister’s Way, and the second is the minister’s position. It is also yielding, compliant, centered, and correct. With these four qualities complete, it attains the purity of Kun’s virtue and is therefore principal. The Judgment’s “Going first, one loses the way; following, one finds a lord,” “finding companions,” and “losing companions” all speak principally of the minister’s Way.
- **Difficulty at the Beginning (3) · 屯.** The initial nine and the nine in the fifth place are principal, for the hexagram has only these two yang lines. The initial nine below is a lord capable of bringing peace to the people; the fifth nine above can establish such lords to bring them peace.
- **Unformed Understanding (4) · 蒙.** The nine in the second place and six in the fifth are principal. The second nine has firm, centered virtue, and the fifth six answers it. The second nine below is a teacher capable of instructing others; the fifth six above can honor the teacher so that others may be taught.
- **Waiting (5) · 需.** The nine in the fifth place is principal. All affairs require waiting; the kingly Way especially requires time to reach completion. The Judgment Commentary’s “Occupying Heaven’s position through correctness and centeredness” refers to the fifth.
- **Contention (6) · 訟.** The nine in the fifth place is principal. The other lines are disputants; the fifth nine hears and judges their disputes. The Judgment Commentary’s “It benefits one to see the great person: centeredness and correctness are honored” likewise refers to the fifth.
- **The Army (7) · 師.** The nine in the second place and six in the fifth are principal. The second nine below is the mature leader; the fifth six above can employ that leader.
- **Holding Together (8) · 比.** The nine in the fifth place is principal: the hexagram’s only yang occupies the honored position, and those above and below attach themselves to it.
- **Small Restraint (9) · 小畜.** The six in the fourth place constitutes the hexagram; the nine in the fifth governs it. The fourth six is the one yin restraining yang. Hence the Judgment Commentary says, “The yielding attains its position, and those above and below respond to it.” The fifth nine joins its purpose to complete the restraint. Hence “Firmness is centered, and its purpose is carried out.”
- **Treading (10) · 履.** The six in the third place constitutes the hexagram; the nine in the fifth governs it. The third six, one yielding line treading among many firm ones, encounters much danger and apprehension: this gives the hexagram its name. The occupant of the honored position should especially keep danger and apprehension continually in mind. Thus the fifth nine’s statement says “Steadfastness brings danger,” while the Judgment Commentary says, “Firm, centered, and correct, treading the sovereign’s position without distress.”
- **Peace (11) · 泰.** The nine in the second place and six in the fifth are principal. Peace means that upper and lower communicate and share a purpose. The second nine fulfills the minister’s Way in communicating upward; the fifth six fulfills the ruler’s Way in communicating downward. Both constitute the hexagram, and both govern it.
- **Obstruction (12) · 否.** The six in the second place and nine in the fifth are principal. Obstruction means that upper and lower do not communicate. The second six finds success within obstruction, gathering in its virtue and avoiding difficulty. The fifth nine brings obstruction to rest, changing Obstruction into Peace. Thus the second six constitutes the hexagram, while the fifth nine governs it.
- **Fellowship (13) · 同人.** The six in the second place and nine in the fifth are principal. The second six, the one yin, can unite with the many yang, and the fifth nine answers it. Hence the Judgment Commentary says, “The yielding attains its position and the center, and answers Qian.”
- **Great Possession (14) · 大有.** The six in the fifth place is principal. Inwardly open and occupying the honored position, it can possess the many yang. Hence the Judgment Commentary says, “The yielding attains the honored position, the great center, and those above and below respond to it.”
- **Modesty (15) · 謙.** The nine in the third place is principal. The hexagram’s only yang is appropriately placed within the lower body: this is the image of modesty. Its line statement therefore agrees with the hexagram statement. The Commentary says, “The third often brings misfortune,” yet this line is especially fortunate.
- **Enthusiasm (16) · 豫.** The nine in the fourth place is principal. It is the hexagram’s only yang and occupies an upper position; through it the hexagram becomes Enthusiasm. Hence the Judgment Commentary says, “The firm receives response, and its purpose is carried out.”
- **Following (17) · 隨.** The initial nine and nine in the fifth place are principal. The hexagram becomes Following because firmness can place itself beneath yieldingness. Both the initial and fifth lines are firm beneath yielding lines; hence both are principal.
- **Decay (18) · 蠱.** The six in the fifth place is principal. All the lines undertake the work of correcting decay, but only at the fifth is the task accomplished. Thus all the other lines contain warnings, while the fifth alone says, “Through this, praise.”
- **Approach (19) · 臨.** The initial nine and nine in the second place are principal: these are the Judgment Commentary’s “Firmness gradually grows.”
- **Viewing (20) · 觀.** The nine in the fifth place and top nine are principal: these are the Judgment Commentary’s “The great object of viewing stands above.”
- **Biting Through (21) · 噬嗑.** The six in the fifth place is principal: this is the Judgment Commentary’s “The yielding attains the center and moves upward.”
- **Adornment (22) · 賁.** The six in the second place and top nine are principal: these are the Judgment Commentary’s “The yielding comes to adorn the firm” and “The firm ascends to adorn the yielding.”
- **Splitting Apart (23) · 剝.** The top nine is principal. Though yin strips away yang, yang ultimately cannot be stripped away altogether; hence it is the principal line.
- **Return (24) · 復.** The initial nine is principal: it is what the Judgment Commentary calls “The firm returns.”
- **Without Falsity (25) · 无妄.** The initial nine and nine in the fifth place are principal. The initial nine is the beginning of yang’s movement, like the first stirring of a sincere heart. The fifth nine is the purity of Qian’s virtue, like perfect sincerity that never ceases. Thus the Judgment Commentary’s “Firmness comes from outside and becomes principal within” refers to the first; “Firm and centered, it receives response” refers to the fifth.
- **Great Restraint (26) · 大畜.** The six in the fifth place and top nine are principal: these are the Judgment Commentary’s “The firm is above, and the worthy are honored.”
- **Nourishment (27) · 頤.** The six in the fifth place and top nine are likewise principal: these are the Judgment Commentary’s “Nourishing the worthy, and thereby reaching the myriad people.”
- **Great Excess (28) · 大過.** The nines in the second and fourth places are principal. The second nine is firm and centered without excess; the fourth nine is the ridgepole that does not bend.
- **Repeated Danger (29) · 坎.** The two yang lines, second and fifth, are principal, especially the fifth: water flows when its accumulation is full.
- **Clinging (30) · 離.** The two yin lines, second and fifth, are principal, especially the second: fire is bright as it begins to blaze.
- **Influence (31) · 咸.** The nine in the fourth place occupies the heart’s position. Since the heart governs influence, the fourth is principal. Yet the fifth nine occupies the back’s position: it is Gen within Influence, stillness within responsiveness. This is movement that can remain still; therefore the fifth is especially principal.
- **Constancy (32) · 恒.** Constancy means the enduring; what is centered endures. Only the second and fifth are centered, and the yielding centeredness of the fifth six is less excellent than the firm centeredness of the second nine. Thus the second is principal.
- **Retreat (33) · 遯.** The two yin lines make the hexagram Retreat, so the first and second constitute it. Yet only the nine in the fifth place handles the situation with complete goodness; the fifth nine therefore governs it. Hence the Judgment Commentary says, “Firmness occupies its proper place and receives response, moving with the time.”
- **Great Strength (34) · 大壯.** Its strength derives from the four yang lines. The nine in the fourth place stands at their summit, and is therefore principal.
- **Advance (35) · 晉.** The hexagram is constituted by brightness emerging above the earth. The six in the fifth place governs Li and occupies the place of Heaven’s midcourse; thus the fifth is principal. Hence the Judgment Commentary says, “The yielding advances and moves upward.”
- **Darkening of the Light (36) · 明夷.** The hexagram is constituted by the sun entering the earth. The top six is the accumulated thickness of earth that darkens another’s light; it is the constituting line. The sixes in the second and fifth places both hold to centered, compliant virtue: their light is darkened by another, and they govern the hexagram. Hence the Judgment Commentary says, “King Wen practiced this” and “Jizi practiced this.”
- **The Family (37) · 家人.** The nine in the fifth place and six in the second are principal. Hence the Judgment Commentary says, “The woman is correctly placed within; the man correctly placed without.”
- **Opposition (38) · 睽.** The six in the fifth place and nine in the second are principal. Hence the Judgment Commentary says, “The yielding advances and moves upward, attaining the center and answering the firm.”
- **Limping (39) · 蹇.** The nine in the fifth place is principal. Hence the Judgment Commentary says, “Going attains the center.” The great person mentioned in the Judgment is precisely the fifth.
- **Release (40) · 解.** The nine in the second place and six in the fifth are principal. The Judgment Commentary’s “Going gains the multitude” refers to the fifth; “Then attaining the center” refers to the second.
- **Decrease (41) · 損.** The hexagram means decreasing the upper stroke of the lower trigram and increasing the upper stroke of the upper trigram. Thus the six in the third place and top nine constitute it. Yet when the lower is decreased to increase the upper, the beneficiary is the ruler. The six in the fifth place therefore governs the hexagram.
- **Increase (42) · 益.** The hexagram means decreasing the lower stroke of the upper trigram and increasing the lower stroke of the lower trigram. Thus the six in the fourth place and initial nine constitute it. Yet in decreasing the upper to increase the lower, the ruler bestows and the minister receives. The nine in the fifth place and six in the second therefore govern it.
- **Breakthrough (43) · 夬.** Its meaning arises from the one yin reaching its extreme above; the top six therefore constitutes it. Yet the five yang decisively remove yin, and the fifth stands highest among them and holds the honored position. The nine in the fifth place therefore governs it.
- **Coming to Meet (44) · 姤.** Its meaning arises from one yin being born below; the initial six therefore constitutes it. All five yang are responsible for restraining yin, but only the second and fifth have firm, centered virtue. One restrains it through immediate proximity; the other from the honored position above. The nines in the fifth and second places therefore govern it.
- **Gathering (45) · 萃.** The nine in the fifth place is principal, with the fourth nine secondary. These are the hexagram’s only two yang lines; occupying high positions, they are where the many yin gather.
- **Pushing Upward (46) · 升.** The six in the fifth place is principal. The Judgment Commentary says, “The yielding ascends with the time,” and the fifth six has ascended to the most honored station. Yet ascent must begin below. The hexagram images wood growing within the earth, and its initial six governs Xun: it is the root of the wood. Thus the initial six is also a constituting line.
- **Oppression (47) · 困.** The nines in the second and fifth places are principal. The hexagram means that firmness is concealed: the second and fifth have firm, centered virtue, yet both are covered by yin. Both therefore constitute the hexagram, and both govern it.
- **The Well (48) · 井.** The nine in the fifth place is principal. The well accomplishes its work through water, and the fifth nine governs Kan. The well means nourishing the people, and the fifth nine is the ruler who nourishes them.
- **Revolution (49) · 革.** The nine in the fifth place is principal. Holding the honored position gives authority to institute change; firmness, centeredness, and correctness enable it to accomplish that change well. Hence its statement says, “The great person changes like a tiger.”
- **The Cauldron (50) · 鼎.** The six in the fifth place and top nine are principal. The cauldron means nourishing the worthy, and the fifth six honors the worthiness of the top nine. Their image is the mutual fitting of the cauldron’s lifting bar and ears.
- **Shock (51) · 震.** Its two yang lines are principal in the broad sense; yet shock is yang moving below. Thus the fourth is not the governing focus: the first is.
- **Keeping Still (52) · 艮.** Its two yang lines are likewise principal in the broad sense; yet keeping still is yang stopping above. Thus the third is not the governing focus: the top is.[^p-governor-variants]
- **Gradual Progress (53) · 漸.** Its meaning is a woman marrying. Among the lines, only the second six corresponds with the fifth in a way that fits this image; therefore the second six is principal. Yet Gradual Progress also means advance. The fifth nine has advanced to a high station and possesses firm, centered virtue; thus it too is principal.
- **The Marrying Maiden (54) · 歸妹.** Its meaning is a woman going to marriage on her own initiative, a quality that is not good. Hence the Judgment Commentary says, “Nothing is beneficial: the yielding rides above the firm.” The third six and top six therefore constitute the hexagram. Yet the fifth six occupies the honored position and associates downward, reversing what is not good into goodness and transforming misfortune into good fortune. It therefore governs the hexagram.[^p-marrying-lines]
- **Abundance (55) · 豐.** The six in the fifth place is principal. The Judgment says, “The king reaches it. Do not be anxious. Be like the sun at midday.” The fifth six occupies the king’s position; yielding yet centered, it has the virtue of the sun at midday.
- **The Wanderer (56) · 旅.** The six in the fifth place is likewise principal. The Judgment Commentary says, “The yielding attains the center in the outer body,” and “Stopping and clinging to brightness.” The fifth’s place in the outer body images wandering abroad; its central position, governing Li, images attaining the center and clinging to brightness.
- **Penetration (57) · 巽.** Although its two yin lines are principal in its constitution, among yin trigrams only Li takes yin as its governing focus, because there yin is centered. Xun’s two yin lines constitute the hexagram but cannot govern it. The governing line is the nine in the fifth place: repeating commands and carrying out affairs requires the honored position. Thus the Judgment Commentary’s “The firm submits through centeredness and correctness, and its purpose is carried out” refers to the fifth.
- **Joy (58) · 兌.** Its two yin lines likewise constitute the hexagram but cannot govern it. The governing lines are the second and fifth. Hence the Judgment Commentary says, “Firm within and yielding without, joy is beneficial through steadfastness.”
- **Dispersion (59) · 渙.** The nine in the fifth place is principal. Gathering up what is scattered throughout the world requires the honored position. Yet the second nine within strengthens the foundation, and the fourth six supports the fifth to accomplish the work; these too are weighty in the hexagram’s meaning. Hence the Judgment Commentary says, “The firm comes and is not exhausted; the yielding attains its place without and unites with what is above.”
- **Limitation (60) · 節.** The nine in the fifth place is likewise principal. Establishing institutions to regulate the world can be done only by one who holds the honored position and possesses virtue. Hence the Judgment Commentary says, “Limiting from the proper position, communicating through centeredness and correctness.”
- **Inner Sincerity (61) · 中孚.** The hexagram is constituted by emptiness within, so the third and fourth sixes constitute it. Yet sincerity takes its meaning from inner substance, so the second and fifth nines govern it. As for sincerity transforming the state, that is the work of the honored position; hence the hexagram’s chief governing line is the fifth.
- **Small Excess (62) · 小過.** The second and fifth are principal. Yielding yet centered, they meet a time of excess without themselves going too far.
- **After Completion (63) · 既濟.** The six in the second place is principal. After Completion has good fortune at the beginning and disorder at the end. The second six stands in the inner body, precisely at the time of initial good fortune. Hence the Judgment Commentary says, “Good fortune at the beginning: the yielding attains the center.”
- **Before Completion (64) · 未濟.** The six in the fifth place is principal. Before Completion begins in disorder and ends in order. The fifth six stands in the outer body, precisely when ordered conditions are opening. Hence the Judgment Commentary says, “Before Completion has success: the yielding attains the center.”

**Imperial Compilers.** All these meanings can be inferred from the Judgment Commentary and line statements. Broadly, the Changes is a book for accomplishing great undertakings, and such accomplishment must rest with people who possess both virtue and position. Hence the fifth is especially often principal. Occasionally, the meaning of the time prevents the fifth from being taken as the king’s position, but this concerns only a few hexagrams. Outside the fifth, statements mentioning a king do not make the line itself king: they speak in relation to the fifth. Following’s top says, “The king presents offerings at the Western Mountain,” because it is bound to the fifth. Increase’s second says, “The king presents offerings to the Supreme Deity,” because it corresponds with the fifth. Pushing Upward’s fourth says, “The king presents offerings at Mount Qi,” because it supports the fifth. In each case the line’s virtue matches the time, so the king selects and employs it to answer the intention of spiritual illumination. Some top lines also continue the fifth’s meaning and bring it to completion. The Army’s top says, “The great ruler issues commands”: the fifth sent out the army to settle disorder, and here success is reported. Clinging’s top says, “The king employs it in a punitive expedition”: the fifth labored anxiously for good government, and here the root of disorder is removed. Both carry forward the fifth’s meaning and speak of the resulting achievement. This relationship between fifth and top is especially common in the Changes; it likewise does not make the top line itself king.[^p-kings-offerings]

## Translator’s Endnotes

The base is the repository’s unchanged preliminary juan. Selective comparison used [the Siku transcription, revision 759600](<https://zh.wikisource.org/w/index.php?title=御纂周易折中_(四庫全書本)/卷首&oldid=759600>), the separately transmitted Zhouyi Zhengyi preface identified below, and historical page images [0129](https://www.eee-learning.com/eeebook/middleyi/0129.png) and [0130](https://www.eee-learning.com/eeebook/middleyi/0130.png). Supported corrections, contextual punctuation, disagreements between commentators, and unresolved readings are distinguished. This is not systematic leaf-by-leaf facsimile collation.

[^p-transmission]: The accounts of authorship and transmission are the attributed scholars’ historical arguments. They are not harmonized into one chronology. The same name may appear under a courtesy name: Zheng Kangcheng is Zheng Xuan; Wang Fusi is Wang Bi; Han Kangbo is Han Bo; Cheng Yichuan is Cheng Yi. Fuxi’s several written names are rendered consistently as Fuxi. The Chinese source remains available for the precise transmitted forms.

[^p-three-lost]: The Sui History quotation says “three chapters of the Discussion of the Trigrams.” This can designate the final three Wings beginning with that text—the Discussion, Sequence, and Miscellaneous Hexagrams—rather than three internal chapters of the extant Discussion. The English retains the compressed wording and does not certify the reported discovery as a historical fact.

[^p-liu-yi]: Both consulted transcriptions end this excerpt abruptly with 其子軼, “his son Yi.” The English preserves the surviving identification without supplying a claim about his teaching. The sentence may be truncated; no lost continuation is silently reconstructed.

[^p-two-jings]: The Jing Fang who first taught Liangqiu He and the Jing Fang taught by Jiao Yanshou are presented as separate links in the source’s genealogy. The shared written name is retained; the passage should not automatically be read as one man appearing at two incompatible stages. “Academician” translates 博士, an appointed classical specialist, not a modern doctorate.

[^p-ancient-script]: 古文 means the ancient-script textual tradition, not “classical Chinese” as opposed to modern Chinese. 中古文 here is understood as the ancient-script copy held in the palace, not “medieval writing.” 章句 denotes exposition organized by chapters and sentences; the absence of it does not mean the text had no divisions at all.

[^p-three-four]: The source explicitly explains why one genealogy counts three sages while another counts four: it subsumes the Duke of Zhou’s work under his father, King Wen. The competing claims about Fuxi, Shennong, Yu, and King Wen are left distinct. Kong’s appeal to the thirteen invention accounts should be read alongside Zhu Xi’s later warning that resemblance to a hexagram does not prove that an invention was made by consulting that hexagram.

[^p-appended-word]: 繫辭 can mean words appended to figures as well as the separately titled Appended Statements. In Kong Yingda’s discussion of Fuxi and the later composition of the oracle, “appended words” is generic; translating every occurrence as the title of the Wing would confuse the authorship argument.

[^p-layout-history]: The arguments about Fei Zhi, Zheng Xuan, and Wang Bi concern where commentary is physically placed relative to the oracle. The compilers preserve differing accounts of how the twelve-part arrangement was altered. The claims about authorial intention and ancient book arrangement remain attributed claims, not conclusions independently established by this translation.

[^p-calendar-repair]: Shui Yuquan’s first date is 元豐王戌 in the base. The parallel reads 元豐壬戌, Yuanfeng renxu, which supplies the valid sexagenary designation (1082). The other dated stages are Jianzhong Jingguo xinsi (1101), Shaoxing xinwei (1151), and Chunxi renyin (1182). The base’s 巳 in xinsi is retained against the parallel’s erroneous 已.

[^p-heading-repair]: The base heading has 精媼, an apparent character error. The parallel has 精緼, the subtle riches or inner content of the Way of the Changes. The English follows the latter; the original Chinese is unchanged.

[^p-time-position]: 時 means the configuration of circumstances and its appropriate moment, not clock time alone. 位 is a line’s position and, by analogy, its station or office. 應 is correspondence, normally between lines one and four, two and five, and three and six; 比 is adjacency. 承 means supporting the line above, and 乘 riding above the line below. Whether these relations are beneficial remains dependent on the hexagram and situation.

[^p-forgetting]: Wang Bi’s “forgetting” words and images means passing through them to what they disclose, not erasing the book or treating every interpretation as interchangeable. His claim is later challenged and qualified by Zhu Xi in this same juan. In 尋言以現象 the base has 現; the parallel has 觀, “observe,” which the English follows.

[^p-extreme-positions]: Wang Bi here denies fixed yin–yang assignments to the first and top positions. Zhu Xi later rejects that view, and the compilers’ final Principles of Interpretation recast it as a claim about official station rather than yin–yang position. Those statements are not the same. The translation preserves their disagreement instead of silently making Wang say what the later writers prefer.

[^p-three-meanings]: The three senses attributed to 易 are ease and simplicity, change, and the unchanging. They are the source’s semantic and philosophical account, not three mutually interchangeable English translations. “Substance and function” elsewhere renders 體用: what constitutes something and how it operates, not two separate substances.

[^p-shao-change]: Shao’s sequence deliberately juxtaposes origination and good fortune, success and misfortune, and then change and response within one another. Both transcriptions have 亨則凶, “success leads to misfortune”; it is not silently changed to the more immediately agreeable “success leads to good fortune.” The passage distinguishes phases of a process rather than offering a universal equation between success and disaster.

[^p-cheng-attribution]: The source often gives only 程子, “Master Cheng,” without identifying which Cheng brother is intended. That form is retained in the English. The preface to Cheng’s Commentary identifies Cheng Yi through its work, but the unqualified attribution should not be used to assign every other quotation automatically to Yi rather than Hao.

[^p-repunctuation]: The base’s punctuation divides 既具聖人 as though “the sage” completed the preceding clause. The parallel has the same continuous words. The English repunctuates: the meaning has already been established; the sage then introduces another meaning. This is a punctuation decision, not a recovered textual variant.

[^p-yin-ethics]: This explicit distinction matters for reading the work: yin does not invariably mean an ethically inferior person. The source calls it petty when it harms yang, noble when it helps yang complete things. Likewise 利, benefit or advantage, is not condemned in itself; its relation to 義, rightness, is decisive. The later gendered and political analogies have not been erased, but neither should they replace this stated qualification.

[^p-ultimate]: “Supreme Ultimate” translates 太極, the comprehensive source or organizing principle; 無極 is rendered “without limit.” The passage does not posit a separate creator deity. “Substance and function have one source” and “the manifest and subtle admit no gap” are preserved as assertions within the tradition, not translated into claims about modern physics.

[^p-images-open]: Zhu Xi distinguishes an image’s determinate wording from the open range of situations to which it can apply. “Hypothetical” here translates 虛說 or 虛設: the image is not pinned to one actual event or social rank. It does not mean that the text has no constraints or that every reading is equally justified.

[^p-image-methods]: The source distinguishes the historical derivation of an image from its interpretive use. Zhu criticizes both forced attempts to reconstruct every derivation and the assumption that all images are arbitrary illustrations. His willingness to leave a derivation unknown is part of the argument, not a gap supplied by the translator. The techniques named are overlapping trigrams, hexagram transformation, five-phase correlations, stem assignments, and manifest-and-hidden configurations.

[^p-divining-blocks]: 環珓 refers to paired divining blocks tossed for an answer. The Forest of Fire Pearls (火珠林) represents a method that interprets line configurations without depending on the received line statements. Zhu’s comparison is a historical conjecture about early practice, not a claim that the two procedures are identical. His example of Difficulty at the Beginning changing to Holding Together should be compared with the differently assessed examples in Juan Twenty.

[^p-steadfastness]: “There is no good fortune in not being steadfast” expresses Zhu’s ethical reading of 貞. It does not say that every occurrence of 貞 is followed by 吉: the oracle also contains warnings such as 貞凶. He argues that rightful steadiness is necessary, not that blind persistence in any course guarantees a favorable outcome.

[^p-fourth-trigrams]: Xu Heng’s “fourth lines of Qian, Gen, and Kun” refers to hexagrams with those trigram bodies in the upper position, not to three examples chosen from the whole book without qualification. Qian’s fourth line is firm, Gen’s yielding beneath a firm top, and Kun’s yielding throughout. His judgments are interpretive generalizations; correspondence and moral worth are not automatically identical.

[^p-yuan]: Hu Bingwen explicitly reads 元 as greatness of goodness, not merely numerical or worldly magnitude. His “one in a hundred” and “one in a thousand” emphasize rarity; they are not statistical estimates. The final insistence on no lapse even for a breath gives the ethical force of his interpretation.

[^p-three-readings]: Zhu distinguishes the Changes of Fuxi, the words of King Wen and the Duke of Zhou, and Confucius’s commentaries. The same 元亨利貞 is first read as a casting formula, “great success; benefit in steadfastness,” and then as four virtues. At “great success,” the base has 大亭; the parallel and historical page image 0129 read 大亨. This is an attested correction, not a change in Zhu’s interpretive position.

[^p-several-sages]: The base, parallel compilation transcription, and inspected historical page image 0130 all have 幾聖, “several sages.” A separate transmission of Kong Yingda’s original preface reads 九聖, “nine sages” ([Zhouyi Zhengyi preface](<https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/周易注疏_(四庫全書本)/序>)). The English preserves the compilation’s intelligible wording rather than replacing it with that other transmission. The river dragon and wounded qilin invoke traditional accounts of Fuxi’s diagrams and Confucius’s final work; the gold mirror and pearl bag are images of cultural or cosmological order.

[^p-calendar-schools]: “Six days and seven parts” means six days plus seven eightieths of a day within the calendrical model discussed here, not 6.7 days. The twelve “sovereign hexagrams” are the monthly ruling figures; the four principal seasonal figures are treated as doubled trigrams, hence twenty-four lines. Zhu is criticizing the attempt to combine distinct arrangements, not offering this paragraph as an agreed casting procedure. His reference to the two additional appraisals 踦 and 贏 concerns the supplementary reckoning in Yang Xiong’s Supreme Mystery.

[^p-lineage]: The asserted secret transmission through outsiders and alchemical practice is Zhu Xi’s claim. It remains distinct from the compilers’ qualified discussion of the diagrams’ origins in Juan Nineteen. “Master Cheng” is not expanded to Cheng Yi where the source leaves the attribution unqualified.

[^p-su-and-wang]: The Elder Su is Su Xun; Dongpo is Su Shi. In Wang Yinglin’s following judgment, “Yan” is Wang Yan, distinguished from Wang Bi and He Yan. These are historical polemics preserved in the collection, not verdicts endorsed by the translator. The praise of Cheng’s literary manner is retained beside criticism of his interpretive restrictions.

[^p-compilers]: The final Principles of Interpretation is the compilers’ systematic exposition. Its model of ruler, minister, worthy helper, and private faction is preserved rather than replaced by modern psychological roles. These are interpretive analogies, not a claim that every consultation concerns government.

[^p-correct-centered]: “Correctness” has both a formal positional sense and an ethical sense. Formally, yang belongs to odd positions and yin to even ones; centeredness belongs to the second and fifth. The assertion that centeredness is never without correctness concerns appropriate conduct, not the false geometrical claim that every centered line occupies a position of matching parity.

[^p-sixteen-groups]: Each of the four configurations discussed here fixes two of six line polarities, leaving four free: each therefore occurs in sixteen hexagrams. The lists have been checked against the six-line figures. “All favorable” is the compilers’ reading of the possibilities disclosed by these lines, including conditions, warnings, and freedom from blame; it does not mean that every quoted statement literally contains 吉 or promises unconditional success.

[^p-oracle-excerpts]: These are the compilers’ short citations, sometimes abridged, paraphrased, or drawn from the Images rather than the oracle proper. They are not silently expanded into the full line statements. The base’s 安節亭 is read 安節亨, “ease in limitation; success,” with the parallel transcription. The textual and interpretive discussions under the corresponding numbered juans remain applicable.

[^p-revolution-quotation]: The compilers here quote Revolution as 已日乃孚征吉, “only after the day has passed is there trust; going forward brings good fortune.” Both consulted transcriptions have 孚. The second-line oracle instead has 己日乃革, “on one’s own day, then revolution,” while the hexagram Judgment contains 已日乃孚. This apparent conflation is retained and identified, not silently corrected to the line oracle.

[^p-governing-two]: 成卦之主 is the constituting line: the feature through which the hexagram takes on its distinctive meaning. 主卦之主 is the governing line: the line qualified to direct the situation through virtue, timing, and position. A harmful line can constitute a hexagram without governing it beneficially. 卦主 alone is rendered “principal line” where the text has not yet distinguished the two, or where both senses are in view. The sixty-four explanations below preserve the source’s own distinctions, including plural principal lines.

[^p-governor-variants]: In the governing-line list, After Completion’s 初言, “initial words,” is corrected to 初吉, “initial good fortune,” from the parallel transcription. Keeping Still’s 亦為二陽為主 is read with the parallel 亦以二陽為主. These repairs clarify the transmitted statements without changing the selected lines. Penetration’s distinction between yin that constitutes a figure and the line qualified to govern it should not be flattened into a rule that yin can never govern a hexagram.

[^p-marrying-lines]: The Marrying Maiden entry selects the third six and top six as constituting lines and invokes “the yielding rides above the firm.” The third six does sit immediately above a firm second line, but the top six sits above a yielding fifth. The selection is retained; the quotation by itself does not mechanically establish both lines through direct adjacency. Neither consulted transcription supplies a different line designation.

[^p-kings-offerings]: 亨 in the three ritual citations is understood as 享, “present offerings,” as the context requires. The source’s 益之二日 is read 益之二曰, “Increase’s second says,” with the parallel. The argument distinguishes the king referred to by a line statement from the person represented by that line; it is not claiming that every appearance of 王 identifies the line itself as sovereign.
