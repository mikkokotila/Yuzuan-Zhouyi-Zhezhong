## 8. Bi 比 — Holding Together

<!-- Source: https://www.eee-learning.com/book/4553 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:001 -->

![Bi: Kun below, Kan above](../assets/eee-learning/208707197f03-yi08b.png) **Bi 比 — Holding Together** — Kun below; Kan above.[^j02-bi]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:002 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Concerning Bi, the *Sequence of the Hexagrams* says: “Where there is a multitude, there must be something to which it joins; therefore The Army is followed by Holding Together.” Bi means to draw close and lend support. Human beings must draw close to one another and help one another before they can find security. Thus, once there is a multitude, there must be something to which it joins. This is why Bi follows The Army. The hexagram has Kan above and Kun below. In terms of the two constituent figures, it is water upon the earth. Nothing touches and adheres more closely, without any interval, than water upon the earth; hence Holding Together. Moreover, all the lines are yin except the fifth, which occupies the ruler's place with the firmness of yang. It is the one to which the others draw close and attach themselves, while the one above also draws close to those below. Hence Holding Together.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:002 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:003 -->

### Holding Together: good fortune. Consult the oracle again. Fundamental goodness, enduring constancy, and steadfastness in what is right: no blame. Those without peace are now coming. For the latecomer, misfortune.[^j02-repeat-divination]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:004 -->

**Original Meaning.** Bi means to draw close and lend support. Nine in the fifth place, with the firmness of yang, occupies the center of the upper trigram and attains correctness. The five yin lines above and below join with it and follow it. This is the image of one person caring for the myriad states, and all within the four seas looking up to that one person. Thus, someone who obtains this hexagram through divination should be one to whom others draw close and lend their support. Yet one must divine again to examine oneself: only with the virtues of fundamental goodness, lasting constancy, and correctness firmly maintained can one bear the allegiance of the multitude without blame. Those who have not yet joined and therefore remain unsettled will also all come to give their allegiance. But should anyone delay still further and arrive late, the association here will already be secure, while that person's arrival will be too late: misfortune. Someone seeking to join with another should consider the same principles from the opposite side.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:005 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Holding Together is a way to good fortune. That people draw close and join with one another is in itself a way to good fortune. Thus the *Miscellaneous Hexagrams* says, “Holding Together is gladness; The Army, anxiety.” But people must have a proper way of joining. If they do not follow that way, there will be regret and blame. One must therefore investigate thoroughly and judge whom it is right to join, and then join with that person. Here “divining” means judging and considering; it does not mean using milfoil stalks or a tortoise shell. If the one with whom one joins possesses “fundamental goodness, enduring constancy, and steadfastness in what is right,” there is no blame. Fundamental goodness means possessing the Way of a ruler and leader; enduring constancy means being capable of lasting; steadfastness means attaining the correct Way. Those above must have these three qualities in joining with those below; those below must seek these three qualities in following those above. Then there is no blame. People unable to secure their own peace are now coming to seek association. Once they find the right association, they can preserve their security. While they are unsettled, they should indeed seek it urgently. But if someone stands alone, relying on himself, and is slow and late in seeking to join, even a man of strength meets misfortune. If even such a man meets misfortune, how much more the yielding and weak! *Fu* designates a man who stands firm. The *Tradition* says, “Zinan is a man,” and again, “This is to say that I am no man.” Among all who live between Heaven and Earth, none can sustain themselves without drawing close to others and receiving their support. Even the strongest cannot stand alone. The Way of Holding Together depends on two wills seeking one another. If they do not seek one another, there is estrangement. The ruler cherishes and cares for those below; those below draw close and lend support to the one above. It is the same with kin, friends, and neighbors. Therefore, those above and below should unite their intentions and follow one another. Without the intention to seek one another, separation and misfortune follow. Generally, people unite when they seek one another and become estranged when they hold back against one another. Holding back means that each waits for the other and neither will be first. There is certainly a proper way for people to draw close; nevertheless, the will to join must not be allowed to lag.[^j02-late-man]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:005 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:006 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:006 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:007 -->

**Guo Yong:** Among hexagrams with a single yang line, only The Army and Holding Together have it in its appropriate place. When it occupies the ruler's place, the hexagram is Holding Together; when the minister's place, The Army.[^j02-bi-positions]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:007 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:008 -->

**Feng Yi:** Gathering and Holding Together share the same lower trigram, compliant Kun, and their upper trigrams, water and marsh, are not far apart in meaning. But Gathering has a nine in the fourth place, which presents an image of divided authority. Thus “fundamental goodness, enduring constancy, and steadfastness” is said of its fifth line. In Holding Together there is no one below who divides the authority, so these words are said of the hexagram as a whole. Each usage has its reason.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:008 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:009 -->

**Hu Yigui:** Of the sixty-four hexagrams, only Unformed Understanding and Holding Together speak expressly of divination. The former values the first consultation; the latter, renewed consultation. In opening unformed understanding, one should attend to the undivided sincerity of the first inquiry. In joining openly, one should carry out renewed inquiry with care and discrimination. This accounts for the difference.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:009 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:010 -->

**Hu Bingwen:** In “consult the oracle again,” the *Original Meaning* reads *yuan* as in *yuancan*, a second raising of silkworms, *yuanmiao*, a second ancestral temple, and *yuantian* 原田: in each instance it glosses the word as “again.” “Good fortune,” “no blame,” and “misfortune” are all oracular statements. “Good fortune” is the prognostication for those above and below joining with one another: it speaks comprehensively. “No blame” is the prognostication for the one whom others join; “misfortune,” for the one joining another: these speak separately. The hexagram statements of Unformed Understanding and Holding Together expressly introduce the word “divining” to show a general rule for the diviner. Those who obtain the statement of Unformed Understanding can apply it either as people seeking to have their understanding opened or as people opening the understanding of others. Those who obtain this statement can apply it either as people whom others join or as people seeking to join. Everything depends on their circumstances and what they hold within. In Unformed Understanding, one questions another; without singleness of inquiry, there is no undivided intent. In Holding Together, one questions what lies within oneself; without renewed inquiry, there is no thorough examination. “Those without peace are now coming” refers to the four yin lines below. Those who come do so of themselves; those who are late are late of themselves. I ask only whether I am fit to be joined. Whether others come to join me is not what I ask. This is precisely the sovereign's Way of great impartiality, and it constitutes the “open joining” of nine in the fifth place.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:010 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:011 -->

### First Six: Join with sincerity. No blame. Sincerity fills the earthen jar; in the end, further good fortune comes.[^j02-earthen-jar]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:011 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:012 -->

**Original Meaning.** At the beginning of Holding Together, trustworthiness is what matters; with it, there can be no blame. If it is full and substantial, there will also be further good fortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:012 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:013 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Initial six is the beginning of Holding Together. The Way of joining with one another rests upon sincerity and trustworthiness. If one is not trustworthy at heart but seeks closeness with others, who will associate with one? Thus, at the beginning of Holding Together, there must be inward sincerity before there can be no blame. *Fu* is trustworthiness within. Sincerity and trustworthiness fill the interior, like something filling an earthen jar to the brim. The jar is a plain, unadorned vessel. This means that if one is full and substantial within, like the filled jar, without adding external embellishment, further good fortune will eventually be brought to one. “Further” means something other than what is here: something from outside. If sincerity fills the interior, everyone will trust one. What need is there to adorn the exterior in order to seek association? When sincerity and trustworthiness are substantial within, even those beyond one's immediate circle will be moved to come and follow. Inward trustworthiness is the root of Holding Together.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:013 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:014 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:014 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:015 -->

**Zheng Ruxie:** The fifth is the ruler of Holding Together. The initial line is farthest from it and is not its corresponding partner. How, then, can it have the meaning of good fortune? The subtle beginning arises before one's response to things, and sincerity issues forth before one's intention has altered. What blame can there be in seeking association through trustworthiness? “Full” means filled throughout; the jar is an unadorned vessel. Though one's position is low and humble, one enlarges one's trustworthiness until it fills one's whole being. Then, despite being distant and not a corresponding partner, one will finally meet with a response and receive further good fortune. “Further good fortune” means receiving something one had not insisted on receiving.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:015 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:016 -->

**Hu Bingwen:** In dealings with others, the point of rest is trustworthiness. At the beginning of close association, sincerity and trustworthiness make it possible to join without blame. When that sincerity and trustworthiness become full and substantial, there is not merely an absence of blame, but further good fortune. Initial six does not correspond to the fifth, hence the words “something further.” Nine in the fourth place of Great Excess and initial nine of Inner Sincerity also speak of “something other.” There, the warning concerns turning one's heart elsewhere; here, the promise concerns good fortune arriving from elsewhere.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:016 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:017 -->

### Six in the Second Place: Join from within. Steadfastness in what is right brings good fortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:017 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:018 -->

**Original Meaning.** Yielding, compliant, centered, and correct, it responds above to nine in the fifth place. Holding Together the outer from within and attaining correctness is a way to good fortune. A diviner who is like this is correct and meets good fortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:018 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:019 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The second and fifth are proper corresponding partners. Both attain centeredness and correctness; they join through the Way of centeredness and correctness. The second occupies the inner trigram. “From within” means proceeding from oneself. Although choosing talent and employing it rests with the person above, dedicating oneself to the state must proceed from oneself. If one advances because one has found a ruler with whom one's Way accords, this is correct and auspicious. To answer the call from above through the Way of centeredness and correctness is to proceed from within, without losing oneself. Anxiously pressing for association is not the noble person's way of valuing oneself; it is to lose oneself.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:019 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:020 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:020 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:021 -->

**Liang Yin:** The second joins the fifth: it joins the outer from within. Whenever the text says “steadfastness brings good fortune,” it may describe a line whose character is already good, or warn a line that is not itself correct. Here the words describe the line's inherent goodness. Holding Together the outer from within attains correctness; therefore there is good fortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:021 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:022 -->

**Gu Jiaji:** Join through what one possesses within. In attaining prominence, do not change what one was in obscurity. This itself is correctness; hence good fortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:022 -->

### Six in the Third Place: Holding Together with the wrong people.[^j02-bi-third]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:023 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:024 -->

**Original Meaning.** Yin and yielding, neither centered nor correct, it supports, rides upon, and corresponds to lines that are all yin. This is the image of finding none of the right people to join. The prognostication is great misfortune; it is understood without being stated.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:024 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:025 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The third is neither centered nor correct, and those with whom it joins are likewise neither centered nor correct. The fourth is yin and yielding, but not centered; the second retains its corresponding connection yet joins the initial line. All fail in centeredness and correctness: they are the wrong people. The error of joining with the wrong people is plain. Regret and humiliation need not be stated; hence it is “lamentable.” That the second, though centered and correct, is here called one of the wrong people shows how meanings differ according to the circumstances in which they are taken.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:025 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:026 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:026 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:027 -->

**Wang Bi:** The fourth joins outward; the second corresponds to the fifth. Nearby, none gives it a congenial response; far away, it has no responsive partner. Those with whom it would join are none of them its intimates. Hence “joining with the wrong people.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:027 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:028 -->

**Classified Conversations of Master Zhu:** The initial line corresponds to the fourth and thus joins with the right person. The second corresponds to the fifth and likewise joins with the right person. Only the third corresponds to the top, which is the line of “joining without a head.” Therefore it is “joining with the wrong people.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:028 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:029 -->

**Zhao Yansu:** The initial line joins the fifth by being first; the second, through correspondence; the fourth, through support. Six in the third place has none of these three relationships and therefore will be unable to join the fifth.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:029 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:030 -->

### Six in the Fourth Place: Join outward. Steadfastness in what is right brings good fortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:030 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:031 -->

**Original Meaning.** Yielding occupies a yielding place and joins outward with nine in the fifth place. Attaining correctness is a way to good fortune. A diviner who is like this is correct and meets good fortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:031 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:032 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The fourth and initial lines do not respond to one another, while the fifth joins with the fourth. Holding Together outward with the fifth thus attains correctness and good fortune. Ruler and minister joining is correct; drawing close and associating is fitting. The fifth is yang and firm, centered and correct: it is worthy. It occupies the honored place: it is above. Drawing close to the worthy and following those above is the correct Way of Holding Together, hence “steadfastness brings good fortune.” A six in the fourth place also has the meaning of attaining correctness. Furthermore, when a person who is yin and yielding, and lacks centeredness, can join with someone worthy who is firm, clear, centered, and correct, that person attains correctness and good fortune. Again, in joining the worthy and following those above, one must follow the correct Way for there to be good fortune. These explanations require one another; only together do they give the full meaning.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:032 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:033 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:033 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:034 -->

**Yi Fu:** The *Changes* regards the upper trigram as outer and the lower as inner, yet each constituent figure also has its own inner and outer. The fourth and fifth belong to the same trigram, but the fourth is said to “join outward”: this too describes its joining the fifth.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:034 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:035 -->

**Li Guo:** The second and fourth both join the fifth. The second corresponds to the fifth and is within the hexagram, hence “joining from within.” The fourth supports the fifth and is in the outer part, hence “joining outward.” Outer and inner differ, but in finding the right person to join, their meaning is one. Therefore both are said to have “steadfastness and good fortune.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:035 -->

### Nine in the Fifth Place: Open joining. The king drives the hunt on three sides, letting the game ahead escape. The townspeople are not alerted. Good fortune.[^j02-hunt]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:036 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:037 -->

**Original Meaning.** The single yang occupies the honored place, firm and vigorous, centered and correct. All the hexagram's yin lines come to join it. It makes its joining open and free of partiality, like the Son of Heaven who does not complete the hunting enclosure but opens one side of the net: those who come are not refused; those who depart are not pursued. Hence the image of “driving on three sides, letting the game ahead escape,” and of “the townspeople not being alerted.” Even his own followers understand the intention of the one above and do not warn one another or take precautions to ensure that everything is caught. All this is a way to good fortune. If the diviner is like this, there is good fortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:037 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:038 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The fifth occupies the ruler's place, holds the center, and attains correctness. It fulfills the goodness of the Way of Holding Together. In joining with all under Heaven, a ruler need only make that Way manifest. To meet others with sincerity, extend to them the consideration one gives oneself, enact government and practice benevolence so that all under Heaven receive one's kindness and beneficence—this is how a ruler draws close and joins with the world. When this is so, who under Heaven would not join with the one above? But if a ruler displays petty acts of kindness, departs from the Way to solicit praise, and thereby seeks the allegiance of those below, the Way becomes narrow indeed. How could such a ruler gain the world's allegiance? Thus the sages take nine in the fifth place as fully realizing the correctness of Holding Together, and use the three-sided hunt as a likeness: “The king drives the hunt on three sides, letting the game ahead escape. The townspeople are not alerted. Good fortune.” The former kings considered that the seasonal hunts could not be abolished. Extending their benevolent hearts, they therefore established the rite of the three-sided drive—what the ritual text means by “the Son of Heaven does not complete the enclosure.” Cheng Tang's prayer over the net expresses this meaning. In the Son of Heaven's hunt, three sides are enclosed, while a way is left open ahead so that the animals can escape. Unable to bear the destruction of every creature, he displays the benevolence that cherishes life. Only those that do not heed the command are taken: those that do not go out but turn and enter instead. Birds and beasts that go forward escape; hence “letting the game ahead escape.” When a king makes the Way of Holding Together manifest, all under Heaven naturally come to join. Those who come are cared for; he does not fuss over little kindnesses in order to solicit attachment. It is like the three-sided hunt: animals that leave are allowed to go and are not pursued; those that come are taken. Such is the breadth of the kingly Way. His people live spaciously and at ease, without knowing who has brought it about. “The townspeople are not alerted: good fortune” means that he is utterly impartial, making no distinction between distant and near, intimate and remote. “Town” means the place where people dwell; it has this meaning throughout the *Changes*: the king's capital or the center of a lord's state. “Alerting” means making a prior arrangement. His treatment of everyone is one; he makes no special arrangement with those of his own town. In this way there is good fortune. The sages' government of all under Heaven through great impartiality and freedom from self-interest is visible in open joining. This is not only the ruler's Way of joining with the world: it applies broadly to all human association. Speaking of a minister's relation to a ruler, to exhaust one's loyalty and sincerity and devote all one's ability and strength is to make one's joining with the ruler manifest. Whether one is employed rests with the ruler. One must not flatter and accommodate in order to make the ruler attach himself to one. It is the same with friends: cultivate oneself and make one's intentions sincere in meeting them. Whether they become close to one rests with them. One must not use clever words, ingratiating expressions, crooked compliance, or careless agreement to solicit their attachment. With neighbors, kin, and people generally, it is always so. This is the meaning of driving the hunt on three sides and letting the game ahead escape.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:038 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:039 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:039 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:040 -->

**Classified Conversations of Master Zhu:** A question: “In Yichuan's explanation of ‘open joining: the king drives the hunt on three sides, letting the game ahead escape,’ he speaks of caring for those who come and not pursuing those who leave. Yet letting the game ahead escape and killing the animals that do not leave seem rather unlike what they are meant to illustrate. How is this?” The reply: “In the hunting rite, banners are set up to make a gateway and grass is cut to form a long enclosure. The hunters drive inward from the gateway. Animals that come toward us and pass out are all spared; only those driven inward are all caught. Thus the game ahead illustrates not pursuing those who leave, while what is caught illustrates receiving those who come. That is the general meaning; there is no reason why the details should match one by one. There is no need to doubt Yichuan's explanation of this phrase. It is only his explanation of ‘the townspeople are not alerted: good fortune’ that seems doubtful. I suspect the wording of the *Changes* does not mean this.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:040 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:041 -->

**He also said:** “‘The townspeople are not alerted’ is like ‘there is a report, but no sound.’ It means that there is naturally no need to alert one another. It also resembles ‘those going to market did not stop; those plowing did not change what they were doing.’”[^j02-no-alarm]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:041 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:042 -->

**Hu Bingwen:** The yin lines all say “join with it”: yin joins yang. The fifth says “open joining”: yang is the one joined by yin. The fifth lines of The Army and Holding Together both take an image from hunting. In The Army, “game in the field” means creatures that harm things; in Holding Together, “the game ahead that escapes” means creatures turning their backs on oneself. In The Army, seizing them is the king's righteousness. In Holding Together, being able to let them go is the king's benevolence.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:042 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:043 -->

**Liang Yin:** Nine in the fifth place is yang and firm, centered and correct, the ruler of Holding Together. Its yang firmness makes it clear rather than obscure; its centeredness and correctness make it impartial rather than self-serving. This is why its joining is open. In terms of the image, it is like a hunt in which one drives on three sides and lets the game ahead escape, refusing none who come and pursuing none who leave. This is the joining of the one above with those below: certainly it is open joining. Once the one above has found the Way of joining with those below, even the ruler's own followers understand that intention without needing to be told. This is the joining of those below with the one above: it too is open joining. Above and below join through the same manifest Way. How could there fail to be good fortune?

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:043 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:044 -->

**Lin Xiyuan:** “Open” contrasts with “hidden.” To be luminous, correct, and broad, without concealment, crookedness, obscurity, or narrowness, is to be open. To be concealed, crooked, obscure, and narrow, without such luminosity, correctness, and breadth, is to be hidden. The king's task is to be father and mother to all under Heaven, fostering life, nourishing, teaching, and guiding. He knows only what his allotted responsibility requires and fulfills its Way in action. Whether the people feel grateful is left to them. He does not fuss over displaying personal favors and petty kindnesses, violating the Way and soliciting praise so that the people will be close to him. How luminous, correct, and broad are such acts! How could they suffer from concealment, crookedness, obscurity, or narrowness? Hence “open joining.” It is like the king opening one side of the net and using a three-sided drive. Animals that come toward him and enter are taken; those that turn their backs and go forward are let go. He never insists on catching them all. Even his own followers understand the intention of the one above and do not alert one another or take precautions to ensure that all are caught. The king's three-sided drive and his letting the game ahead escape show that the kingly Way has been attained. The townspeople's not being alerted shows that the king's transforming influence is at work. All this is a way to good fortune. A king who can join openly like nine in the fifth place likewise attains the kingly Way and brings its transforming influence into effect.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:044 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:045 -->

**Lu Zhenqi:** To drive on three sides and let game escape, remaining untroubled by loss and gain, depicts the king's broad and even heart. The townspeople, unalerted, lose all self-conscious awareness in great compliance: this depicts the king's radiant and spacious transforming influence.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:045 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:046 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** The *Original Meaning* explains “the townspeople are not alerted” as their not warning one another or taking precautions to ensure that all are caught. This seems to mean seeking the game that has escaped ahead. But the *Classified Conversations* understands it simply in the sense of “there is a report, but no sound,” which is still more precise and apt. It means that while the king hunts, the places near the capital are scarcely disturbed at all. The *Original Meaning* is a work Zhu Xi did not finish revising. The differences so often found in his later teaching and discussion are instances of this. Broadly, the line uses hunting to portray the king's spacious, untroubled bearing. The game ahead escapes and is not pursued; the townspeople remain where they are and are not alerted. Those who go far away seem unaware of the king's solicitude: this is precisely solicitude at its utmost. Those who remain close seem unaware of the king's exalted standing: this is precisely exalted standing at its utmost. In an age of open joining, all who possess blood and breath feel reverence and affection. Yet the great compliance and great transformation spoken of leave no visible trace: such is their character.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:046 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:047 -->

### Top Six: Holding Together without a head. Misfortune.[^j02-no-head]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:047 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:048 -->

**Original Meaning.** Yin and yielding, occupying the top, it has no means of joining with those below. This is a way to misfortune. Hence the image of “having no head,” and the prognostication of misfortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:048 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:049 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Six occupies the top: it is the end of Holding Together. “Head” means beginning. In the Way of Holding Together, a good beginning leads to a good end. There may be those who have a beginning but fail to have an end; there has never been anyone who had no beginning yet achieved an end. Thus, in “joining without a beginning,” misfortune comes at the last. This explanation speaks from the standpoint of Holding Together's end. Moreover, top six is yin and yielding, lacks centeredness, and occupies the extreme of danger: it is certainly not capable of bringing things to a good conclusion. The world has many instances of associations begun without the Way and ending in estrangement.

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:050 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4553:051 -->

**Wang Bi:** “Without a head” means being late. Occupying the hexagram's end, it is the latecomer. Abandoned by the time, it fittingly meets misfortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4553:051 -->

**Wang Shenzi:** The fifth occupies the honored place as the single yang, and the four yin lines join it from below. Thus the *Commentary on the Judgments* says, “Those below comply and follow.” Top six, however, stands alone outside and does not follow. Is this not the image of the latecomer?

## 9. Xiaoxu 小畜 — Small Restraint

<!-- Source: https://www.eee-learning.com/book/4554 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4554:001 -->

![Xiaoxu: Qian below, Xun above](../assets/eee-learning/6c467b76b5af-yi09b.png) **Xiaoxu 小畜 — Small Restraint** — Qian below; Xun above.[^j02-restraint]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4554:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4554:002 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Concerning Small Restraint, the *Sequence of the Hexagrams* says: “Holding Together necessarily involves holding together; therefore it is followed by Small Restraint.” When things attach themselves to one another, they gather. Gathering is *xu*, holding together. Moreover, when people draw close and join, their intentions hold one another. This is why Small Restraint follows Holding Together. *Xu* means stopping; when things stop, they gather. The hexagram has Xun above and Qian below. Qian is something that belongs above, yet here it is below Xun. Nothing restrains and checks firmness and vigor so well as deference and compliance. Qian is restrained by Xun; hence Restraint. Yet Xun is yin, yielding and compliant in its constitution. It can only soften firmness and vigor through deference and compliance, not forcibly stop them. This is restraint on a small scale. Moreover, the fourth, a single yin line in its appropriate position, gives pleasure to the five yang lines. Its attaining the appropriate position means attaining the Way of yielding deference. It can hold the intentions of the many yang lines; hence Restraint. Small Restraint means using the small to restrain the great; what is held and gathered is small, and the matters restrained are small, because the restraining power is yin. The *Commentary on the Judgments* treats six in the fourth place restraining the yang lines as the defining meaning of the hexagram, without discussing its two constituent figures. It thus singles out what matters most.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4554:002 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4554:003 -->

### Small Restraint: success. Dense clouds, no rain, from our western outskirts.[^j02-western-clouds]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4554:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4554:004 -->

**Original Meaning.** Xun is also the name of a three-line figure. A single yin lies beneath two yang lines; therefore its virtues are deference and penetration, and its images are wind and wood. “Small” means yin; “restraint” means checking. With Xun above and Qian below, yin restrains yang. Moreover, six in the fourth place is the hexagram's only yin line, and the five yang lines above and below are all restrained by it; hence Small Restraint. Again, when yin restrains yang, it can bind but cannot hold fast: this also gives the image of what is restrained being small. There is vigor within and deference without. The second and fifth are both yang, each occupying the center of a trigram and exercising its function. This gives the image of firmness capable of keeping the center and of intentions finding expression. Thus the prognostication promises free passage. Yet restraint has not reached its fullness and beneficent influence has not taken effect. Hence the image of “dense clouds, no rain, from our western outskirts.” Dense clouds are a yin phenomenon; the western outskirts are a yin quarter. “Our” is King Wen speaking of himself. When King Wen unfolded the *Changes* at Youli, he looked toward Qizhou in the west: it was precisely a time of Small Restraint. For one who obtains this hexagram through divination, the prognostication likewise follows its image.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4554:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4554:005 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Clouds are the energies of yin and yang. When the two energies meet and harmonize, they hold one another fast and produce rain. Yang leading and yin responding is compliant; hence harmony. If yin takes the lead ahead of yang, this is not compliant; hence no harmony. Without harmony, rain cannot form. That clouds gather densely yet fail to produce rain is because they come from the western outskirts. Northeast is the yang quarter; southwest, the yin quarter. Beginning from yin therefore brings no harmony and cannot produce rain. From the human standpoint, rising clouds come from the distant regions on every side; hence “outskirts.” Speaking specifically from the west, the text says “from our.” The fourth is what restrains yang: it is the ruler of Restraint.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4554:005 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4554:006 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4554:006 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4554:007 -->

**Hu Yuan:** When yin and yang meet, rain's beneficence is bestowed. If yang energy rises but yin energy cannot enclose and hold it fast, there is no rain. If yin energy can enclose it but yang energy does not meet it, there likewise will be no rain. It is like the vapor of a cauldron and steamer: cover it with something, and the steam becomes water. “From our western outskirts” means that the cloud energy arises from the yin position of the western outskirts; it necessarily cannot become rain.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4554:007 -->

**Recorded Sayings of Master Cheng:** Someone took Small Restraint to mean a minister restraining a ruler, and Great Restraint to mean a ruler restraining a minister. He replied: “There is no need for this. Great Restraint simply means that what is restrained is great; Small Restraint, that what is restrained is small. One need not fix on a single matter. Even a ruler restraining a minister or a minister restraining a ruler operates through this same principle, applied according to whether the restraint is great or small.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4554:008 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4554:009 -->

**Zhang Jun:** Although the minister's sincere intention reaches the one above, the ruler's virtue has not yet responded in trust. It is like Heaven's energy not yet answering: “dense clouds, no rain.” The western outskirts occupy a yin position. “From our western outskirts” means that yang energy has not yet responded.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4554:009 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4554:010 -->

**Classified Conversations of Master Zhu:** Asked about “dense clouds, no rain, from our western outskirts,” he replied: “Rain always comes when yin energy is abundant and condenses densely enough to become moist and descend as rain. Here Qian advances upward, and the single yin cannot stop it. Thus the *Commentary on the Judgments* says, ‘It still goes on’: this refers to the image of Qian seeking to advance upward. When we reach top nine, the account concerns the hexagram's beginning and end. Restraint reaches its limit and disperses; thus ‘rain has fallen; rest has come.’ Yin's power has reached such fullness that there is the warning, ‘For the noble person to advance brings misfortune.’”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4554:010 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4554:011 -->

**Qiu Fuguo:** Qian is by nature something that belongs above. Now it is below Xun and is restrained by yieldingness; hence Small Restraint. Yet six in the fourth place, a single yin, restrains and checks five yang lines. It can bind their intentions but cannot hold them fast: this too is restraint on a small scale. When things are restrained, they stop; when stopping reaches its limit, they move. Therefore Small Restraint also has the meaning of success. Dense clouds are yin energy. The overlapping figure formed by lines two through four is Dui, which belongs to the west; hence “western outskirts.” The fourth has yieldingness in a yielding place; hence this image. When clouds move from east to west, there is rain; when they move from west to east, there is no rain: yin has taken the lead. Small Restraint takes yieldingness as its ruling power, which cannot hold yang fast and stop it. Therefore, though the clouds are dense, there is no rain.[^j02-nuclear-trigrams]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4554:011 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4554:012 -->

**Lin Xiyuan:** Small Restraint has two meanings: first, using the small to restrain the great; second, what is restrained being small. Precisely because the small restrains the great, what it restrains is small. The two meanings ultimately come to one. A question: “Heaven's energy belongs to yang, Earth's to yin. Here yin restrains yang, yet Heaven's energy is instead called yin and Earth's energy yang. Why?” The reply: “Speaking of the distinction between the Two Modes, what occupies the lower position and sends its energy upward is yin; what occupies the upper position and sends its energy downward is yang. Speaking of the interaction of the Four Images, however, the ascending movement of yin is itself yang, and the descending movement of yang is itself yin. This is the explanation in the *Mengyin*. It can bring out what Zhu Xi left unexpressed.”[^j02-rain-disagreement]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4554:012 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4554:013 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Once the intention behind the imagery of this hexagram is understood, its meaning becomes clear of itself. The Judgment speaks of “dense clouds, no rain” because Earth's energy rises but Heaven's energy has not yet responded: the clouds come “from our western outskirts,” and yin leads while yang has not answered in harmony. Speaking of yin and yang above and below, Earth's energy is yin and Heaven's energy is yang. Speaking of yin and yang in the four directions, west is yin and east is yang. Yin initiates an influence to which yang has not yet responded: this is the meaning that makes the hexagram Small Restraint. The *Commentary on the Judgments* says “it still goes on” because yin energy is ascending, and “its bestowal has not yet taken effect” because yin energy has not yet formed rain and descended. Applied to human affairs, this is the image of ministers and sons whose intentions dwell upon state and household, but who have not yet gained the harmony of ruler and father. Some commentators take the rising energy of Earth to be yang and the answering, descending energy of Heaven to be yin; they therefore also assign “it still goes on” to yang. Only Master Zhang's explanation—that Heaven's energy has not yet responded—accords exceptionally well with the hexagram's meaning.

### First Nine: Return by your own way. What blame could there be? Good fortune.[^j02-return]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4554:014 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4554:015 -->

**Original Meaning.** The lower trigram is Qian; its lines all by nature belong above. Their intention is to advance upward, but they are restrained by yin. Initial nine, however, belongs to Qian, occupies the bottom, and attains correctness; the yin before it is far away. Although it is the fourth's proper corresponding partner, it can maintain itself through correctness and avoid being restrained. Hence the image of advancing and returning by its own way. If the diviner is like this, there is no blame and there is good fortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4554:015 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4554:016 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Initial nine is a yang line and belongs to Qian. Yang is something that belongs above. Moreover, its firm and vigorous capacity is sufficient for upward advance, and it shares the intention of those above. Advancing and returning upward is therefore its Way; hence “return by your own way.” Since its return follows its own Way, what error or blame could there be? There is no blame, and there is also good fortune. When the other lines say “no blame,” they mean that acting in this way brings no blame. Thus it is said, “‘No blame’ means being good at remedying one's errors.” Even where a line's meaning is inherently good, this does not exclude the implication that acting otherwise would bring blame. Initial nine, however, simply proceeds by its own Way and has no error or blame. Hence “what blame could there be?” Its freedom from blame is especially clear.

### Nine in the Second Place: Drawn together in returning. Good fortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4554:023 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4554:024 -->

**Original Meaning.** The three yang lines share one intention. Nine in the second place is coming nearer to yin, but because it is firm and centered, it can link itself with initial nine and return. This too is a way to good fortune. If the diviner is like this, there is good fortune.

### Nine in the Third Place: The carriage loses its wheel-spokes. Husband and wife turn hostile eyes upon one another.[^j02-spokes]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4554:029 -->

**Original Meaning.** Nine in the third place also wishes to advance upward, but it is firm without centeredness, presses close to yin, and is not its proper corresponding partner. **Cheng's Commentary.** The third is a yang line without centeredness, closely joined to the fourth. ### Nine in the Fifth Place: Sincerity binds them together. Wealth enables one to enlist one's neighbors.[^j02-wealth-neighbor]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4554:043 -->

The fifth must extend a helping hand and cooperate with them: this is “wealth shared with one's neighbors.” The power of the fifth's honored position is like a rich person extending financial resources and strength to share with neighboring households. **Editorial Judgment.** The meaning of this line has never been clear. If we now infer it from the hexagram as a whole, six in the fourth place is the position close to the ruler: it is what is called “the small that restrains.” Nine in the fifth place is the ruler's position: it can cultivate its virtue so as to accept restraint from its ministers. The fourth says “there is sincerity”: sincerity is accumulated in order to reach the ruler. The fifth also says “there is sincerity”: sincerity is extended in receiving those below. Only when above and below trust one another is the Way of Restraint complete. Thus “uniting its intention with the one above,” said of the fourth, refers to the fifth; “enlisting its neighbor,” said of the fifth, refers to the fourth. The fourth and fifth are close to one another, hence “neighbor.” Moreover, “neighbor” means minister, as in the *Documents*: “Ministers! Neighbors!” Wealth means the fullness of accumulated sincerity. When sincerity is accumulated to the point of enabling one to enlist one's neighbor, that neighbor also responds with sincerity. Thus the *Commentary on the Images* says, “It is not rich alone”: sincerity evokes sincerity. Broadly, those above and below cannot meet without substantial sincerity of heart; hence “wealth enables one to enlist one's neighbors.” Yet neither can they meet without openness of heart; hence elsewhere, “without wealth, together with one's neighbors.” The images are based on yang's solidity and yin's openness, but their meaning is one.

### Top Nine: Rain has fallen; rest has come. Esteem for virtue has brought fullness. For a woman, steadfastness brings danger. The moon is nearly full. For the noble person to advance brings misfortune.[^j02-moon]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4554:049 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4554:050 -->

**Original Meaning.** Restraint reaches its limit and is accomplished; yin and yang have harmonized. Hence the image of “rain has fallen; rest has come.” This occurs because yin's virtue has been honored until it has accumulated to fullness. Yin has been placed above yang: thus, even with correctness, there is danger. Yet yin has now grown strong and resists yang, so the noble person likewise cannot proceed. Such is the prognostication. Its warning is profound.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4554:050 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4554:051 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Nine, at the utmost of deference and compliance, occupies the hexagram's top and the end of Restraint. It accepts restraint and stops: it is stopped by the fourth. “Rain has fallen” means harmony; “rest has come” means stopping. When yin restrains yang, it cannot stop yang without harmony. Once they harmonize and yang stops, the Way of Restraint is accomplished. Great Restraint is restraint on a great scale; therefore, at its limit, it disperses. Small Restraint is restraint on a small scale; therefore, at its limit, it is accomplished. “Esteem for virtue has brought fullness” means that the fourth employs yielding deference, accumulating it to fullness until its purpose is accomplished. Yielding yin does not restrain firmness in a single morning or evening: it arrives there through accumulation. Can one fail to be on guard? *Zai* means filling up through accumulation, as the *Odes* says, “His renown fills the road.” “For a woman, steadfastness brings danger”: the woman means yin. Yin restrains yang; yieldingness controls firmness. If the woman persists firmly in this, it is a way to danger. How could a woman control her husband, or a minister control his ruler, and remain secure? At full moon, the moon stands opposite the sun as its equal. “Nearly full” means that its growing strength is about to rival the sun. A question: “Yin can already restrain yang; why say only ‘nearly full’?” The reply: “Here yielding deference restrains yang's intention; it is not a matter of controlling it by force. Yet if the process does not stop, yin will become stronger than yang, bringing misfortune. Therefore, while the moon is nearly full, the warning is given: the woman is about to become an opponent; if the noble person acts, there is misfortune.” The noble person means yang; “advance” means action. “Nearly full” is the time just before fullness. If the moon were already full, yang would already be diminishing. What warning would remain?

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4554:051 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4554:052 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4554:052 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4554:053 -->

**Yang Shi:** Three yang lines advance from below, and a single yin cannot hold them fast. Hence “dense clouds, no rain: it still goes on.” At top nine, however, advance reaches its limit; hence “rest has come.” When yin and yang harmonize, there is rain, and a woman's correctness lies in compliance. Although restraint has brought rain, to take this restraint itself as the abiding norm brings danger. The moon faces the sun and thereby receives its light; when full, it stands opposite the sun as its equal. Thus one must not go beyond the point of near fullness. If the noble person reaches this point and still advances, it is a way to misfortune. Small Restraint takes yin's restraining yang as its main meaning. At its utmost, yin must become a rival to yang; hence this warning.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4554:053 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4554:054 -->

**Xiang Anshi:** Top nine occupies the utmost of Restraint, and the Way of Restraint is accomplished. Where formerly there was no rain, rain has now fallen; where formerly there was continued advance, rest has now come. The success spoken of in the Judgment becomes visible here. *Zai* means accumulation. When Restraint reaches the top, its virtue has accumulated to fullness, and what has been held together is great. Yet using the small to restrain the great cannot be a permanent arrangement. Once the moon is full, it wanes; once yin reaches its limit, it diminishes. This is the natural principle.

In six in the fifth place of The Marrying Maiden, it brings good fortune because yin responds to yang. In six in the fourth place of Inner Sincerity, it brings no blame because yin follows yang.

**Editorial Judgment.** This line, too, simply speaks of the Way of Restraint having been accomplished. Master Yang's explanation is the most complete and sound.

## 10. Lü 履 — Treading

<!-- Source: https://www.eee-learning.com/book/4555 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4555:001 -->

![Lü: Dui below, Qian above](../assets/eee-learning/1d2f4126aa0a-yi10b.png) **Lü 履 — Treading** — Dui below; Qian above.[^j02-treading-ritual]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4555:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4555:002 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Concerning Treading, the *Sequence of the Hexagrams* says: “Once things are gathered, there is ritual propriety; therefore Restraint is followed by Treading.” When things gather, distinctions of great and small, ranks of high and low, and differences of good and bad arise. This is how gathering things is followed by ritual propriety, and why Treading follows Restraint. Treading means ritual propriety; ritual propriety is what people tread and practice. The hexagram has Heaven above and marsh below. Heaven being above and marsh occupying the lower place—the distinction of upper and lower and the right relationship of honored and humble—accord with principle and form the root of ritual propriety. This is the constant Way of conduct; hence Treading. “Treading” means stepping and also serving as a support beneath. To tread upon something is to step on it; to be trodden upon is to support it. Yieldingness supports firmness, hence Treading. It does not say that firmness treads on yieldingness, but that yieldingness treads in relation to firmness, because firmness riding upon yieldingness is an ordinary principle not requiring mention. Thus the *Changes* speaks of yieldingness riding upon firmness, not of firmness riding upon yieldingness. Speaking of treading and supporting firmness reveals the meaning of humble compliance and a glad response.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4555:002 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4555:003 -->

### Treading on the tiger's tail. It does not bite the person. Success.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4555:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4555:004 -->

**Original Meaning.** Dui is also the name of a three-line figure. One yin appears above two yang lines; therefore its virtue is gladness and its image is the marsh. Treading has the meaning of following in something's steps and advancing. Dui encounters Qian: with harmony and gladness it treads behind firmness and strength. This gives the image of treading on a tiger's tail without suffering injury. Thus the hexagram is called Treading, and its prognostication is as stated. If a person can act in this way, there will be danger but no injury.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4555:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4555:005 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Treading is the Way people tread in their conduct. Heaven is above and marsh below; yieldingness treads beneath and supports firmness. Upper and lower each attain their proper relationship. This is perfect compliance in affairs and perfect appropriateness in principle. If people's conduct is like this, even treading on the most dangerous ground will bring no harm. Thus one treads on a tiger's tail without being bitten, and can attain success.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4555:005 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4555:006 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4555:006 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4555:007 -->

**Classified Conversations of Master Zhu:** Treading has Qian above and Dui below. Yin steps after yang: it follows behind, as though treading in its footprints. Hence “treading on the tiger's tail.” The third and fourth lines develop the meaning of the tiger's tail: these are precisely the places where yin goes to tread behind yang.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4555:007 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4555:008 -->

**Li Jian:** Treading is ritual propriety. Practiced harmoniously, it enables one to advance and retreat among the many firm lines without being injured. “In the practice of ritual, harmony is valued”—does this not mean just that?

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4555:008 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4555:009 -->

**Hu Bingwen:** Cheng's *Commentary* explains treading as stepping and supporting, speaking in terms of above and below. The *Original Meaning* speaks of following in something's steps and advancing, in terms of before and behind. This is especially apt to the word “tail.” Most commentators take Dui as the tiger; the *Original Meaning*, following Cheng's *Commentary*, takes Qian as the tiger, grounding itself in the Master's *Commentary on the Judgments*. “It does not bite the person: success.” In people's passage through the world, dangerous junctures are frequent. Not being injured by them reveals the quality of one's conduct. The *Great Commentary* says, “Did the rise of the Changes occur at the time of King Wen and Zhou of Shang? Therefore its words speak of danger.” No expression speaks more dangerously than “treading on the tiger's tail.” Thus, of the nine hexagrams concerning conduct amid anxiety and adversity, Treading stands first.[^j02-nine-hexagrams]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4555:009 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4555:010 -->

**Liang Yin:** Treading means putting into practice. In ritual propriety, people likewise put Heaven's principle into practice; hence Treading means ritual propriety. The tiger is a firm and fierce beast. Qian's three yang lines give the image of the tiger. The top is its head; the fourth, then, is its tail. Dui treading behind Qian gives the image of treading on the tiger's tail. Tigers bite people; yet when one treads with harmony and gladness, one is not bitten and instead attains success. Seen in this way, where could a person whose conduct is humble and deferential go without success? Yet harmony is not indulgent compliance, and gladness is not ingratiating flattery. It is simply reverence and compliance without loss of correctness. Dui's *Commentary* says, “Firm within and yielding without”: this is the Way.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4555:010 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4555:011 -->

**Cai Qing:** Of the eight trigrams, Dui alone represents the utmost weakness and Qian the utmost vigor. Here the weakest follows in the steps of the strongest. This is inherently a dangerous juncture; therefore this hexagram alone is named Treading. The *Commentary on the Judgments* then takes its virtues into account and says, “Treading on the tiger's tail: it does not bite the person. Success.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4555:011 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4555:012 -->

### First Nine: Tread in simplicity and go forward. No blame.[^j02-plain-treading]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4555:012 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4555:013 -->

**Original Meaning.** Yang occupies the bottom at the beginning of Treading. Not yet altered by things, it follows its unadorned conduct. If the diviner is like this, going forward brings no blame.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4555:013 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4555:014 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Treading is not staying still: it signifies going forward. The initial line occupies the very lowest place and has always been below. Yet its capacity of yang firmness enables it to advance upward. If it goes forward content with its original humble condition, there is no blame. A person unable to be content with an original condition of poverty and low station advances out of greed and impatience, seeking merely to escape poverty and low station rather than to accomplish anything. Once advancement is gained, arrogance and excess inevitably follow. Such going forward brings blame. A worthy person, by contrast, treads contentedly in the original condition: at rest, such a person is happy; advancing, such a person intends to accomplish something. Thus, when advancement comes, there is effective action and nothing that is not good. This is someone who preserves unadorned conduct.[^j02-lu-readings]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4555:014 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4555:015 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4555:015 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4555:016 -->

**Hu Bingwen:** The initial line has not yet engaged with things; hence the image of simplicity. On examination, both the *Original Meaning* and Master Cai say, “At the beginning of Treading, it is not altered by things.” Master Cai also says, “Plainness means the absence of ornament.” Treading is ritual propriety. Its initial line speaks of plainness because ritual is rooted in substance. Adornment is pattern and ornament. Its top line speaks of whiteness because ornament at its utmost returns to substance. “White adornment: no blame”—is this not the same as “tread in simplicity and go forward: no blame”?

### Six in the Third Place: One with impaired sight can see; one who is lame can tread. Treading on the tiger's tail: it bites the person. Misfortune. A warrior acts for the great ruler.[^j02-warrior]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4555:022 -->

**Original Meaning.** Six in the third place is neither centered nor correct, yielding in capacity but firm in intention. To tread upon Qian in this way inevitably brings injury. Hence this image, and misfortune for the diviner. It is also the image of a forceful warrior who gains his ambition and indulges in violence, like Zheng of Qin or Xiang Ji. How could such a course endure?

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4555:023 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4555:024 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The third is yin in a yang place. Its intention aspires to firmness, but its constitution is inherently yin and yielding. How could it make its conduct firm? It is therefore like a dim-eyed person's seeing, without clear vision, or a lame person's treading, without the ability to go far. Its capacity is already insufficient; moreover, it lacks centeredness in its position, treads where it is not correct, and strives for firmness despite being yielding. Such conduct treads dangerous ground; hence “treading on the tiger's tail.” To tread dangerous ground without skill in treading must bring calamity; hence “it bites the person: misfortune.” “A warrior acting as the great ruler” is like a violent martial man placed above others, merely giving free rein to impatience and recklessness, unable to tread compliantly and go far. Neither centered nor correct but firm in intention, he nevertheless has the support of the many yang lines. Thus he treads danger with rigid impatience and meets misfortune.

**Collected Explanations.**

Can one persist in it? Even someone possessing firmness and clarity follows a dangerous Way by relying exclusively on them. How much more someone whose firmness and clarity are insufficient! **Xiang Anshi:** In hexagrams containing a single yin or a single yang, when the solitary line is at the bottom, the hexagrams are Return and Encounter; when at the top, Breakthrough and Stripping Away. Their principal meaning concerns waning and growth. When it is in the second or fifth place, yang in the second is the commander of The Army, and yang in the fifth is the ruler of Holding Together; yin in the second is the noble person of Fellowship, and yin in the fifth is the noble person of Great Possession. Their principal meaning concerns attaining the appropriate position. When it is in the third or fourth place, yang in the third acts with firmness through yieldingness and is the “laboring modesty” of Modesty; yang in the fourth controls yieldingness through firmness and is the “source of enthusiasm” in Enthusiasm. Yin in the third acts with yieldingness upon firmness and is Treading; yin in the fourth controls firmness through yieldingness and is Small Restraint. Their principal meaning concerns the exercise of function. Broadly, a functioning line below concerns conducting one's own affairs; above, it concerns regulating the affairs of others.[^j02-single-lines]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4555:053 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4555:054 -->

**He also said:** Throughout the six lines of Treading, treading upon yieldingness brings good fortune. Thus nine in the second place is “broad and level”; nine in the fourth is “apprehensive, with good fortune in the end”; and top nine has “a complete course, with great good fortune.” All tread yielding places. Six in the third place, though inherently favorable in the hexagram statement, ultimately meets misfortune because it treads firmness. Initial nine and nine in the fifth place both tread correct positions, yet the initial can merely avoid blame, while the fifth cannot escape danger. Both tread firmness. Therefore the initial is taught to preserve its simplicity, lest it lose the correctness of its original intention; the fifth is taught caution in decision, lest it rely on the correctness of its power and position. Firmness delights in movement and favors decision. Those who act in reliance on firmness often leave many things to regret afterward.

## 11. Tai 泰 — Peace

<!-- Source: https://www.eee-learning.com/book/4557 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4557:001 -->

![Tai: Qian below, Kun above](../assets/eee-learning/14f472529130-yi11b.png) **Tai 泰 — Peace** — Qian below; Kun above.[^j02-peace]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4557:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4557:002 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Concerning Peace, the *Sequence of the Hexagrams* says: “Through treading, there is ease; only then is there security. Therefore Treading is followed by Peace.” When conduct finds its proper place, there is spacious ease; with ease comes security. This is why Peace follows Treading. In the hexagram, Kun's yin is above and Qian's yang below. The energies of Heaven and Earth, yin and yang, meet and harmonize; then the myriad things are born and brought to completion. Hence free communication and peace.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4557:002 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4557:003 -->

### Peace: the small goes; the great comes. Good fortune and success.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4557:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4557:004 -->

**Original Meaning.** Tai means free passage. In this hexagram, Heaven and Earth meet and the two energies communicate; hence Peace. It is the hexagram of the first month. The small means yin; the great, yang. Kun goes to occupy the outer position, and Qian comes to occupy the inner. Again, in its derivation from The Marrying Maiden, a six goes to occupy the fourth place and a nine comes to occupy the third. If the diviner possesses the virtue of firm yang, there is good fortune and success.[^j02-months-transformations]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4557:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4557:005 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The small means yin; the great, yang. Going means going outward; coming means coming to occupy the inner position. Yang energy descends, and yin energy rises to meet it. When yin and yang harmonize freely, the myriad things are born and fulfilled: this is the peace of Heaven and Earth. In human affairs, the great is the ruler above, the small the ministers below. The ruler extends sincerity in entrusting matters to those below; the ministers fulfill sincerity in serving the ruler. The intentions of above and below communicate: this is the peace of the court. Yang represents noble people; yin, petty people. Noble people come to occupy the inner positions, while petty people go outward. Noble people attain office and petty people remain below: this is peace throughout the world. The Way of Peace brings both good fortune and success. It does not say “great good fortune” or “great success,” because times have their decline and flourishing, and government its lesser and greater achievements. Even peace cannot be treated as uniform. “Good fortune and success” can encompass them all.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4557:005 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4557:006 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4557:006 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4557:007 -->

**Liu Mu:** Going and coming are said in relation to the inner and outer trigrams. Moving from within to without is going; returning from without to within is coming.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4557:007 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4557:008 -->

**Cai Qing:** The hexagram is named Peace because Heaven and Earth meet and the two energies communicate. This meaning is drawn from the root of creation and transformation, in which neither can exist without the other. The hexagram statement says “the small goes; the great comes,” placing noble people within and petty people without. This meaning is drawn from the distinction between goodness and wickedness. Are there, then, two meanings of Peace? No: one. It is simply that when Heaven and Earth meet and the two energies communicate, yang must be within and yin without.[^j02-yinyang-ethics]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4557:008 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4557:009 -->

### First Nine: Pull up the thatch-grass, its roots entwined with those of its kind. To advance brings good fortune.[^j02-grass]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4557:009 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4557:010 -->

**Original Meaning.** Three yang lines below are linked together and advance: this is the image of pulling up thatch-grass with its entwined roots, a prognostication of good fortune in going forward. If the diviner is yang and firm, the advance is auspicious. Guo Pu's *Donglin* ends the sentence after the word “kind.” The following hexagram follows the same reading.[^j02-tai-readings]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4557:010 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4557:011 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The initial yang line occupies the bottom: it is someone with firm and clear capacity who remains below. In a time of Obstruction, noble people withdraw and live in obscurity. Now that the time is Peace, their intention is to advance upward. When noble people advance, they invariably draw and support their companions and kind, like the roots of thatch-grass: pull up one, and the others rise linked with it. *Ru* means roots drawing one another along; hence the image. *Hui* means kind. Worthy people advance with their kind, sharing the intention to put their Way into practice; therefore there is good fortune. Noble people always advance with their kind, not merely because they wish to bring one another forward and delight in associating with goodness, but because they depend on one another to accomplish things. Neither noble nor petty people can stand alone without the assistance of their companions. Since antiquity, when noble people have attained office, the worthy throughout the world have gathered at court, sharing purpose and joining strength to bring peace to all under Heaven. When petty people hold office, the unworthy advance together; their party then prevails, and the world falls into stagnation. Each follows its own kind.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4557:011 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4557:012 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4557:012 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4557:013 -->

**Liu Xiang:** When worthy people occupy high positions, they draw in their kind and gather them at court. When they occupy low positions, they think of advancing together with their kind. Above, they draw their kind forward; below, they urge their kind onward. Thus, when Tang employed Yi Yin, the unbenevolent withdrew and the many worthy arrived: like brings like.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4557:013 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4557:014 -->

**Classified Conversations of Master Zhu:** “With those of its kind” belongs with the preceding phrase. I once saw that Guo Pu's *Donglin* also divided the sentence this way. People of that time were therefore already reading it thus. “Pulling up thatch-grass with entwined roots” is the image of a thing; “with those of its kind” concerns people.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4557:014 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4557:015 -->

**Lin Xiyuan:** Cheng's *Commentary* says, “*Ru* means roots drawing one another along.” Judging by the *Original Meaning*—“three yang lines below, linked together and advancing”—these are the roots of other clumps of grass, not simply the roots of the original clump. One yang line advances and the other two are linked with it, just as one clump is pulled up and the roots of other clumps are linked with it.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4557:015 -->

### Nine in the Second Place: Embrace the uncultivated; use the courage to ford the river. Do not neglect the distant; let partisanship fall away. Find accord with the central course.[^j02-embrace-wilderness]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4557:016 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4557:017 -->

**Original Meaning.** Nine in the second place has firmness in a yielding place, holds the center below, and has six in the fifth place as its responsive partner above. It governs Peace and attains the central Way. If the diviner can embrace what is rough and impure, yet act with firm decision; omit nothing distant, yet avoid intimacy with partisan associates, then the diviner accords with this line's Way of walking the central course.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4557:017 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4557:018 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The second, firm yang and centered, responds above to the fifth; the fifth, yielding, compliant, and centered, responds below to the second. Ruler and minister share the same virtue. Through its firm, centered capacity, the second is entrusted exclusively by the one above. Thus, though occupying the minister's place, it governs the ordering of Peace. This is what is meant by “above and below meet, and their intentions are one.” The Way of governing Peace is therefore explained chiefly through the second. “Embrace the uncultivated; use the courage to ford the river; do not neglect the distant; let partisanship fall away”: these four are the Way of dwelling in Peace. When people become comfortable and unrestrained, administration slackens, laws and standards fall into disuse, and affairs lose their proper measure. To govern such a condition, one must possess the breadth to contain what is rough and impure. Then one's measures will be generous, unhurried, thorough, and precise: abuses are removed, affairs put in order, and people remain at ease. Without expansive tolerance, a heart full of indignation and aversion lacks deep and far-reaching consideration and brings the harm of violent disturbance. Before deep-rooted abuses have been removed, immediate troubles have already arisen. Hence the need to embrace the uncultivated. “Use the courage to ford the river”: in an age of peace and security, people grow accustomed to prolonged ease, content to preserve routine, lazy through habit, and fearful of change. Without the courage to cross a river on foot, one cannot accomplish anything at such a time. Fording the river means having sufficient firmness and resolution to cross deep water and pass through danger. Since antiquity, ages of peaceful order have gradually declined because of accustomed ease and habitual routine. Without a firm, decisive ruler and outstanding, resolute assistants, no one can stand forth and exert themselves to reform the abuses. Hence “use the courage to ford the river.” Some may doubt this: above, “embrace the uncultivated” means tolerance and accommodation; here, fording the river means vigorous reform. They seem opposed. Such doubt fails to recognize that the sages and worthies exercise firmness and resolution through a capacity for tolerance. “Do not neglect the distant”: in a peaceful age, hearts accustomed to ease seek merely to remain comfortable. How could they still think deeply and look far ahead to remote matters? Those governing Peace should attend comprehensively to every affair, neglecting nothing even at a distance. Hidden details of affairs and worthy talent in obscure, remote places are all “distant.” In peaceful times, these are precisely what tends to be neglected. “Let partisanship fall away”: once the time is peaceful, people grow accustomed to security, their feelings run unchecked, and they lose restraint. To bring them back within bounds and correct them is impossible without severing private attachment to one's associates. Hence “partisanship falls away.” Since antiquity, laws and regulations have often failed to take effect because they were entangled in personal considerations. A prohibition on extravagance harms close relatives; a limit on landed property inconveniences powerful households. In such cases, failing to decide with great impartiality and ensure enforcement means being held back by partisan attachment. Governing Peace without letting such attachment fall away is difficult indeed. Possessing these four qualities accords with the virtue of nine in the second place. Hence “find accord with the central course”: one can match and join the meaning of walking the central course. *Shang* means matching.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4557:018 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4557:019 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4557:019 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4557:020 -->

**Hu Bingwen:** Tolerance without decisive control is not the center in which firmness and yieldingness aid one another. Only embracing what is rough and impure, and yet acting with firm decision, accords with the center. Even when nothing distant is neglected, private attachment to one's own party can readily produce an imbalance; this is not the center free from partiality in assigning greater and lesser weight. Only neglecting nothing distant, and yet avoiding intimacy with partisan associates, means neither forgetting the distant nor slighting the near, and thus accords with the center. The two occurrences of “and yet” in the *Original Meaning* deserve close reflection.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4557:020 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4557:021 -->

**Gong Huan:** Initial nine is to advance “with its kind,” while nine in the second place is to let its associates fall away. Why? Initial nine is a worthy person below, and is therefore urged to bring its kind forward. Nine in the second place is the great minister who advances or withdraws the world's talent, and is therefore urged to let private associations fall away. Only then can the worthy throughout the world be employed. If the minister favors only personal companions, some worthy people will be unable to advance into service. This accounts for the difference.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4557:021 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4557:022 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Judging this line by the Master's *Commentary on the Images*, the two words “embrace the uncultivated” must be primary. The hearts of sages and worthies abandon nothing; the Way of Yao and Shun wishes all to live together. Without embracing the uncultivated, one cannot embody the heart of Heaven and Earth or fulfill the Way of ruler and teacher. Yet this does not mean indiscriminate confusion. One must therefore act decisively, encompass matters with clarity, and deal with them impartially. Only then will every employment and rejection, every raising up and setting aside, accord with the central Way. The *Analects of Lu* speaks of generosity, trustworthiness, diligence, and impartiality; the meaning is similar. Of the four, generosity is the root. Hence: “To occupy high office without generosity—how could I bear to look upon it?”[^j02-four-virtues]

### Nine in the Third Place: No level ground without a slope; no going without return. Steadfast in difficulty: no blame. Do not fret over what is assured. In your sustenance, there is blessing.[^j02-turning-cycle]

**Original Meaning.** It is about to pass beyond the middle, when Peace approaches its limit and Obstruction is about to come. *Xu* means anxiety; *fu*, the assured fulfillment of what is expected. The diviner is warned to maintain steadfast correctness through difficulty; then there will be no blame and there will be blessing.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4557:024 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The third stands at the middle of Peace, above all the yang lines: Peace is flourishing. The principle of things is like a revolving cycle: what is below must rise, and what is above must descend. Prolonged Peace must become Obstruction. Thus, while Peace flourishes and yang is about to advance, the warning is given: nothing remains level and secure forever without becoming steep and dangerous—Peace does not last unchanged. Nothing goes on forever without returning—yin must come back. When level becomes sloping and what has gone returns, there is Obstruction. One should understand the necessity of Heaven's principle. Even amid Peace, one must not dare to rest in ease, but should always keep difficulty and danger in one's thoughts and make one's actions correct and firm. In this way there can be no blame. In dwelling in Peace, if one can remain steadfast amid difficulty, one can preserve that peace continually and obtain what one seeks without anxious labor. Not failing in what was expected is *fu*, assured fulfillment. In this way, one's livelihood and sustenance receive increased blessing. Livelihood and sustenance mean one's blessings and good fortune. Those skilled in dwelling in Peace can prolong their blessings. As virtue and goodness accumulate daily, blessings and emoluments arrive daily. When virtue exceeds one's emoluments, one may flourish without becoming overfull. Since antiquity, none in a state of great prosperity has met ruin without first losing the Way.

**Collected Explanations.**

**Xiang Anshi:** “No level ground without a slope” speaks of the three yang lines; “no going without return,” of the three yin lines. Twice saying “none without” makes clear that these are inevitable and assured developments of Heaven's Way. Knowing this, people at the height of Peace must exhaust human effort to guard against them. Presiding over the utmost flourishing of Peace, they must keep the heart apprehensive. Then their actions will contain no error or blame. Only then need they not fret over that assured arrival, and can long enjoy the blessings already theirs.

Someone asks: “Yin and yang alternate in their movements; Obstruction and Peace succeed one another. This is the tendency of the time. Steadfastness amid difficulty and freedom from anxiety are what human effort must fulfill. Heaven and humanity can each prevail upon the other. Those who stand at their intersection and tread where they meet must possess a Way both of adapting to change and of holding fast. ### Six in the Fifth Place: Di Yi gives the maiden in marriage. Through this, blessing and great good fortune.[^j02-diyi]

### Top Six: The city wall falls back into the moat. Do not deploy the army. Issue commands from your own town. Steadfastness brings humiliation.[^j02-wall-moat]

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4557:045 -->

**Original Meaning.** Peace reaches its limit and becomes Obstruction: this is the image of the city wall falling back into the moat. The diviner is warned not to contend by force, but only to preserve oneself. Even with steadfast correctness, humiliation cannot be avoided.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4557:045 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Earth is dug from the moat and piled up to make the wall, just as the Way of good government is built up to make Peace. The top is Peace's end, and a six occupies it as a petty person: Obstruction is imminent. “Do not deploy the army”: a ruler can employ the multitude because feelings communicate between above and below and hearts follow. Now Peace is ending and its Way has been lost. Feelings no longer communicate between above and below; the people's hearts are scattered and they no longer follow their ruler. How could they be employed? To employ them would bring disorder. Since the multitude cannot be employed, commands are only now proclaimed beginning with those close at hand. Even if the commands proclaimed are correct, this is humiliating. The town is one's place of residence and means those nearby. Broadly, the proclamation of commands must begin close to home. “Steadfastness brings misfortune” and “steadfastness brings humiliation” each have two possible meanings: persisting firmly in this brings misfortune or humiliation, or even attaining correctness still brings them. Here the text says humiliation rather than misfortune because only beginning to issue commands when Obstruction is imminent is shameful. Obstruction is not caused by issuing the commands.

**General Discussion.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4557:050 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4557:051 -->

**Liu Dingzhi:** Peace takes its meaning from Heaven and Earth meeting so that the myriad things communicate, and from above and below meeting with one intention. Thus, among its six lines, the meaning of mutual meeting is especially important. The initial and fourth meet: this is the beginning of Peace. Therefore the initial says “with its kind,” like grass with entwined roots, while the fourth says “with its neighbors,” like birds fluttering together. The second and fifth meet: this is the middle of Peace. Therefore the fifth speaks of a ruler lowering his exalted standing to entrust affairs to a minister, and the second of a great minister fully discharging his responsibility in answering the ruler. The third and top meet: this is the end of Peace. Therefore the third speaks of level ground turning to a slope, and the top of the wall returning to the moat. Noble people advancing and petty people withdrawing bring about Peace. The ruler entrusting affairs and the minister giving loyal service bring about Peace. Yet in Heaven's revolving movement, Peace reaching its limit and becoming Obstruction has an inevitability. Within the intention to preserve Peace lies an unspoken demand that one cannot but be apprehensive. How grave, then, is the purport of level ground and slope, wall and moat!

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4557:052 -->

**Wu Yueshen:** The initial and fourth speak in terms of kindred energy, being the beginnings of the two constituent figures. The third and top speak in terms of the movement of the times, being their ends. The second and fifth speak in terms of governing Peace, being their centers.

## 12. Pi 否 — Obstruction

<!-- Source: https://www.eee-learning.com/book/4558 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4558:001 -->

![Pi: Kun below, Qian above](../assets/eee-learning/a32c0fdcb7e5-yi12b.png) **Pi 否 — Obstruction** — Kun below; Qian above.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4558:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4558:002 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Concerning Obstruction, the *Sequence of the Hexagrams* says: “Peace means free passage. Things cannot enjoy free passage forever; therefore Peace is followed by Obstruction.” The principle of things involves going and returning. Free passage and peace, at their utmost, must give way to stagnation; this is why Obstruction follows Peace. The hexagram has Heaven above and Earth below. When Heaven and Earth meet and yin and yang harmonize freely, there is Peace. Here Heaven stays above and Earth below: they are separated and do not communicate. Hence Obstruction.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4558:002 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4558:003 -->

### Obstruction: not the human Way. Unfavorable to the noble person's steadfastness in what is right. The great goes; the small comes.[^j02-not-human]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4558:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4558:004 -->

**Original Meaning.** Pi means closing and obstruction. It is the hexagram of the seventh month, exactly the reverse of Peace. Thus it says “not human,” meaning not the human Way. Its prognostication is unfavorable to the noble person's correct Way. Qian goes to occupy the outer position, and Kun comes to occupy the inner. Again, in its derivation from Gradual Progress, a nine goes to occupy the fourth place and a six comes to occupy the third. Some suspect that the three characters “of not-human” are an interpolation mistakenly introduced from six in the third place of Holding Together. That the *Commentary* does not separately explain their meaning also lends support to this suspicion.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4558:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4558:005 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Heaven and Earth meet, and the myriad things are born between them; only then are the Three Powers complete. Humanity is the most numinously intelligent and therefore stands at the head of the myriad things. All that is born between Heaven and Earth belongs to the human Way. If Heaven and Earth do not meet, the myriad things are not born: there is no human Way. Hence “not human,” meaning not the human Way. Waning and growth, closing and opening, follow one another without cessation. Peace reaches its limit and reverses; Obstruction reaches its end and is overturned. Nothing remains constantly unchanged. How, then, could the human Way cease to exist? After Obstruction there is Peace. Above and below communicating, firmness and yieldingness meeting in harmony: this is the noble person's Way. Obstruction is its reverse; hence it is unfavorable to the noble person's steadfastness. The noble person's correct Way is obstructed and cannot proceed. “The great goes; the small comes” means yang goes and yin comes. This is the image of the petty person's Way growing and the noble person's Way diminishing; hence Obstruction.

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4558:006 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

## 13. Tongren 同人 — Fellowship

<!-- Source: https://www.eee-learning.com/book/4559 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4559:001 -->

![Tongren: Li below, Qian above](../assets/eee-learning/c41a60bb5c99-yi13b.png) **Tongren 同人 — Fellowship** — Li below; Qian above.[^j02-fellowship]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4559:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4559:002 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Concerning Fellowship, the *Sequence of the Hexagrams* says: “Things cannot remain obstructed forever; therefore Obstruction is followed by Fellowship.” When Heaven and Earth do not meet, there is Obstruction; when above and below unite, there is Fellowship. Their meanings are opposed, so they follow one another. Moreover, when the world is obstructed, one must unite with others before there is strength to overcome the obstruction. This is why Fellowship follows Obstruction. The hexagram has Qian above and Li below. In terms of the two images, Heaven is above, and fire by nature flames upward, joining with Heaven; hence Fellowship. In terms of the two constituent figures, the fifth occupies its correct position and is the ruler of Qian; the second is the ruler of Li. These two lines respond through centeredness and correctness, uniting above and below: this is the meaning of Fellowship. Moreover, the hexagram has only one yin, with which all the yang lines wish to unite; this too is the meaning of Fellowship. Other hexagrams certainly also contain a single yin, but here, in the time of Fellowship, the second and fifth respond, and Heaven and fire unite. Thus its meaning is great.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4559:002 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4559:003 -->

### Fellowship in the open fields. Success. It is beneficial to cross the great river. The noble person's steadfastness in what is right is beneficial.[^j02-open-fields]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4559:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4559:004 -->

**Original Meaning.** Li is also the name of a three-line figure. A single yin clings between two yang lines; therefore its virtues are attachment and luminous clarity, and its images are fire, the sun, and lightning. Fellowship means uniting with others. Li encounters Qian: fire rises and unites with Heaven. Six in the second place attains its proper position and the center, responding above to nine in the fifth place. Moreover, it is the hexagram's only yin, and the five yang lines all associate with it; hence Fellowship. “In the open fields” means spacious, far-reaching, and free of self-interest. Here is a way to success. Acting with vigor, one can therefore cross the river. The hexagram has luminous clarity within and firmness and vigor without; six in the second place is centered and correct and has a responsive partner. This is the noble person's Way. If the diviner can be like this, there is success, and danger can also be crossed. Yet the fellowship must accord with the noble person's Way before it is beneficial.[^j02-li-reading]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4559:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4559:005 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** “Fields” means the open countryside, taking the sense of what is distant and outside. Fellowship, understood through the Way of great unity throughout the world, is the great impartiality of the hearts of sages and worthies. Ordinary people's fellowship follows the agreements of their private intentions: it is merely the affection of intimate association. Thus it must be “in the open fields.” This means not following the private attachments of intimate feeling, but meeting in the spacious and distant countryside. Once freed from private attachment, it is the utterly impartial Way of great fellowship. No distance lies beyond its union; its success is evident. To achieve great fellowship with all under Heaven is to have all under Heaven unite with oneself. When all unite, what dangerous obstruction cannot be overcome? What hardship cannot be passed through? Hence “it is beneficial to cross the great river; the noble person's steadfastness is beneficial.” Above, “in the open fields” merely says that fellowship is not confined to intimate association. Here it adds that one should follow the noble person's correct Way. The noble person's steadfastness means the utterly impartial Way of great fellowship throughout the world. Thus, even at a distance of a thousand *li*, or born a thousand years later, people accord like the matching halves of a tally. Extend and practice this Way, and throughout the breadth of the four seas and the multitude of the people, none fail to unite. Petty people, by contrast, use only their private intentions. They agree with their intimates even when those people are wrong, and disagree with those they dislike even when those people are right. Their fellowship therefore becomes a partisan clique, because their hearts are not correct. Thus, in the Way of Fellowship, benefit lies in the noble person's steadfast correctness.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4559:005 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4559:006 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4559:006 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4559:007 -->

**Kong Yingda:** Fellowship means harmonizing and uniting with people. The fields are a broad and distant place; their name is borrowed to illustrate breadth and reach. In harmonizing with others, one must be expansive, leaving no one beyond the possibility of fellowship, and impartial in the use of one's heart. Only then is there free passage; hence “fellowship in the open fields: success.” Unity of heart with others is sufficient to cross difficulty; hence “it is beneficial to cross the great river.” Harmonizing with others can easily stray into crookedness; hence “the noble person's steadfastness is beneficial.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4559:007 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4559:008 -->

Fearing righteousness and being able to change, it fittingly meets good fortune.

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4559:033 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4559:033 -->

The Appended Statements says, ‘Those who are near but do not accord meet misfortune.’ The initial and top lines, by contrast, each stand outside the affair and do not interfere with one another; therefore there is no contention.”

**Xiang Anshi:** Wherever a line speaks of inability to prevail, yang occupies a yin position. Because it is yang, there is litigation or attack. <!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4559:035 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** The hexagram is named Fellowship, yet its third and fourth lines present images of estrangement and contention because, in human feeling, unity at its utmost must become difference, and difference at its utmost returns to unity. It is just as perfect order gives way to disorder, and extreme disorder returns to order. These are the beginnings of separation and reunion in human affairs, and the revolving principle of the Way of the Changes. In the inner trigram, unity becomes difference: “at the gate” and “within the clan” are union; at the third, the image of concealed troops shows difference becoming overwhelming. In the outer trigram, difference becomes unity: climbing the wall but being unable to attack, and the great army prevailing so that the parties meet, gradually reverse the estrangement. At the top, the image of fellowship in the outskirts returns to unity. The third and fourth stand precisely at the juncture where unity becomes difference and difference becomes unity. Thus the sages derive their images from these lines' positions and virtues. In the third, “its opponent is firm” means that it opposes the top line. In the fourth, “climbing the wall” means attacking the initial line. Since these are not responsive partners, they do not unite; lacking union, they present images of mutual opposition and attack. To understand them as contending for six in the second place's response, opposing and attacking nine in the fifth place, does not seem to accord with the hexagram's intention.

### Nine in the Fifth Place: In fellowship, first loud weeping, afterward laughter. A great army prevails, and they meet.[^j02-weeping-laughter]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4559:037 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4559:038 -->

**Original Meaning.** The fifth is firm, centered, and correct. The second, yielding, centered, and correct, responds below: they are of one heart. Yet the third and fourth separate them, preventing their union. What is united in righteousness and principle, however, cannot be sundered by anything; hence this image. Yet six in the second place is weak and yielding, while the third and fourth are strong and firm. Thus a great army must overcome them before the two can meet.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4559:038 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4559:039 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Nine in the fifth place unites with the second but is separated from it by the two yang lines, third and fourth. The fifth considers its claim upright and its principle superior, and cannot bear its angry frustration: it weeps aloud. Yet crookedness cannot prevail over correctness. Though separated, the two must finally unite; hence laughter afterward. “A great army prevails, and they meet”: the fifth and second are proper corresponding partners, but the two yang lines wrongly obstruct and attempt to seize their union. A great army must overcome them before they can meet. “Great army” and “prevails” reveal the strength of the two opposing yang lines. Nine in the fifth place occupies the ruler's position, yet this line does not take the meaning of a ruler's fellowship with others. The fifth answers the second exclusively through private intimacy, losing the virtue of centeredness and correctness. A ruler ought to share great fellowship with the entire world; favoring one person alone is not the ruler's Way. Again, first weeping when separated and then laughing when reunited expresses private affection, not the constitution of great fellowship. Even the second below is humiliated by fellowship within the clan; how much more a ruler! Since the fifth cannot here be taken as exemplifying the ruler's Way, the text leaves that Way aside and illuminates instead the meaning of two people of one heart who cannot be separated. The *Appended Statements* says, “The noble person's Way may lead out into service or remain at home, may be silent or speak. When two people are of one heart, their sharpness cuts metal.” United in inward sincerity, they are one whether in service or retirement, speech or silence. Nothing under Heaven can divide them. Unity is one; one cannot be divided—divided, it becomes two. Oneness can penetrate metal and stone, brave water and fire: there is nothing it cannot enter. Hence “their sharpness cuts metal.” The principle is exceedingly subtle, and so the sage praises it: “The words of those of one heart have the fragrance of orchids.” This means their words have depth and lingering savor.

## 14. Dayou 大有 — Great Possession

<!-- Source: https://www.eee-learning.com/book/4560 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4560:001 -->

![Dayou: Qian below, Li above](../assets/eee-learning/1aea54c6c468-yi14b.png) **Dayou 大有 — Great Possession** — Li above; Qian below.[^j02-great-possession]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4560:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4560:002 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Concerning Great Possession, the *Sequence of the Hexagrams* says: “Things must come to one who unites with others; therefore Fellowship is followed by Great Possession.” One who unites with others is where things come to belong. This is why Great Possession follows Fellowship. In the hexagram, fire is above Heaven. Fire situated high sends its light far, and among the myriad things there is nothing it does not illuminate and reveal. This is the image of Great Possession. Moreover, a single yielding line occupies the honored place, and the many yang lines all respond. To occupy the honored place while maintaining yieldingness is to become the one to whom things come. Above and below responding to it constitutes the meaning of Great Possession. Great Possession means flourishing greatness and abundant possession.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4560:002 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4560:003 -->

### Great Possession: great success.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4560:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4560:004 -->

**Original Meaning.** Great Possession means the greatness of what one possesses. Li stands above Qian: fire is above Heaven, illuminating everything. Moreover, six in the fifth place, a single yin, occupies the honored position and attains the center, while the five yang lines respond to it. Hence Great Possession. Qian is vigorous and Li clear; occupying the honored place and responding to Heaven, there is a way to success. If the diviner possesses these virtues, there is great goodness and success.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4560:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4560:005 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The capacities of the hexagram make great success possible. In describing a hexagram's virtues, sometimes the name itself contains the meaning, as in “Holding Together: good fortune” and “Modesty: success.” Sometimes its meaning directly gives rise to instruction and warning, as in “The Army: steadfastness; a seasoned elder brings good fortune” and “Fellowship in the open fields: success.” Sometimes the statement speaks from the hexagram's capacities, as in “Great Possession: great success.” Because of firmness, vigor, and luminous clarity, responding to Heaven and acting according to the time, it can attain great success.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4560:005 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4560:006 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4560:006 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4560:007 -->

**Zheng Ruxie:** Yang is great and yin small. A single yin occupies the honored place, and the five yang lines come to it: what it possesses is great. Such greatness is not something weak yieldingness can possess. Only one who is open and empty, without self-satisfaction, can possess it. Six in the fifth place belongs to clarity and has an open center: this is why there is Great Possession and why there is great success. To understand Great Possession simply as abundant wealth and flourishing greatness is to miss its meaning.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4560:007 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4560:008 -->

**Qiu Fuguo:** A single yin occupies the center of the upper trigram, and the five yang lines acknowledge it as their head. What every line possesses is possessed by six in the fifth place. Is this not great? Precisely because its possession is great, its success is also great.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4560:008 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4560:009 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Holding Together, with a nine in the fifth place, is superior in that respect to Great Possession, with its six in the fifth place. Yet those who respond in Holding Together are five yin lines, images of the common people. Those who respond in Great Possession are five yang lines, images of worthy people. When the worthy respond, what possession could be greater? Thus, although Great Possession's yielding centeredness does not equal Holding Together's firm centeredness, Holding Together's “good fortune” and “no blame” do not equal Great Possession's direct statement of “great success.” Only this hexagram and The Cauldron have a Judgment that says simply “great success,” with no further words. Both do so because of honoring and nourishing the worthy.[^j02-cauldron-judgment]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4560:009 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4560:010 -->

### First Nine: No involvement in harm. This is not blame. Remain mindful of difficulty, and there is no blame.[^j02-possession-caution]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4560:010 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4560:011 -->

**Original Meaning.** Though it is a time of Great Possession, yang occupies the lowest place, with no binding correspondence above, and the undertaking is at its beginning. It has not yet become involved in harm. What blame could there be? Yet one must still approach the situation with an awareness of difficulty before there can be no blame. The diviner is warned to act in this way.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4560:011 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4560:012 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Nine occupies the beginning of Great Possession and has not yet reached abundance. It is low, without a responsive associate, and has not yet committed the fault of arrogance and overfullness. Thus there is “no involvement in harm”: it has not entered into harm. Generally, few who possess wealth escape injury from it. Even the worthy Zigong could not entirely avoid it; how much more those below him! “This is not blame; remain mindful of difficulty, and there is no blame” means that wealth and possession are not in themselves blameworthy. People create blame for themselves through their wealth. If one can enjoy abundance while recognizing the difficulty of dwelling in it, there will naturally be no blame. If one possesses abundance but cannot think of difficulty and remain vigilant and apprehensive, an arrogant and extravagant heart arises. This is what brings blame.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4560:012 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4560:013 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4560:013 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4560:014 -->

**Hu Bingwen:** A time of Great Possession can, on the contrary, readily bring harm. The initial yang is below and has not yet engaged with things; therefore it has not yet become involved in harm. What blame could there be? Yet if one thinks “this is not blame” and approaches it with a careless heart, blame will instead arise. “No involvement in harm” describes Great Possession at its beginning. “Mindful of difficulty, there is no blame” is how Great Possession should be approached from beginning to end.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4560:014 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4560:015 -->

### Nine in the Second Place: A great carriage for carrying a load. There is somewhere to go. No blame.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4560:015 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4560:016 -->

**Original Meaning.** Firm and centered below, it gains a response above: this is the image of a great carriage carrying a load. If one has somewhere to go and is like this, blame can be avoided. The diviner must possess this virtue for the prognostication to apply.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4560:016 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4560:017 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Nine occupies the second place with yang firmness and is relied upon and entrusted with affairs by the ruler, six in the fifth place. Firmness and vigor give it sufficient capacity; occupying a yielding place makes it modest and compliant; attaining the center keeps it from excess. Such are its capacities, enabling it to bear the responsibility of Great Possession. It is like the construction of a great carriage, strong and sturdy enough to bear heavy things. It can take up a weighty burden and travel far; hence “there is somewhere to go: no blame.” In this time of rich abundance, possession has not yet reached its limit. Thus, with the second's capacities, one can go forward without blame. Once abundance reaches its utmost, however, one must no longer advance.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4560:017 -->

### Nine in the Third Place: A lord presents offerings to the Son of Heaven. A petty person cannot do this.[^j02-offering]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4560:020 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4560:021 -->

**Original Meaning.** The character 亨 is written 享 in the *Spring and Autumn Tradition*: it means presenting offerings at court. In antiquity, 亨 meaning free passage, 享 meaning presentation, and 烹 meaning cooking were all written 亨. Nine in the third place occupies the top of the lower trigram: the image of a duke or feudal lord. Firm and correct, it has above it the ruler, six in the fifth place, whose open center bows to the worthy. Hence the image of presenting offerings to the Son of Heaven. If the diviner possesses this virtue, the prognostication is as stated. Petty people lack the virtue of firm correctness. Even if they obtain this line, they cannot fulfill it.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4560:021 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4560:022 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The third occupies the top of the lower figure. Below the sovereign yet above other people, it presents the image of a feudal lord. Dukes and lords serve the Son of Heaven above. The Son of Heaven occupies the world's highest position: “To the bounds of the land, none is not the king's subject.” How could those below dare to claim exclusive possession of what they have? The wealth of the land and the multitude of the people all belong to the king. This is the correct principle. Thus, in the time of Great Possession, the third occupies a lord's position and possesses rich abundance. It must have free communication with the Son of Heaven: that is, it must regard what it possesses as the Son of Heaven's possession. This is the constant duty of a minister. A petty person in such a position instead claims the wealth exclusively as private property and does not understand the Way of serving the one above impartially. Hence “a petty person cannot do this.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4560:022 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4560:023 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4560:023 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4560:024 -->

**Classified Conversations of Master Zhu:** “Ancient writing had no separate character 享; 亨, 享, and 烹 were used interchangeably. In a phrase such as ‘a lord presents offerings to the Son of Heaven,’ it is simply wrong to explain the graph as 亨, ‘success.’” He also said: “According to the Explaining Graphs and Analyzing Characters, 亨 and 享 were originally one character, and therefore the Changes often uses them interchangeably. Thus ‘the king makes an offering at Mount Qi’ should likewise be read with 享, as in ‘the king makes an offering to the Supreme Ruler.’ The forms and sounds of characters are the shallow matters of classical study, so earlier scholars who grasped the great meanings often gave them little attention. Yet they did not realize that failing to understand these points leads to an immense waste of explanation and forced patching, without ever attaining the great meaning. This, too, does serious harm.”[^j02-heng-xiang-correction]

### Nine in the Fourth Place: Not excessive in abundance. No blame.[^j02-peng]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4560:025 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4560:026 -->

**Original Meaning.** The pronunciation and meaning of the character *peng* 彭 are not fully known. Cheng's *Commentary* calls it the appearance of abundance, and this may well be right. Six in the fifth place is a yielding, centered ruler. Nine in the fourth place approaches it with firmness, inviting suspicion of encroachment and usurpation. Yet because it occupies a yielding place, it presents the image of not carrying its abundance to the utmost, and so avoids blame. The diviner is warned to act accordingly.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4560:026 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4560:027 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Nine in the fourth place, in the time of Great Possession, has passed the middle: Great Possession is flourishing. Excessive abundance is where misfortune and blame arise. Thus the Way of dwelling in it is “not to be excessive in abundance,” and then there is no blame. This means being able to lower oneself modestly and not occupy a condition of excessive fullness. The fourth holds high office close to the ruler. To dwell in too great an abundance there brings misfortune and blame. *Peng* describes flourishing multitude. The ode *Driving On* says, “The Wen River flows mightily; travelers crowd the road.” This describes the great number of travelers. The *Greater Odes*, in *Great Brightness*, says, “Teams of four red horses with white bellies, in thronging array.” It describes the abundance of King Wu's war-horses.

### Six in the Fifth Place: Its sincerity brings mutual trust; its bearing inspires awe. Good fortune.[^j02-trust-awe]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4560:030 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4560:031 -->

**Original Meaning.** In an age of Great Possession, yielding, compliant, and centered, it occupies the honored position. Emptying itself to respond to the worthiness of nine in the second place, it receives allegiance from above and below. This is the mutual meeting of sincerity and trust. Yet the ruler's Way values firmness: excessive yieldingness leads to neglect and failure. It should be aided by awe-inspiring authority; then there is good fortune. Hence this image and prognostication, which is also a warning.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4560:031 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4560:032 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Six in the fifth place, in the time of Great Possession, occupies the ruler's position. Its open center is the image of sincerity and trustworthiness. When a ruler maintains yieldingness and keeps the center, meeting those below with sincerity and trust, those below likewise exhaust their trustworthiness and sincerity in serving the one above. Above and below meet in mutual trust. Yet yieldingness occupies the honored position at a time of Great Possession, when people's hearts are comfortable and careless. If yielding compliance alone is valued, insolence and disrespect will arise. Thus only an awe-inspiring bearing brings good fortune. Such a bearing means possessing dignity and authority. Having received those below with gentle harmony and sincere trust, and gained their glad allegiance, one also possesses an authority that inspires apprehension. This is skill in dwelling amid possession; its good fortune is evident.

### Top Nine: Heaven gives its aid. Good fortune; nothing that is not beneficial.[^j02-heavens-aid]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4560:035 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4560:036 -->

**Original Meaning.** In an age of Great Possession, firmness occupies the top yet can follow six in the fifth place below. This is being able to tread in trustworthiness, keep compliance in mind, and honor the worthy. Full without overflowing, it receives this prognostication.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4560:036 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4560:037 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Top nine stands at the hexagram's end, in a place without office. It is at the utmost of Great Possession yet does not dwell possessively upon what it has. At the top of Li, it is at the utmost of clarity. Only through supreme clarity can it avoid dwelling possessively upon what it has and thus avoid passing beyond the limit. Possession reaching its utmost without one's resting in possession brings no calamity of overfullness: this is the ability to comply with principle. The fifth possesses sincerity and trustworthiness, and the top treads above it; this signifies treading in sincerity and trust. The fifth possesses luminous clarity, and the top can lower its intention to respond to it; this signifies honoring the worthy and exalting goodness. To conduct itself in this way is to accord with the Way at its utmost. It should naturally enjoy blessing and felicity. “Heaven gives its aid”: acting in compliance with Heaven, it receives Heaven's assistance. Thus wherever it goes there is good fortune, and nothing is without benefit.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4560:037 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4560:038 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4560:038 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4560:039 -->

**Guo Yong:** The *Appended Statements* says, “‘Aid’ means assistance. Heaven assists what is compliant; people assist what is trustworthy. Treading in trustworthiness, keeping compliance in mind, and also honoring the worthy.” The ruler, six in the fifth place, actually fulfills all of this. That it is spoken of under top nine means that Great Possession's good fortune reaches its completion here. Thus the *Commentary on the Images* says, “Great Possession's good fortune at the top.” We therefore know that this good fortune belongs to Great Possession as a whole, not merely to what is said of top nine.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4560:039 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4560:040 -->

**Zheng Ruxie:** “Treading in trustworthiness, keeping compliance in mind, and also honoring the worthy” speaks of the fifth. Its “sincerity brings mutual trust” is treading in trustworthiness. Occupying the honored place while employing yieldingness is keeping compliance in mind. Having top nine above it is honoring the worthy. The fifth receives Heaven's assistance, with good fortune and nothing without benefit, because it possesses these qualities. Why speak of the fifth but attach the statement to the top? The fifth is the ruler that makes the hexagram what it is; the top is its completion. The fifth's virtue deserves this blessing, and its outcome can be verified at the end. The *Changes* often takes its meanings this way. Top nine of Small Restraint says, “For a woman, steadfastness brings danger; the moon is nearly full.” This speaks of six in the fourth place restraining yang: upon reaching the top, it becomes the woman whose persistence brings danger and the moon nearly full. If the words referred to top nine itself, top nine is yang and could not be the woman or the moon. Interpreters go wrong by clinging to the individual line in their search for meaning, and therefore assign treading in trustworthiness, thinking of compliance, and honoring the worthy to top nine. What the *Changes* calls “honoring” is setting above oneself. The fifth honors the worthiness of top nine; thus Heaven's assistance becomes manifest at top nine.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4560:040 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4560:041 -->

**Wang Zongchuan:** Six in the fifth place, a single yielding line, possesses the five firm lines. Top nine alone is above the fifth, and the fifth can honor it. The *Commentary on the Appended Statements* says, “And also honoring the worthy”: this refers to top nine as the worthy one honored. Assistance from Heaven, good fortune, and nothing without benefit mean that Great Possession, upon reaching this point, flourishes still more without decline. At the utmost of Great Possession, nothing is greater than gaining Heaven's support; and in gaining Heaven's support, nothing is greater than honoring the worthy.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4560:041 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4560:042 -->

**Hu Bingwen:** Top nine of Small Restraint is the end of restraint. Its prognostication speaks of danger and misfortune, continuing the account of six in the fourth place. Top nine of Great Possession is the end of possession. Its prognostication is good fortune with nothing without benefit, continuing the account of six in the fifth place. In Small Restraint, one yin restrains the many yang lines, and its ending is therefore as described there. In Great Possession, one yin possesses the many yang lines, and its ending is therefore as described here. Is the great distinction between ruler and minister not clear? The fifth's sincerity is treading in trustworthiness; its yielding centeredness is thinking of compliance; honoring the single yang of top nine is honoring the worthy. Thus, at the end, Heaven gives its aid: good fortune, nothing without benefit.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4560:042 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4560:043 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Cheng's *Commentary* and the *Original Meaning* both make treading in trustworthiness, thinking of compliance, and honoring the worthy the business of top nine. Yet the *Changes* very often uses the top line to complete the meaning of the fifth. Examples include The Army's “the great ruler has a command,” Li's “the king sets forth on campaign,” and Release's “the lord shoots a falcon.” None of these makes the top line itself a king or lord. Each carries forward the fifth line and completes its meaning. The explanations of Master Guo, Master Zheng, and Master Wang all accord with the intention of the hexagram and the meaning of the line. Master Hu is among the most faithful adherents of the *Original Meaning*, yet here alone follows Master Guo and the others. This is because the former interpretation did not satisfy his own heart either.

## Translator’s Endnotes

These notes belong to the translator, not to the imperial compilers. **E** denotes the repository’s archived 易學網 transcription, [Juan Two](../source/juan-02.md), which remains unchanged. **S** denotes the [Wikisource 四庫全書 transcription, fixed revision 528525][siku02], consulted on 25 September 2026. S is a parallel transcription, not an infallible facsimile. Corrections adopted in English are identified below; unresolved readings and genuine disagreements are not silently repaired. This is selective textual comparison, not a leaf-by-leaf collation of historical printings.

[^j02-bi]: **比: holding together and joining.** The title continues the name already used in Juan One. The commentators define 比 through 親輔, drawing close and lending support. The verb is consequently often translated “join,” while the hexagram title is “Holding Together.” It includes reciprocal personal association as well as ruler–subject allegiance. “Above,” “below,” “within,” and “without” may indicate line positions, social relationships, or both; the English retains these shifts rather than replacing them with a single psychological scheme. Unless expressly qualified, line positions are counted upward from the bottom.

[^j02-repeat-divination]: **原筮 and 元永貞.** Zhu Xi and Hu Bingwen read 原 as “again”: a second consultation examines one’s fitness for the allegiance others offer. Cheng Yi instead reads the phrase as tracing matters to their grounds and judging the person to be joined, explicitly excluding actual stalk or shell divination. The contrast with Meng’s first consultation is therefore an argument within the tradition, not an unnoticed contradiction. 元永貞 is rendered as three qualities, following the explanations printed here; 元 is not simply another occurrence of the hexagram number or a separate promise of success. Hu’s examples 原蠶, 原廟, 原田 occur in both E and S. The last, a field-expression, is retained without inventing a secure English etymology for its use as evidence for “again.” Hui Dong’s later [*Zhouyi shu*, on Bi](https://eee-learning.com/book/6523), expressly distinguishes the graph underlying 原田 from 原 meaning “again”; this is a genuine philological complication, not grounds for silently deleting Hu’s example.

[^j02-late-man]: **後夫.** “Latecomer” follows the main line of Zhu Xi’s interpretation. Cheng divides the force differently: someone who postpones seeking association meets misfortune even if he is a firm, self-reliant man. His quotations about being a 夫 establish that sense of the word, rather than identifying the person addressed by the oracle. The old masculine language is preserved where it is doing interpretive work. The passage’s claim that no one is entirely self-sufficient is Cheng’s argument, not an instruction to submit indiscriminately to whoever offers association.

[^j02-bi-positions]: **Position is not always parity.** Guo Yong’s statement that only The Army and Holding Together have their solitary yang “properly placed” concerns the roles of minister/commander at two and ruler at five. It cannot mean merely that yang occupies an odd-numbered position: The Army’s sole yang is at two. Elsewhere “correct position” commonly means yang at one, three, or five, and yin at two, four, or six. “Centered” normally refers to two or five, the middle positions of the two trigrams. These criteria must not be collapsed into a single automatic rating of a line.

[^j02-earthen-jar]: **孚, 缶, and 他.** 孚 is inward sincerity that can establish trust, not merely confidence that one’s desired outcome will occur. The unadorned earthen jar makes inner fullness visible without requiring outward ornament. 他 means what is other or beyond the immediate relation; here the commentators understand an additional benefit arriving without insistence. Hu contrasts this with an ulterior attachment in other hexagrams. The same character therefore need not name the same moral condition each time it appears.

[^j02-bi-third]: **An unresolved sentence and differing uses of response.** Cheng’s puzzling 二存應而比初, translated “the second retains its corresponding connection yet joins the initial line,” occurs in both E and S. No supported replacement has been established here; the difficulty is not concealed. The following observation that the centered, correct second can nevertheless be called the wrong person expressly allows contextual interpretation. Zhu’s quoted “the initial line corresponds to the fourth” likewise should not be silently changed: both are yin in this hexagram. Positional counterparts are one–four, two–five, and three–six, whereas a *proper responsive pairing* often additionally requires opposite polarities. The commentators do not always employ 應 with identical strictness.

[^j02-hunt]: **The three-sided drive.** The text explains 三驅 as an enclosure with one side left open, not as three separate hunts. Its ethical comparison concerns accepting those who come and allowing those who leave to depart. Zhu Xi expressly rejects the demand that every feature of the hunt correspond exactly to every feature of political or personal association. The translation preserves the concrete hunt; the limits of the comparison are part of Zhu’s own argument.

[^j02-no-alarm]: **邑人不誡.** Zhu’s *Original Meaning* understands the followers’ failure to alert one another as their sharing the ruler’s refusal to insist on catching everything. Cheng understands a ruler who makes no special prior arrangement with his own town. Zhu’s later discussion, preferred by the compilers, points instead to a hunt that scarcely disturbs nearby inhabitants. These are related but not identical. “A report, but no sound” preserves 有聞無聲; the market-goers and plowers similarly exemplify activity continuing undisturbed. The later discussion is also found in [*Zhuzi yulei*, juan 70](https://ctext.org/zhuzi-yulei/70). No biographical setting for these allusions is supplied without firmer evidence.

[^j02-no-head]: **無首 is not automatically Qian’s “without a head.”** Zhu reads the image here through the failure of the top line to provide a focus for association. Cheng reads 首 as “beginning,” and Wang Bi connects the phrase with lateness. In Qian’s Using Nine, the same word belongs to a favorable image; in this line it belongs to misfortune. Reusing the earlier translation mechanically would erase the argument. The retained “head” lets the successive commentaries disclose their distinct senses.

[^j02-restraint]: **畜: restraint, gathering, and nurture.** 畜 can hold something back or hold things together; the commentaries derive gathering from stopping. “Small Restraint” keeps the governing sense here without suppressing accumulation and nurture where the argument requires them. 小 denotes yin, the small restraining the great, and the limited extent of the restraint. These are the source’s explanatory associations, not interchangeable dictionary definitions in every context. Cheng expressly resists restricting the whole hexagram to one fixed ruler–minister situation.

[^j02-western-clouds]: **“Our western outskirts.”** 我 is retained as a first-person possessive. Zhu identifies the speaker with King Wen at Youli, looking toward Qizhou in the west. This is the commentary’s traditional historical interpretation, not a claim that the wording independently establishes its author or date. Likewise, the correspondences between west, yin, clouds, and incomplete rain belong to the explanatory system of these authors. Their weather statements are translated as historical claims within that system, not as modern meteorological laws.

[^j02-nuclear-trigrams]: **The overlapping Dui.** Qiu Fuguo reads lines two, three, and four together as a three-line figure: two solid lines below a broken line form Dui. This is an overlapping or nuclear trigram, not the upper or lower trigram of the six-line figure and not the result of changing cast lines. His western direction comes from Dui’s association with the west. Making that procedure explicit prevents the seeming contradiction between the named upper trigram Xun and the appearance of Dui in the explanation.

[^j02-rain-disagreement]: **Which energy is yin?** The source offers more than one account. Lin Xiyuan distinguishes classification by original position from classification by ascending or descending function; the compilers prefer Earth’s ascending energy as yin and Heaven’s answering energy as yang. They therefore assign “it still goes on” to yin, whereas Zhu’s quoted explanation assigns the continued advance to Qian. Neither account has been silently rewritten to agree with the other. Lin’s cited title 蒙引 is retained as *Mengyin*; it is a work of explanation, not the name of another hexagram.

[^j02-return]: **復: upward return or withdrawal?** Zhu and Cheng understand yang returning to its proper place above. Wang Shenzi, Gong Huan, Yu Yan, and He Kai understand restraint followed by withdrawal or return to the Way, and the compilers prefer their account. The oracle’s “return by your own way” and “drawn together in returning” deliberately do not predetermine the direction. Gong’s assertion that the classics never use 復 for upward advance is his philological argument; it is preserved as such, not presented as a separately verified universal claim.

[^j02-spokes]: **輻 and 輹.** E and S both print 輻 in the oracle, translated “wheel-spokes.” Xiang Anshi argues for 輹, a fitting associated with the axle and carriage body, because a fitting may be deliberately removed to stop the carriage. Hu Bingwen instead distinguishes intentional removal of that fitting in Great Restraint from disabling damage to the spokes here. The translation keeps the transmitted oracle and the disagreement. “Axle fitting” avoids pretending that this passage settles the precise reconstruction of the ancient component. Claims about how the wheel or axle turns remain attributed to the quoted commentator.

[^j02-wife-language]: **Husband, wife, and control.** The source’s patriarchal assumptions are not replaced by a gender-neutral account of “inner energies.” Cheng explicitly treats a wife’s control of her husband as a reversal of the proper relationship. Xiang’s distinction between 妻 and 婦 is local to his contrast between estrangement and harmony; both words can refer to a wife or woman, and they do not everywhere encode that contrast. The translation conveys the historical argument without making its social hierarchy a prescription from the translator.

[^j02-blood-fear]: **血去惕出.** The commentaries printed here take the compact phrase as bloodshed or injury departing and fear being left behind. The English follows that syntax. It does not change the characters or import a different reconstruction of the oracle. The scene matters because a single yielding line attempting to restrain several firm lines risks injury unless sincerity establishes a workable relation.

[^j02-wealth-neighbor]: **富以其鄰.** Zhu reads wealth as the means to enlist neighboring lines in the upper trigram; Cheng reads the powerful fifth as sharing strength with fellow yang lines against their restraint. The compilers instead read wealth as accumulated sincerity and the “neighbor” as the minister in the fourth place. Their quotation 臣哉鄰哉 explicitly makes ministers the ruler’s neighbors. Thus “enlist,” “share with,” and the compilers’ ethical explanation are intentional differences, not terminology drift. The contrast with “without wealth” in Peace’s fourth line is explained through solid yang and open yin, not through a blanket claim that either wealth or poverty is inherently superior.

[^j02-moon]: **Near the full moon and 貞厲.** 幾望 is the moon approaching fullness. The explanations concern the point before yin becomes fully opposed to yang; no exact calendar date or waiting period is imposed. Zhu reads the woman’s 貞厲 as danger even with correctness, while Cheng emphasizes the danger of maintaining this relation unchanged. The oracle’s shorter “steadfastness brings danger” leaves both commentaries room to speak. Wang Yinglin’s comparison with The Marrying Maiden and Inner Sincerity expressly shows that the near-full moon is not an invariant omen with a single favorable or unfavorable meaning.

[^j02-treading-ritual]: **履 and 禮.** 履 means treading, the course one follows, and conduct put into practice. Cheng links this with 禮, ritual propriety, understood as embodied right relationship rather than mere ceremony. His 藉 refers to supporting something from beneath, as ground supports a foot. Zhu instead foregrounds following behind and stepping in another’s tracks. The translation keeps both spatial accounts. It does not reduce the tiger to a fixed modern equivalent such as an employer, nor remove the danger inherent in the literal image.

[^j02-nine-hexagrams]: **Nine hexagrams of cultivating virtue.** Hu refers to the sequence beginning “Treading is the foundation of virtue” in the [Lower Appended Statements](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/易傳/繫辭下), also preserved with commentary in [Juan Fifteen](../source/juan-15.md). “Nine” here does not mean nine cast lines or nine consultations. The same discussion connects the rise of the *Changes* with apprehension and adversity. “Zhou of Shang” distinguishes the Shang ruler 紂 from the Zhou dynasty 周; the historical association is transmitted in the commentary, not established by the translator as the date of the oracle’s composition.

[^j02-plain-treading]: **素履.** 素 suggests unadorned substance and, in Cheng’s explanation, the humble condition in which a person originally finds himself. The contrast is not simply wealth against poverty: it is advancing to accomplish something rather than advancing from an impatient desire to escape one’s station. Hu’s comparison with “white adornment” concerns the relation between substance and ornament. The oracle’s “simplicity” should therefore not be read as intellectual naivety or a rejection of cultivated form.

[^j02-lu-readings]: **Textual corrections in Lü.** The following E readings are corrected in English with S’s support: 貧踐 → 貧賤, “poverty and low station,” in Cheng on the initial line; 故口 → 故曰, “hence it says,” in Wang Bi on the fourth; 其置辭 → 其爻辭, “its line statement,” in the editorial judgment on the fifth; and 占凶之小大 → 吉凶之小大, “the lesser or greater measure of good and ill fortune,” in Cheng on the top line. E’s 項藉 is normalized to 項籍, as S has it, yielding Xiang Ji; “Zheng of Qin” renders 秦政. None of these changes alters the repository’s Chinese source. They are corrections to this English reading, not a claim to have settled every variant in the juan.

[^j02-secluded]: **幽人.** The “secluded person” is interpreted here through quiet contentment, restraint in seeking office, and the absence of an external responsive attachment. The phrase need not prescribe literal flight to a mountain hermitage. Cheng also reads the image as a warning to an upward-aspiring yang line, while Zhu describes the virtue that makes the favorable prognostication applicable. The difference between an existing virtue and an injunction to acquire it is expressly discussed elsewhere in this juan.

[^j02-warrior]: **武人為于大君.** The main rendering follows the compilers and Wang Shenzi: forceful courage can be employed in the great ruler’s service. Zhu and Cheng instead describe a forceful warrior acting as ruler, which they criticize. Their meanings remain explicit in their own paragraphs. The compilers’ allusion to grappling with a tiger and crossing a river on foot invokes Confucius’s rejection of reckless courage in *Analects* 7.11. Impaired sight and lameness are the source’s physical analogies for capacity falling short of intention.

[^j02-fourth-proximity]: **An elliptical clause retained.** Both E and S read 四雖近而不處, literally “although the fourth is near, it does not occupy/remain.” “It does not occupy his place” supplies the ruler’s position as the understood object, since the preceding discussion concerns proximity to the fifth. This is a contextual interpretation of an elliptical sentence, not an attested insertion of missing Chinese characters. The reading remains open to revision upon collation of Cheng’s commentary and historical witnesses.

[^j02-danger]: **貞厲 is not harmonized.** Zhu and Cheng take the fifth line to warn that decisiveness remains dangerous even when correct. The compilers instead allow 貞厲 to express abiding awareness of danger: precisely what enables decisive conduct not to go wrong. They cite Biting Through and Breakthrough as comparisons. The oracle’s compact rendering is followed by the competing explanations, rather than expanded into a single synthesized lesson. A line may be praised in the *Commentary on the Judgments* and warned in its individual statement because the passages attend to different features.

[^j02-review-treading]: **祥 and 旋.** 祥 here denotes signs or indications whose favorable or unfavorable character must be examined; it is not assumed in advance to mean a lucky omen. 旋 is read by these commentators as the completion of a full course, not as a literal instruction to turn in a circle. The favorable outcome is conditional upon the course being without deficiency. “Great good fortune” therefore remains the conclusion of an examination of conduct, not an unconditional promise attached to reaching the final line.

[^j02-single-lines]: **A structural comparison, not a casting method.** Xiang’s final discussion groups hexagrams containing one minority-polarity line by where that line occurs. He treats the ends as images of waxing and waning, the centers as governing positions, and the third and fourth as places of active function. This is an interpretation of already-formed figures. It does not tell the reader to generate new hexagrams by moving lines during a consultation. 豫 is called *Enthusiasm*, as in Juan One; its “source of enthusiasm” preserves the language of 由豫 without making enthusiasm merely an emotional state.

[^j02-peace]: **泰: peace through communication.** The established title *Peace* is retained, but the commentaries define 泰 as 通, free passage or communication. Heaven below Earth means their energies can move toward one another; it does not mean that the drawing literally reverses the physical locations of sky and ground. Human “peace” is likewise explained through communication between above and below. Its opposite, 否, is called *Obstruction*, continuing Juan One’s terminology; “stagnation” is sometimes used descriptively in the English.

[^j02-months-transformations]: **Months and derived figures.** “First month” here and “seventh month” under Obstruction belong to the traditional calendrical sequence of waxing and waning hexagrams, not to January and July in the Gregorian calendar. The two derivations described by Zhu exchange the third and fourth positions: The Marrying Maiden becomes Peace, and Gradual Progress becomes Obstruction. This is 卦變, an explanatory account of a hexagram’s formation, not a direction to move or change particular lines in every divination. The original and resulting figures are distinct from the base and changed hexagrams of a particular cast.

[^j02-yinyang-ethics]: **Complementarity and moral discrimination.** Cai Qing explicitly distinguishes the mutual necessity of yin and yang in creation from their use to discriminate noble and petty people. The source then argues that these can express one pattern. The translation does not replace this historical moral cosmology with a claim that yin is inherently wicked, nor does it remove the source’s hierarchy in favor of a wholly egalitarian symbolism. What an individual yin or yang line represents is determined by each argument, as the following disagreement over Peace’s fourth line makes especially clear.

[^j02-grass]: **茅, 茹, and 彙.** The image is thatch-grass pulled up with interconnected roots, used to explain bringing one’s associates along. Lin Xiyuan specifies roots linking separate clumps, not merely several roots of the same plant. 彙 means kind or group. Following the punctuation explicitly defended here, “with those of its kind” belongs to the pulling-up image rather than to a separate command to advance. In Obstruction the same image is given a different application; the common picture does not make both prognostications identical.

[^j02-tai-readings]: **Textual corrections in Tai.** E’s opening *Original Meaning* on the initial line has 三陰在下, “three yin lines below”; S has 三陽在下, “three yang lines below,” which agrees with the actual figure. The English adopts 陽. In the same passage E has 征行之占 and S 征行之吉; “a prognostication of good fortune in going forward” retains E’s explicit oracular reference and conveys the favorable outcome stated by S and by the line itself. In Xu Zhifang’s discussion of the third line, E’s 平陂住復 is read as S’s 平陂往復, “level and sloping, going and returning.” These are documented English reading decisions, not silent alterations to E.

[^j02-embrace-wilderness]: **包荒, 馮河, 朋亡, and 中行.** 包荒 accommodates what is rough, uncultivated, or impure; the commentators do not treat it as approval of every fault. 馮河 evokes crossing a river on foot and is taken here as decisive courage. The juxtaposition with tolerance is deliberate: firmness must work through breadth of heart. 朋亡 is the falling away of partisan attachment, not a prediction that friends will die. 中行 is the central course in conduct, and 得尚 is explained as matching or joining it. These phrases should not be reduced to four disconnected pieces of fortune-telling advice.

[^j02-four-virtues]: **Generosity as the foundation.** The compilers’ 寬、信、敏、公 correspond to the virtues named near the close of [*Analects* 20.1](https://ctext.org/analects/yao-yue): generosity, trustworthiness, diligence, and impartiality. Their further quotation about high office without generosity is from *Analects* 3.26. “The Analects of Lu” translates the source’s 魯論; it is not the title of a newly introduced work by a commentator named Lu. The comparison supports their emphasis on 包荒 without erasing the need for decision, clarity, and impartial judgment.

[^j02-turning-cycle]: **No going without return.** 陂, sloping or uneven ground, contrasts with level ground; it is rendered “slope” rather than treated as a newly named place. In this passage 孚 has the sense of an expected development proving true, explained by Zhu as 所期之信. It is therefore rendered “what is assured,” not mechanically “sincerity.” The commentators differ over the extent to which human vigilance can preserve peace against the turn of events. Xu Zhifang explicitly argues against assigning everything to Heaven’s movement and making human effort irrelevant. The translation preserves that argument rather than converting cyclical imagery into a guarantee of an inevitable date or outcome.

[^j02-fluttering]: **A major disagreement at six in the fourth place.** Zhu and Cheng understand the descending yin lines as the return of petty people once Peace has passed its midpoint. The compilers prefer the explanations of Shen Gai and the following authors: those above humbly reaching down to the worthy below, fulfilling Peace’s union of intentions. The fluttering flock and accompanying neighbors remain the same image under these opposed applications. “Without wealth” is explained through yin’s openness, contrasted with yang’s solidity; it need not denote literal financial poverty. Neither interpretation has been made to disappear from the English.

[^j02-diyi]: **Di Yi and the marriage.** The main text retains 帝乙 as Di Yi and renders 妹 broadly as “the maiden.” Cheng expressly says that he does not know which ruler called Yi is intended; Xiang proposes Tang and speaks of a younger sister, while the royal-daughter interpretation governs Cheng’s account. Zhu instead treats the line as recording an earlier divinatory occurrence. These are transmitted explanations, not one securely established event reconstructed by the translator. No identification is silently chosen to settle the disagreement. The marriage’s hierarchical assumptions are translated rather than rewritten as a modern account of partnership.

[^j02-wall-moat]: **The wall returns to its own excavation.** 隍 is the moat or ditch from which earth has been raised to make the wall; the image reverses that construction. Cheng reads the belated issuing of commands as humiliating even if the commands are correct. The compilers instead read 貞 as adhering to customary practice: using an army now would be humiliating, whereas issuing commands within one’s own town is permissible. The text therefore contains a disagreement about the scope of the warning, not merely different ways of saying the same thing.

[^j02-not-human]: **否之匪人.** Zhu and Cheng explain 匪人 as the absence of the human Way when Heaven and Earth fail to communicate. Several collected commentators instead understand “the wrong people,” whose ascendancy obstructs the noble person’s correctness. Zhu also reports the conjecture that 之匪人 was mistakenly imported from Bi’s third line. The phrase occurs in both E and S and is retained. A conjecture reported by a commentator is not permission for the translator to delete transmitted words without notice.

[^j02-pi-grass]: **Who withdraws or reforms?** Zhu initially treats the three yin lines as petty people advancing together, but sees the first line’s not-yet-manifest wrongdoing as an opportunity for reform. Cheng and the commentators preferred by the compilers instead see noble people preserving the Way together while their outward position is obstructed. The differing identities are part of the received commentary. The oracle image itself has not been expanded to prejudge the debate.

[^j02-pi-readings]: **吉, not 占.** In Hu Yuan’s comment on the initial line, E reads 然後得其占而獲亨, whereas S reads 然後得其吉而獲亨. The English adopts S’s 吉, giving “only then do they obtain good fortune and attain success,” in agreement with 貞吉亨 in the oracle. The source file retains E’s reading. Other differences of graphic form that do not change the English sense have not been catalogued as substantive corrections.

[^j02-containing-compliance]: **包承.** The oracle’s “contain and comply” leaves the object unstated. Zhu supplies the noble person as the one accommodated by the petty person. Cheng emphasizes what the inferior person harbors inwardly: the intention to comply with those above for personal advantage. 大人否亨 then distinguishes the great person’s outward obstruction from the success or free course of the Way. The dash in the English signals that paradox; it does not add another event to the oracle.

[^j02-harboring-shame]: **包羞.** Zhu explains an intention to injure goodness that has not yet issued into action, and therefore notes the absence of an explicit misfortune or blame formula. Other commentators describe remaining in office without virtue or service. The oracle itself contains neither a direction to attack nor a promise that shame will necessarily produce reform. “Harboring” preserves the inward containment common to these readings without deciding in advance whether the person feels shame adequately or merely conceals what is shameful.

[^j02-mandate]: **有命 and 疇離祉.** Zhu identifies 命 as Heaven’s mandate; Cheng makes action under the ruler’s command the safeguard against suspicion and usurpation. Xiang explicitly brings together what Heaven commands and what the ruler establishes. 離 in this line is explained as 麗, attaching or participating, rather than separating. Thus one’s fellows *share in* blessing; they do not depart from it. These readings retain the historical relations of authority rather than substituting a modern, self-authorizing “personal destiny.”

[^j02-mulberries]: **“It may perish!” and secure attachment.** The repeated fear of destruction is not followed by a literal instruction to tie an object to a tree. Zhu’s later discussion explicitly says that vigilance itself gives rise to the image of being securely bound to a mulberry clump. Cheng’s deep roots and clustered growth explain the strength of that image. His historical judgments about Wang Yun and Li Deyu are retained as attributed examples; this translation has not independently adjudicated the causes of their political defeats.

[^j02-overturning]: **傾否 is active.** Wang Zongchuan deliberately distinguishes “overturn obstruction” from “obstruction is overturned.” Hu then contrasts the failing preservation of Peace with the active reversal of Obstruction. Cyclical language and responsible action therefore coexist in this section. The favorable final image is not presented as a guarantee that the mere passage of time resolves every obstructed situation.

[^j02-before-world]: **An allusion to Fan Zhongyan.** He Kai’s being anxious before the world and joyful after it echoes the conclusion of Fan Zhongyan’s [*Record of Yueyang Tower*](https://zh.wikisource.org/w/index.php?title=岳陽樓記&oldid=9467826). He applies that ordering of concern and enjoyment to the end of Obstruction. The allusion illuminates the relation between apprehension and later joy; it does not identify a historical person as the oracle’s subject.

[^j02-fellowship]: **同人.** “Fellowship” renders uniting with other people. 人 is not restricted to males, even though many of the historical examples concern male officeholders. 同 includes agreement and common purpose, but the text repeatedly distinguishes this from agreeing with one’s faction regardless of right and wrong. The large claims made for morally correct agreement belong to the commentators’ ethical vision. Here fellowship is justified by shared righteousness, not by numbers alone.

[^j02-open-fields]: **門, 宗, 郊, 野.** The gate, clan, outskirts, and open fields articulate different degrees and forms of association. The commentaries place 野 beyond 郊 and use its openness to express freedom from private attachment. The English does not supply fixed distances for these zones. Crossing the great river preserves the concrete image of a difficult passage; it is not flattened into the generic phrase “take a risk.” Whether the passage is beneficial depends on the kind of fellowship described.

[^j02-li-reading]: **離 and 麗.** E has 德為離, while S has 德為麗. The preceding sentence in both says that the yin line 麗, “clings,” between two yang lines. The English adopts the explanatory sense “attachment,” supported explicitly by S, rather than merely repeating the trigram name Li or reading the modern sense “separation.” 文明 is rendered “luminous clarity” here, retaining both patterned refinement and illumination rather than importing the modern abstract meaning “civilization.”

[^j02-clan]: **A correct pairing may still be narrow.** The second and fifth are centered and of opposite polarities, in the standard corresponding positions. The criticism of the second does not deny that structure. It argues that, in a hexagram whose standard is expansive fellowship, exclusive attachment is insufficient. The relation between the hexagram as a whole and a single line therefore matters more than a mechanical conclusion that a proper response must always be favorable.

[^j02-three-years]: **Concealed troops and three years.** The line presents a stalled, fearful conflict. Its final 不興 means not rising to military action; it is not a general instruction to avoid all action for a timed period. “Three years” is retained exactly, without converting it into another unit or asserting a calendar forecast. Cheng explicitly withholds a misfortune formula because the threatened action has not been launched. Cheng’s explanation concerns the gap between the display of force and the ability to act.

[^j02-rival-lines]: **Who attacks whom?** Zhu and Cheng relate the third and fourth to a struggle over the second and its connection with the fifth. The compilers instead pair the third with the top and the fourth with the initial line, reading a cycle from union into difference and back into union. The two accounts cannot be merged without changing their arguments. “Unable to attack” also receives a moral interpretation: the fourth recognizes the wrongness of its claim and turns back. It need not signify simply a failed assault against superior defenses.

[^j02-weeping-laughter]: **Private affection or rightful unity?** Cheng refuses to interpret the fifth as a model ruler here, because exclusive attachment to the second falls short of universal fellowship. Zhu emphasizes the pair’s union in righteousness and principle, and Wu Yueshen defends that account against the charge of partiality. The contrast is retained in full. The fragrant 蘭 in the quoted Appended Statements is rendered conventionally as “orchids”; the point is the lingering quality of the words, not a botanical identification. The metal-cutting image expresses undivided purpose, while Yang Wanli separately places the ruler’s heart above an army as the decisive power.

[^j02-forced-identifications]: **A limit on diagrammatic literalism.** The compilers say that attempts by ruler and subject to reach one another naturally invite jealousy and obstruction. Their comparison with “my counterpart has an affliction” in The Cauldron is a comparison of relational circumstances. They expressly reject forcing a particular line to stand for every actor mentioned. This methodological warning belongs to the Chinese commentary itself and is not a modern objection added by the translator.

[^j02-world-family]: **All under Heaven and the central states.** The concluding 四海為一家 and 中國為一人 are rendered as a single household across the four seas and the central states as one person. These are the source’s images of comprehensive ethical and social unity, not references to the boundaries or citizenship of a present-day nation-state. Liang’s conclusion also explains why the absence of blame or regret in an individual line need not equal the fuller success promised by the whole hexagram.

[^j02-great-possession]: **Possessing the great, not merely possessing much.** The established title *Great Possession* allows Zheng Ruxie’s emphasis: 大 can denote the yang lines, here interpreted as worthy people, who respond to an open, yielding center. Other commentators also speak of material abundance. Those meanings coexist; neither has been removed. 元亨 is rendered “great success” in the oracle, while Zhu’s explicit moral gloss 大善 is translated “great goodness” in his explanation.

[^j02-cauldron-judgment]: **Why only Great Possession and The Cauldron?** A reader checking the received text will find 鼎，元吉亨, not simply 元亨. The compilers’ claim presupposes the reading adopted by both Zhu and Cheng under The Cauldron: each expressly treats 吉 as an intrusive character. See [Juan Seven, the beginning of The Cauldron](../source/juan-07.md#eee-4622). The present passage has not been corrected to conceal that premise, and the Chinese text of The Cauldron has not been changed. This is an important example of a commentarial judgment depending on an emendation argued elsewhere.

[^j02-possession-caution]: **匪咎 and 無咎.** The first denies blame in the present condition; the second makes freedom from blame dependent upon maintaining an awareness of difficulty. Cheng additionally distinguishes wealth itself from the faults people commit through it. Hu warns against turning the initial absence of fault into carelessness. The English retains the apparent repetition because it is the center of the explanation, not a redundant phrase to be edited away.

[^j02-offering]: **亨 as 享.** Zhu reads 亨 here as 享, presenting offerings at court, while Cheng reads it through 亨通, free communication with the Son of Heaven. Both understand a lord who does not treat his possession as exclusively private. The oracle follows Zhu’s sacrificial or tributary reading, but Cheng’s alternative remains explicit. “Son of Heaven” translates 天子, and “lord” renders 公 in the political rank described by the commentators; neither title is silently modernized into an employer or a psychological function.

[^j02-heng-xiang-correction]: **A consequential one-character error.** E prints 古文無亨字 in the quotation from Zhu’s conversations, whereas S prints 古文無享字. The English adopts S: ancient writing had no *separate* 享 graph in the account being given, and the readings later written 亨, 享, and 烹 were represented interchangeably. This restores the point of the ensuing argument rather than making Zhu say that the very graph he is discussing did not exist. His historical account of the graphs is translated as his account; it is not offered as a complete modern palaeographic reconstruction. The distinction between court presentation, successful passage, and cooking is essential to the disagreement.

[^j02-peng]: **彭 remains uncertain.** Zhu expressly says that its sound and meaning are not fully known. The oracle’s “not excessive in abundance” follows Cheng’s interpretation of flourishing multitude, also reflected in his two citations from the *Odes*. This is a declared interpretive choice, not a claim that the word has been conclusively decoded. “Red horses with white bellies” renders 騵 in the second citation; the four-horse team and the abundance of war-horses belong to the poem as Cheng invokes it. No textual substitution for 彭 has been made.

[^j02-trust-awe]: **厥孚交如，威如.** The paired phrases describe sincerity that establishes mutual trust and a bearing that inspires awe. The second is not erased in favor of an exclusively gentle model, nor expanded into violence that the words do not state. The repeated 如 gives the quality or manner of each condition. Zhu and Cheng both make dignity and authority a corrective to excessive yieldingness in this particular situation.

[^j02-heavens-aid]: **Whose virtue receives the final blessing?** Zhu and Cheng assign treading in trustworthiness, thinking of compliance, and honoring the worthy to the top line, which follows the fifth below it. Guo Yong, Zheng Ruxie, Wang Zongchuan, and Hu Bingwen instead read the top as completing the fifth’s virtue: the fifth honors the worthy top line above itself. The compilers prefer the latter explanation. Their point is not a minor change of pronoun but a rule of interpretation: the last line can complete the story of a governing line without itself becoming the ruler. “Heaven’s aid” retains the religious and ethical force of 天祐 rather than reducing it to luck.
