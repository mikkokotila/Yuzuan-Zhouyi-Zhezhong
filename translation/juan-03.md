## 15. Qian 謙 — Modesty

<!-- Source: https://www.eee-learning.com/book/4561 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:001 -->

![Qian: Gen below, Kun above](../assets/eee-learning/2b7008e56984-yi15b.png) **Qian 謙 — Modesty** — Gen below; Kun above.[^j03-modesty]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:002 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Concerning Modesty, the *Sequence of the Hexagrams* says: “Those who possess greatness must not become full of themselves; therefore Great Possession is followed by Modesty.” Once one's possessions are great, one must not reach the point of overflowing fullness: one must practice modesty and self-diminishment. This is why Great Possession is followed by Modesty. The hexagram has Kun above and Gen below: a mountain within the earth. The earth's body lies low; a mountain is something high and great, yet here it dwells beneath the earth. This is the image of modesty. To possess lofty virtue yet occupy a humble place: this is the meaning of Modesty.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:002 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:003 -->

### Modesty: success. The noble person carries things through to the end.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:004 -->

**Original Meaning.** Modesty means possessing something without claiming it for oneself. Stillness within and compliance without express its meaning. The mountain is supremely high and the earth supremely low, yet the mountain bends down and rests beneath it: this is the image of modesty. If the diviner is like this, there will be free passage and a successful completion. “Having an end” means first bending and afterward extending oneself.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:005 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Modesty has a way to success. To possess virtue without claiming it for oneself is called modesty. When people conduct themselves with modesty and deference, where can they go without finding free passage? “The noble person carries things through to the end”: the noble person's intention rests in modesty and deference, with an understanding of principle. Thus, delighting in Heaven, such a person does not compete; inwardly fulfilled, such a person yields and gives way without boasting. At ease in the practice of modesty, the noble person does not change throughout life. By lowering oneself, one wins greater respect; by keeping oneself in the shade, one's virtue shines more brightly. This is what it means for the noble person to carry things through to the end. Petty people, by contrast, contend whenever they desire something and boast whenever they possess some virtue. Even if they strive to imitate modesty, they cannot practice it at ease or hold firmly to it, and cannot carry it through to the end.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:005 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:006 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:006 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:007 -->

**Feng Yi:** In hexagrams consisting of one yang and five yin, the images are established as follows. With the single yang at the top or bottom, they are Splitting Apart and Return, imaging the waning and waxing of yang energy. With it in a central place, they are The Army and Holding Together, imaging the one to whom the multitude turns. But the third and fourth places lie at the junction of the two trigrams, in the middle of the six strokes. Thus the figure in which yang withdraws from above to dwell below is Modesty; the figure in which it stirs and emerges from below into the upper part is Enthusiasm. This is the original intention in observing the strokes and establishing the images.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:007 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:008 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Neither Cheng's *Commentary* nor the *Original Meaning*, in explaining the hexagram's name, draws on the meaning of the third nine. Yet this line is in fact what constitutes the hexagram: one can see this by turning to its inverse, Enthusiasm. The reason the Master's *Commentary on the Judgments* does not single it out is that the Duke of Zhou's line statement is the same as the hexagram statement, making it readily apparent that the third line governs the hexagram's formation. Feng's explanation may supplement and complete the others.[^j03-governing-line]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:008 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:009 -->

### First Six: The modest, modest noble person. Undertaking to cross the great river: good fortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:009 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:010 -->

**Original Meaning.** Yielding and occupying the lowest place: this is modesty at its utmost, the conduct of a noble person. With this, where could one undertake a difficulty and fail to cross it? Thus, if the diviner is like this, it is beneficial to undertake the river crossing.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:010 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:011 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The first six, yielding and compliant, occupies the situation of Modesty and also the lowest place in the hexagram. This is the utmost in taking a humble position: modest, and still more modest. Hence “modest, modest.” One who can be like this is a noble person. Those who place themselves in the deepest modesty receive everyone's support. Even when undertaking dangers and difficulties, they suffer no harm—how much less in easy and peaceful circumstances! Where would there not be good fortune? A question: “At the beginning of Modesty, yieldingness occupies the lowest place. Is this not excessive modesty?” The answer: “For yieldingness to dwell below is simply its normal condition. We see only modesty at its utmost, hence ‘modest, modest’; no fault is yet apparent.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:011 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:012 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:012 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:013 -->

**Xun Shuang:** The first line is lowest of all; hence “modest, modest.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:013 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:014 -->

**Hu Yigui:** Crossing a river calls for deliberate steadiness, not haste. To cross by the Way of “modest, modest” is simply to yield, remain behind, and not compete to be first. Naturally, not one attempt in ten thousand goes astray; hence good fortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:014 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:015 -->

**Hu Bingwen:** Modesty is governed by the third nine; hence both its line statement and the hexagram statement say that the noble person carries things through to the end. Why, then, does the first line also speak of a noble person? The third is at the top of the lower trigram: accomplished yet modest, it is the noble person in a high position. The first is at the bottom of the lower trigram: modest and still more modest, it is the noble person in a low position. Those above are honored and radiant; those below are humble yet cannot be overstepped. Both are ways in which the noble person reaches a successful end. “Undertaking to cross the great river: good fortune”: one may even use this to overcome adversity—how much more in ordinary life!

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:015 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:016 -->

### Six in the Second Place: Modesty that sounds forth. Steadfastness in what is right brings good fortune.[^j03-sounding-modesty]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:016 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:017 -->

**Original Meaning.** Yielding and compliant, centered and correct, one becomes known through modesty. This is both correct and fortunate; hence the prognostication.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:017 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:018 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The second line, yielding and compliant, occupies the center: this is the virtue of modesty accumulated within. As that virtue fills the inner being, it issues outward and becomes apparent in the voice and countenance; hence “modesty that sounds forth.” Dwelling centrally and attaining correctness, it possesses centered and correct virtue; hence “steadfastness in what is right brings good fortune.” In such expressions, sometimes the meaning is “correct and also fortunate,” and sometimes “fortunate if one attains correctness.” The correctness and good fortune of the second six are already its own.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:018 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:019 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:019 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:020 -->

**Su Shi:** When the male calls, the female responds; thus the *Changes* conveys the call and response of yin and yang through sounding forth. What makes Modesty Modesty is the third line. The second six is its neighbor, and the top line its counterpart; therefore both answer it and sound forth in modesty.[^j03-qian-textual]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:020 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:021 -->

### Nine in the Third Place: Modest in accomplishment. The noble person carries things through to the end. Good fortune.[^j03-labor-modesty]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:021 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:022 -->

**Original Meaning.** The hexagram's only yang occupies the top of the lower trigram. Firm and correctly placed, it is the one to whom those above and below turn. To possess merit and accomplishments and yet be modest is especially difficult; therefore there is successful completion and good fortune. If the diviner is like this, the response will accord with it.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:022 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:023 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The third line possesses the virtue of yang firmness yet dwells in the lower trigram. The many yin look up to it, and it treads in its proper place at the top of the lower part. Above, the ruler entrusts it with responsibility; below, the multitude follows it. It possesses merit and accomplishments while maintaining modest virtue: hence “modest in accomplishment.” Among the ancients, the Duke of Zhou exemplified this. Bearing the great responsibility for all under Heaven and serving a young and vulnerable sovereign above him, he disciplined himself through modesty and reverence, watchful as though in fear. He could indeed be called accomplished yet modest. Yet, having attained this, one must also be a noble person who carries the practice through to the end; then there is good fortune. Delighting in high position and enjoying superiority are ordinary human inclinations. Few can be modest even in ordinary circumstances—how much less when they have accomplishments deserving honor! Even if one knows the goodness of modesty and forces oneself to practice it, one cannot sustain it while the heart still cherishes pride in its achievements. To ask for successful completion in such a case is impossible. Only the noble person practices modesty and compliance at ease, as ordinary conduct, enduring without change. This is what is called “having an end”; having an end brings good fortune. The third nine is firm and correctly placed, able to carry things through. This line's virtue is greatest, and so the wording of its Image receives particular emphasis.[^j03-duke-zhou]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:023 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:024 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:024 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:025 -->

**Wang Bi:** It occupies the summit of the lower trigram and treads in its proper place. No yang above or below divides its people with it; the many yin look up to it, and none precedes it in honor. Serving those above and meeting those below, accomplished yet modest without slackening: hence good fortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:025 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:026 -->

**Wang Zongchuan:** This one line constitutes Modesty as a hexagram. Thus the hexagram's virtue is expressed as “the noble person carries things through to the end,” and the third nine is the line that truly answers to it.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:026 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:027 -->

**Hu Bingwen:** King Wen's hexagram statement says, “Modesty: success. The noble person carries things through to the end.” In the statement for the third line, the Duke of Zhou replaces “success” with “good fortune” and adds “accomplishment” before “modesty.” For modesty is not the difficulty; being accomplished and yet modest is the difficulty. The third nine's accomplishments deserve a place above, yet its position remains below: this is what is meant by accomplished yet modest. Qian's third line speaks of a noble person; Kun's third speaks of successful completion. Modesty's third combines the prognostications of Qian and Kun. Its “accomplishment” is Qian's vigor throughout the day, while its modesty is Kun's keeping its brilliance within.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:027 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:028 -->

**Wu Yueshen:** The scholars all take “the noble person carries things through to the end” as one phrase. Yet, judging by the first six's “the modest, modest noble person,” this line should be phrased “the noble person modest in accomplishment.” The *Commentary on the Images* makes this clear.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:028 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:029 -->

### Six in the Fourth Place: Nothing is unfavorable. Give expression to modesty.[^j03-wielding-modesty]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:029 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:030 -->

**Original Meaning.** Yielding and correctly placed, above yet able to lower oneself: the prognostication is that nothing is unfavorable. Yet because it dwells above the third nine, it is admonished to give still fuller expression to its modesty, showing that it does not dare rest complacently in its position.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:030 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:031 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The fourth line dwells in the upper trigram, very close to the ruler's place. The ruler at the fifth six also conducts himself with modest yieldingness. The third nine, moreover, possesses great merit and virtue, is entrusted with responsibility from above, and is revered by the multitude. Yet the fourth occupies a place above it. It must therefore serve the ruler of modest virtue with reverence and apprehension, and defer with humility to the minister who is modest in accomplishment. In every action and undertaking, nothing is more beneficial than giving expression to modesty. *Hui* suggests spreading something abroad, as when a hand sweeps outward. In movement and rest, advance and withdrawal, one must express modesty, because one occupies a place of many apprehensions and also stands above a worthy minister.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:031 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:032 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:032 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:033 -->

**Liang Yin:** Yielding and correctly placed, above yet able to lower itself, the fourth six may indeed be called modest: nothing is unfavorable. Yet it stands close to the ruler and above a meritorious minister; therefore it is admonished to express its modesty still more fully. There are certainly ministers who maintain yieldingness, keep to correctness, and do not compete with others. Yet some are obscure about the principles of affairs and fail to decline or accept appropriately, receiving emolument without accomplishment or holding a title without the substance to match it. Such people have lost the Way of Modesty. One cannot neglect the warning.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:033 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:034 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** The *Original Meaning* divides “nothing is unfavorable; give expression to modesty” into two clauses; Cheng's *Commentary* takes it as one. Judging by the Master's *Commentary on the Images*, Cheng's interpretation is nearer the meaning.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:034 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:035 -->

### Six in the Fifth Place: Not wealthy, yet able to enlist one's neighbors. It is beneficial to invade and chastise. Nothing is unfavorable.[^j03-modesty-force]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:035 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:036 -->

**Original Meaning.** Yielding and occupying the honored position, above yet able to be modest: hence the image of being able to enlist one's neighbors without wealth. Many indeed follow it. If some remain unsubmissive, it is beneficial to campaign against them; nor is anything unfavorable in other affairs. If someone possesses this virtue, the prognostication applies accordingly.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:036 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:037 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The multitude turns toward wealth: material resources can gather people. But the fifth, with the dignity of the ruler's position, holds to modesty and compliance in receiving those below; the multitude turns toward it. Hence, without wealth, it can have its neighbors. “Neighbors” means those who are near: one wins people's closeness without wealth. A ruler who maintains modesty and compliance wins the allegiance of all under Heaven. Yet the ruler's Way must not value modest yieldingness alone. Martial authority must complement it; only then can one win and secure the submission of all under Heaven. Hence it is beneficial to undertake invasion and chastisement. Only when authority and virtue are both manifest does one fulfill everything appropriate to the ruler's Way, with nothing unfavorable. Because the fifth's modest yieldingness must be guarded against excess, this meaning is brought forth.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:037 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:038 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:038 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:039 -->

**Yang Wanli:** With the dignity of the sovereign, the fifth embodies modest, yielding virtue, humbly making no claim to the advantages of exalted position and wealth. This is the fullness of modest virtue within the hexagram. Extending the heart that is “not wealthy,” it brings its ministers and neighbors into willing accord. Where could it go without benefit? “It is beneficial to invade and chastise” merely selects the greatest undertaking as an example.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:039 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:040 -->

**Hu Bingwen:** The word “modesty” was brought forth by Boyi when Yu campaigned against the Miao. Why does the fifth six alone not say “modesty,” but instead say that invasion and chastisement are beneficial? “Not wealthy” means that the fifth six is open at the center and capable of modesty. “Able to enlist one's neighbors” means that none fail to submit to its modesty. If any still refuse submission under these circumstances, campaigning against them is certainly appropriate.[^j03-miao]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:040 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:041 -->

### Top Six: Modesty that sounds forth. It is beneficial to dispatch an army to chastise one's own domain.[^j03-own-domain]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:041 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:042 -->

**Original Meaning.** Modesty at its utmost becomes renowned and wins people's support; thus one may dispatch an army. Yet because this line is yielding in constitution and has no official position, it may chastise only its own domain.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:042 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:043 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The sixth line, yielding, occupies the utmost of yielding compliance and also the utmost of Modesty: it is supremely modest. Yet while supremely modest, it occupies the highest place, and has not fulfilled its intention to be humble. Thus this intention issues in its voice. Moreover, yieldingness at the utmost of Modesty must become apparent in voice and countenance; hence “modesty that sounds forth.” Although it dwells in a place without official position and does not bear responsibility for the world's affairs, people's own conduct must always balance firmness and yieldingness. The top is the extreme of modesty; if carried too far, modesty becomes a fault. Hence the benefit lies in using martial firmness to govern oneself. One's domain is what belongs privately to oneself. “Dispatching an army” means employing martial firmness; “chastising one's own domain” means governing what is private to oneself.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:043 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:044 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:044 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:045 -->

**Yang Shi:** When the noble person's conduct fails to achieve its purpose, the noble person turns back and seeks the cause within. Hence “it is beneficial to dispatch an army to chastise one's own domain.” The domain is what belongs privately to oneself; chastising it means self-government. No one has ever overcome private selfishness without firmness in self-mastery.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:045 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:046 -->

**Zhu Zhen:** Chastising one's own domain is not invasion and punishment; it means mastering oneself. Through self-mastery the noble person becomes sincere, and with sincerity nothing fails to respond. If something does not respond, sincerity has not yet reached its fullness.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:046 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:047 -->

**Classified Conversations of Master Zhu:** A question: “Modesty means not contending with others. Why, then, do the fifth and top lines both speak of invasion and chastisement or of dispatching an army as beneficial?” The reply: “Laozi says, ‘A great state lowers itself before a small state and thereby wins the small state; a small state lowers itself before a great state and thereby wins the great state.’ He also says, ‘When opposing armies engage, the one that grieves wins.’ Generally, modesty is itself a Way of using troops: one simply takes a step back. ‘One must face the task with apprehension’ carries this same intention.”[^j03-laozi]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:047 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:048 -->

**He Kai:** The chastisement extends only to one's own domain; one does not dare invade and attack others. This too is an image of modesty.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:048 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:049 -->

**General Discussion.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:049 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:050 -->

**Wang Bi:** “Good fortune, misfortune, regret, and humiliation arise from movement.” Movement originates in the pursuit of advantage. Hence food and drink necessarily bring contention, and contention necessarily brings the rising of a multitude. Yet no one who dwells in a place others dislike is harmed by those who act, and no one who occupies ground beyond competition is dispossessed by those who contend. Thus, although the six lines include incorrect placement, lack of correspondence, and riding upon the firm, none has misfortune, blame, regret, or humiliation: modesty governs them all. “Modesty is honored and radiant; lowly, it cannot be overstepped.” How true indeed!

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:050 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4561:051 -->

**Hu Yigui:** In Modesty, all three lower lines have good fortune without misfortune, and all three upper lines have benefit without harm. In the *Changes*, fortune and benefit are seldom so pure and complete. Such indeed is the efficacy of modesty.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4561:051 -->

## 16. Yu 豫 — Enthusiasm

<!-- Source: https://www.eee-learning.com/book/4564 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4564:001 -->

![Yu: Kun below, Zhen above](../assets/eee-learning/f2cee21dec64-yi16b.png) **Yu 豫 — Enthusiasm** — Kun below; Zhen above.[^j03-enthusiasm]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4564:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4564:002 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Concerning Enthusiasm, the *Sequence of the Hexagrams* says: “Possessing greatness and being able to remain modest necessarily brings delight; therefore Modesty is followed by Enthusiasm.” Its place in the sequence carries forward the meanings of the two preceding hexagrams. When one's possessions are great and one can remain modest, there is delight. *Yu* means ease, harmony, pleasure, and joy. The hexagram has Zhen above and Kun below: the image of compliant movement. Movement in harmony and compliance brings delight. The fourth nine governs movement, and all the yin above and below respond to it. Kun, moreover, supports it with compliance. Thus, when it moves, those above and below respond compliantly: this is the meaning of harmonious delight. In terms of the two images, thunder emerges above the earth. Yang was at first hidden and enclosed within the earth; when it moves and emerges, it sends forth its resounding voice, freely unfolding in harmonious delight. Hence Enthusiasm.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4564:002 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4564:003 -->

### Enthusiasm: it is beneficial to establish lords and dispatch armies.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4564:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4564:004 -->

**Original Meaning.** Enthusiasm is harmonious joy: people's hearts respond to those above in harmony and joy. The fourth nine is the single yang, and those above and below respond to it, allowing its purpose to be carried out. Moreover, Kun encountering Zhen means movement through compliance. Thus the hexagram is Enthusiasm, and its prognostication favors establishing rulers and employing armies.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4564:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4564:005 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Enthusiasm is movement in compliance. Its meaning is that benefit lies in establishing lords and dispatching armies. Establishing lords and setting up protective bulwarks are means of securing peace together throughout the world. When the lords are harmonious and compliant, the myriad people gladly submit. When armies are raised, harmony and joy in the multitude's hearts bring willing obedience and accomplishment. Thus the Way of glad enthusiasm favors establishing lords and dispatching armies. Furthermore, movement above and compliance below give the image of lords following the king and troops obeying commands. To rule myriad states and gather a great multitude, one cannot secure their obedience without harmony and joy.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4564:005 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4564:006 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4564:006 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4564:007 -->

**Kong Yingda:** It is called *Yu* in the sense of ease and enjoyment. Moving in harmony and compliance, one's movement does not oppose the multitude, and all delight in it. Hence Enthusiasm. Movement that gladdens the multitude favors establishing lords; movement through compliance allows one to dispatch an army.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4564:007 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4564:008 -->

**Qiu Fuguo:** Difficulty at the Beginning has Zhen but not Kun, and therefore speaks of establishing lords but not dispatching armies. Modesty has Kun but not Zhen, and therefore speaks of dispatching armies but not establishing lords. Here Zhen and Kun combine to form the hexagram, and so both are included.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4564:008 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4564:009 -->

### First Six: Sounding forth one's delight. Misfortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4564:009 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4564:010 -->

**Original Meaning.** A yielding yin petty person has powerful support above, which holds sway and governs affairs. Thus this person cannot contain the delight and proclaims it aloud: a way to misfortune. Hence the prognostication. The hexagram takes its name from harmonious joy, but the hexagram statement concerns the joy of the multitude. Apart from the fourth nine, which agrees with the hexagram as a whole, the line statements all concern delight in oneself. This accounts for their differing good and ill fortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4564:010 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4564:011 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The first six dwells below with yin yieldingness, and the fourth, which governs Enthusiasm, corresponds with it. This is a petty person without centeredness or correctness, favored by one above in a time of delight. Its wishes are fulfilled to the utmost, and it cannot contain its pleasure, which bursts forth in its voice. Such frivolity and shallowness necessarily end in misfortune. “Sounding forth” means issuing in sound.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4564:011 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4564:012 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4564:012 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4564:013 -->

**Shi Jie:** The fourth governs Enthusiasm, and the first corresponds with it. When petty people attain their wishes, they carry their desires to extremes and thereby reach misfortune. The expression of this in vocal proclamation is delight taken to excess.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4564:013 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4564:014 -->

**Su Shi:** What makes Enthusiasm Enthusiasm is the fourth line, and the first answers it; hence “sounding forth.” Having no source of delight in itself, it depends upon its counterpart for delight. Misfortune is inevitable.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4564:014 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4564:015 -->

**Wang Yinglin:** Modesty sounding forth brings good fortune; delight sounding forth brings misfortune. Sound is the heart's voice issuing outward.

## 17. Sui 隨 — Following

<!-- Source: https://www.eee-learning.com/book/4565 -->

![Sui: Zhen below, Dui above](../assets/eee-learning/0ec999ab4049-yi17b.png) **Sui 隨 — Following** — Zhen below; Dui above.

**Cheng's Commentary.** Concerning Following, the *Sequence of the Hexagrams* says: “Enthusiasm necessarily brings following; therefore it is followed by Following.” The Way of pleasure and delight is something others follow: hence Following comes after Enthusiasm. The hexagram has Dui above and Zhen below. Dui is pleasure; Zhen is movement. Moving gladly and taking pleasure in movement both express following. A woman follows another; the youngest daughter following the eldest son expresses following. Moreover, Zhen is thunder and Dui is the marsh. Thunder resounds within the marsh, and the marsh follows its movement: the image of Following. In terms of hexagram transformation, Qian's top line comes to occupy the bottom of Kun, while Kun's first line goes to occupy the top of Qian: yang comes and lowers itself before yin. When yang lowers itself before yin, yin necessarily follows gladly. This too is Following's meaning. In the constitution of hexagrams, the meanings of the two trigrams are considered; sometimes the meanings of particular lines are also taken, and sometimes the meaning of hexagram transformation is added. Following provides an especially full example.[^j03-sui-transformations]

### Following: great success. It is beneficial to be steadfast in what is right. No blame.

**Original Meaning.** Following means going along with another. In terms of hexagram transformation, it comes from Oppression when a nine moves to the first place; it also comes from Biting Through when a nine moves to the fifth. When it comes from Before Completion, it combines these two transformations. All convey the firm coming to follow the yielding. In terms of the two trigrams, this side moves and the other is pleased: also a meaning of following. Hence Following. If one can follow others and others come to follow oneself, the two sides go along with one another, and free passage is easy. Hence the prognostication of great success. Yet benefit must lie in steadfast correctness before one can be without blame. If what one follows is not correct, even great success will not prevent blame. **Yu Yan:** Following's six lines take their meaning specifically from neighboring and following one another, not from correspondence. The first nine belongs to Zhen, which is governed by its firm line: this is the governing one. Although a governing office values constancy, in the time of Following one must not cling to customary practice without understanding change. What is change? Keeping pace with the time and acting through appropriate discretion, without claiming the position of master for oneself. Hence “what governs changes.” The first nine is the line that governs the formation of the hexagram. Since the governing one cannot simply follow another, the text says “associate,” not “follow.” Being bound means following with a clinging attachment that will not let go. The second six, third six, and top six are all yielding yin in nature: they cling and follow without letting go. Thus all three speak of being bound.[^j03-sui-relations]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4565:018 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4565:019 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** The firm governs yin; hence it is called the governing one. Yang governing and yin following it is the normal, correct relation. Here, however, the firm lowers itself before the yielding: this is its change. Hence “what governs changes.” Yet following when the time calls for following is change that does not lose correctness. Therefore one can attain good fortune, and going beyond the gate to associate brings accomplishment.

### Six in the Second Place: Bound to the boy, one loses the grown man.[^j03-boy-man]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4565:020 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4565:021 -->

**Original Meaning.** The first yang is below and near; the fifth yang, the proper correspondent, is far away. The second, yielding yin, cannot preserve itself while waiting for its proper correspondent. Hence this image. Its misfortune and humiliation are apparent and need not be stated.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4565:021 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4565:022 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The second corresponds with the fifth but adjoins the first. Following gives precedence to what is near, and yieldingness cannot maintain a firm hold. Thus the admonition says: if bound to the boy, one loses the grown man. The first yang below is the boy; the fifth, the proper correspondent above, is the grown man. If the second binds its intention to the first, it loses its proper correspondence with the fifth nine: this is losing the grown man. To bind oneself to the boy and lose the grown man is to abandon proper correspondence and follow what is not proper. The blame is great. The second possesses centered and correct virtue, however, and need not necessarily reach this point. In the time of Following, it should be warned.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4565:022 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4565:023 -->

### Six in the Third Place: Bound to the grown man, one loses the boy. In following, what one seeks is obtained. It is beneficial to remain steadfast in what is right.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4565:023 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4565:024 -->

**Original Meaning.** The grown man is the fourth nine; the boy is again the first. The third binds itself to the nearby fourth and loses the first. Its image is exactly the reverse of the second six's. The fourth, yang, holds responsibility, and the third follows it; what it seeks will certainly be obtained. Yet this is not proper correspondence, and there is a suspicion of improper flattery. Hence this prognostication, with the further warning to remain steadfast in what is right.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4565:024 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4565:025 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The grown man is the fourth nine, and the boy is the first. Yang above is the grown man; yang below is the boy. Although the third belongs to the same trigram as the first, it is immediately next to the fourth and therefore binds itself to the fourth. Generally, yielding yin cannot stand independently and constantly attaches itself to what is near. Binding itself upward to the fourth, it loses the first below. Abandoning the first and following upward accords with what is appropriate in Following. Following upward is good: darkness following clarity, or an affair following goodness, is following upward. Turning one's back on the right to follow the wrong, abandoning clarity to pursue darkness, is following downward. The fourth likewise lacks correspondence and has no one following it. Once it obtains the following of the nearby third, it must receive it with closeness and goodwill. Thus, when the third follows the fourth, whatever it seeks will be obtained. To follow one above and be accepted is already to obtain what one seeks; moreover, particular requests may also be fulfilled. Even so, one must never bend principle and violate the Way in following a superior, merely currying affection and pleasure to get what one wants. Such conduct is the petty person's crooked flattery in pursuit of advantage. Hence “it is beneficial to remain steadfast in what is right.” When one places oneself in correctness, what one seeks and obtains concerns proper affairs: this is the noble person's following.

### Nine in the Fourth Place: Following brings a gain. Steadfastness brings misfortune. With sincerity in the Way, acting through clarity—what blame?[^j03-gaining-followers]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4565:030 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4565:031 -->

**Original Meaning.** The fourth nine, firm at the bottom of the upper trigram, shares virtue with the fifth. Thus its prognostication is that following brings a gain. Yet its influence encroaches upon the fifth, and therefore there is misfortune even with correctness. Only sincerity in the Way together with clarity can keep the superior secure and win the following of those below, allowing freedom from blame. A diviner bearing responsibility in the time should examine this warning carefully.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4565:031 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4565:032 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The fourth nine, with the capacity of yang firmness, occupies the highest ministerial place. If following brings it a gain, there is misfortune even with correctness. “A gain” means obtaining the hearts of all under Heaven in allegiance to oneself. The minister's Way requires favor and authority to issue entirely from above, and the multitude's hearts to follow the ruler. If people's hearts follow the minister personally, this leads to danger and suspicion: hence misfortune. What should one in this position do? Simply accumulate sincerity within, let every action accord with the Way, and conduct oneself with clear discernment: then what blame could there be? Among the ancients, Yi Yin, the Duke of Zhou, and Kongming practiced this. Their virtue reached the people, who followed them. Their winning the people's allegiance served to accomplish their rulers' undertakings and secure their states. Utmost sincerity preserved within is “having sincerity”; no undertaking departing from the middle Way is “being in the Way.” Only clear discernment enables this: hence “through clarity.” What further fault or blame could there be? Thus those below trust them, and those above do not suspect them. Their position is supreme without suggesting pressure upon the sovereign; their influence is weighty without the fault of monopolizing authority. None but sages and the greatly worthy can do this. Next to them is someone such as Guo Ziyi of Tang, whose authority shook his sovereign, yet the sovereign did not suspect him. This too came from inward sincerity and the absence of grave errors in his conduct. Without clear discernment, could he have been like this?[^j03-sui-readings]

### Nine in the Fifth Place: Sincerity toward the good. Good fortune.

**Cheng's Commentary.** The fifth nine occupies the honored position, attains correctness, and is substantial at its center: its inward sincerity lies in following goodness. ### Top Six: Hold and bind the person; then bind the attachment fast. The king uses this in making an offering at the Western Mountain.[^j03-binding-offering]

**Original Meaning.** At the extreme of Following, the bond of following is firm and cannot be undone. Their hearts were bound in following as firmly as this. Through it he could make his royal enterprise flourish at the Western Mountain. The Western Mountain is Mount Qi: Zhou's royal enterprise arose there. Dwelling at Following's extreme is certainly going too far. **Editorial Judgment.** The first line of the hexagram is firm and lowers itself before the yielding second; likewise the firmness of the fifth nine lowers itself before the yielding top. Yet the scholars' explanations of these two upper lines do not address this. To say that the fifth nine's “sincerity toward the good” means correspondence with the second six is still possible, but their account of the top six's binding then has no foundation. In the fifth and top lines of the *Changes*, a fifth six lowering itself before a top nine signifies honoring the worthy: Great Possession, Great Restraint, Nourishment, and The Cauldron are examples. A fifth nine close to a top six signifies association with the wrong person: Great Excess, Influence, Breakthrough, and Dui are examples. Yet the improper private attachment between a fifth nine and top six is specifically drawn from the Dui trigram, because movement through mutual pleasure easily falls into impropriety. This hexagram alone, though it too contains Dui, takes as its meaning the firm lowering itself before the yielding. Thus the fifth nine and top six signify following one another, not impropriety. Hence the fifth nine says “sincerity toward the good,” distinguishing it from Dui's trust in what strips away. At the top six, the text says neither “bound to the boy” nor “bound to the grown man,” but merely “hold and bind the person.” It then says, “The king uses this in making an offering at the Western Mountain,” making clear that the attachment is to the king. Three lines in the *Changes* say “the king uses in making an offering.” All mean that the king employs a person like the one represented by the line to make offerings to mountains, rivers, and the Supreme Ruler; they do not mean that the line itself is the king. Worthy people are born of the mountains and rivers and selected by the Supreme Ruler. Thus, when appointed to preside over sacrifice, the hundred spirits enjoy their offerings and Heaven accepts them. This also shows that nothing better fulfills Heaven's heart than a king's employment of the worthy. This line and Gu's top line are exact inverse counterparts. In a time of Following, one remains bound and does not depart; in a time of Gu, one holds one's purpose high and does not serve. Each does only what is appropriate. Is one bound by emolument here, or fleeing the world there?[^j03-king-worthy]

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4565:053 -->

**General Discussion — Wang Zongchuan:** Half of Following's six lines are yin and half yang. Those with the capacity of yang firmness follow something without being bound: the first nine, fourth nine, and fifth nine. Thus the first has change, the fourth has a gain, and the fifth has sincerity toward the good: these follow without attachment. Those whose capacity is yielding compliance, in a time of Following, cannot avoid being bound: the second six, third six, and top six. Thus the second is bound to the boy and loses the grown man; the third is bound to the grown man and loses the boy; the top says “hold and bind the person.” These all remain subject to attachment.

## 18. Gu 蠱 — Decay

<!-- Source: https://www.eee-learning.com/book/4566 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4566:001 -->

![Gu: Xun below, Gen above](../assets/eee-learning/8d80cdd3b007-yi18b.png) **Gu 蠱 — Decay** — Xun below; Gen above.[^j03-decay]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4566:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4566:002 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Concerning Gu, the *Sequence of the Hexagrams* says: “Those who follow others with delight necessarily have affairs to attend to; therefore Following is followed by Gu.” Its place in the sequence carries forward the meaning of the two preceding hexagrams. Those who follow others gladly must have something to do. Without an affair, why the delight and why the following? This is why Gu follows Following. “Gu means affairs.” It is not that the word *gu* is defined as “affairs”; rather, Gu means that there are affairs requiring attention. The hexagram is wind below a mountain. Wind beneath a mountain meets it and turns back, disturbing things: this is the image of Gu. Gu means decay and disorder. Its written form combines insects and a vessel: insects in a vessel express corruption and decay. The *Zuo Tradition* says, “Wind fells the mountain; the woman bewilders the man.” The eldest daughter lowering herself before the youngest son disorders their feelings. Wind meets the mountain and turns back, disturbing everything: an image of affairs requiring attention. Hence “Gu means affairs.” Once decay has arisen, setting it right is also an affair. The hexagram's image shows how decay comes about; its capacities show how decay is set right.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4566:002 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4566:003 -->

### Decay: great success. It is beneficial to cross the great river. Three days before jia; three days after jia.[^j03-jia-days]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4566:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4566:004 -->

**Original Meaning.** Gu means decay at its extreme, with affairs requiring attention. In this hexagram, firm Gen dwells above and yielding Xun below. Above and below do not communicate: those below are abjectly deferential, and those above simply remain still. Thus the hexagram is Gu. Some say that “the firm above and yielding below” refers to hexagram transformation. When it comes from Adornment, the first line goes up and the second comes down; when it comes from The Well, the fifth goes up and the top comes down; when it comes from After Completion, both changes occur. These too are instances of the firm rising and the yielding descending, all accounting for Gu. When decay reaches its extreme, disorder should return to order. Thus the prognostication is great success and benefit in crossing the great river. *Jia* is the beginning of the days and the starting point of an affair. Three days before jia is xin; three days after jia is ding. When an earlier undertaking has passed its midpoint and approaches decay, one can renew oneself and thereby begin the next undertaking, not allowing matters to deteriorate completely. When the next undertaking has just begun and is still new, one must further bring earnest attentiveness to it, learning from the earlier failure so that it does not quickly decay. This is the sages' profound warning.[^j03-gu-transformations]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4566:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4566:005 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Once decay has occurred, the principle of restoring order is present. From antiquity, order has arisen in response to disorder; disorder opens the way to order. This is natural in principle. If one uses the capacities represented by the hexagram to remedy decay, great success can be achieved. The greatest work in remedying decay is overcoming the time's hardships, dangers, and obstructions. Hence “it is beneficial to cross the great river.” *Jia* is the first in a sequence and the beginning of an affair. The jia and yi used for days, a first-ranked residence, or a foremost ordinance all use jia to mean the first, the starting point of something. In remedying decay, one should deliberate over the three days before and after: investigate what precedes and follows to find a lasting way of rescuing affairs from their defects. “Before jia” means looking before the present matter and investigating how it came to be so. “After jia” means looking beyond it and considering what it may become. One day, two days, and on to three express depth of deliberation and reach of inquiry. Investigate why a condition arose, and one knows how to remedy it; consider what it may become, and one knows how to prepare. Skillful remedy removes earlier defects; skillful preparation allows later benefits to endure. This is how the sage kings of antiquity renewed the world and left their work to later generations. Those who remedy decay in later ages do not understand the sages' warning about before and after jia. Their deliberations are shallow and their undertakings short-sighted. Thus they labor to rescue the age without removing its disorder; accomplishment is not yet complete when defects have already arisen. Jia is the beginning of an undertaking; geng is the beginning of alteration. The establishment of institutions, government, and teaching is spoken of as jia, indicating its beginning. Issuing commands and promulgating orders is spoken of as geng: geng is like “change,” for something is being altered.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4566:005 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4566:006 -->

In a time that calls for action, one must not merely perpetuate old ordinances, but govern people through newly instituted commands.[^j03-gu-readings]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4566:010 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4566:011 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** In the two trigrams, a yang trigram occupies the upper place and a yin trigram the lower. In the six positions, firm lines dwell above and yielding lines below. Among the sixty-four hexagrams, this one likewise uniquely presents yin and yang, firmness and yieldingness, without interchange, and honored and lowly, above and below, without contact. Separation then produces a hundred defects, and the myriad affairs fall into ruin. This is the primary meaning of the hexagram's name Gu. When decay reaches its extreme, there is a principle of renewed free passage. Yet one must undertake a broad rescue from difficulty rather than grow accustomed to ease, and sustain the beginning and attend carefully to the end rather than act lightly. Hence the warnings about crossing the great river and the days before and after jia.

### First Six: Set right the father's decay. Having such a son, the father is without blame. Danger; in the end, good fortune.[^j03-fathers-decay]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4566:012 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4566:013 -->

**Original Meaning.** *Gan*, setting right, is like a tree's trunk, to which the branches and leaves attach and upon which they stand. Gu is an undertaking left in decay by one's predecessors; hence the various lines have images of father or mother. If a son can take responsibility for it, he sets it in order and raises it up anew. At the first six, decay is not yet deep and the matter is easy to remedy. Thus the prognostication is that, with such a son, the decay can be remedied and the father freed from blame. Yet there is danger as well. The diviner is admonished to act in this way and also recognize the danger and be watchful; then there is good fortune in the end.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4566:013 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4566:014 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Although the first six occupies the lowest place, the hexagram's formation proceeds from it, giving it a governing meaning. Governing from within and below signifies a son setting right the father's decay. The Way of doing so is to be equal to the task: then the father “has a son” and is free of blame. Otherwise, the son burdens his father. Thus watchfulness and apprehension are necessary for good fortune in the end. Occupying a low position while presiding over a superior's affairs naturally calls for caution. As for this yin line's capacity, although it can be deferential and compliant, its constitution is yielding yin; governing the repair from below without correspondence does not signify an ability to accomplish it. Yet to speak only of its inability would make the meaning exceedingly narrow. Thus the text speaks exclusively of the Way by which a son sets decay right: successful remedy avoids burdening the father, and apprehension allows good fortune in the end. In this way, the great rule for a son's remedy of inherited decay is fully displayed.

**Collected Explanations.**

The disaster of gu is not the work of a single day. It becomes apparent only across a generation; thus the lines all speak in terms of father and son.

**Hu Bingwen:** Some line statements speak from the standpoint of time and position, others from capacity and constitution. For example, Gu's first six is yin below, with a yielding correspondent: its capacity is insufficient to remedy decay. In terms of time, however, it is decay's beginning. Decay is not yet deep and the affair is still easy to remedy. Thus its prognostication is that, with such a son, the father can be free of blame. Yet once it is called decay, there is already danger. One must not neglect it because it is not yet deep. Hence the further warning: if the diviner recognizes the danger and is watchful, there is good fortune in the end.

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4566:018 -->

### Nine in the Second Place: Set right the mother's decay. One must not be rigidly steadfast.[^j03-mothers-decay]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4566:018 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4566:019 -->

**Original Meaning.** The second nine is firm and centered and corresponds above with the fifth six: an image of a son setting right the mother's decay while attaining the mean. Firmness supports yieldingness and remedies its decay; hence the further warning against being rigidly steadfast. The meaning is that one should enter through deference.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4566:019 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4566:020 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The second nine is yang and firm, and the fifth six responds to it: yang-firm ability below manages the affairs of yielding yin above. Hence the meaning is a son setting right the mother's decay. A firm yang minister assisting a yielding, weak ruler has a similar meaning. The second belongs to Xun and occupies a yielding place; compliance predominates in its meaning. This is the Way of setting right the mother's decay. A son should assist and guide his mother with yielding deference, bringing her into accord with rightness. If lack of compliance brings failure and decay, the fault is the son's. Is there no Way of gently guiding and supporting? When a woman is spoken of, yielding yin is understood. If the son presses forward his own Way of yang firmness and abruptly corrects and opposes her, he injures affection, and the harm is great. How, moreover, can he gain an entrance? He must bend himself, lower his intention, and receive and support her with deference and compliance, simply bringing her conduct into correctness and her affairs into order. Hence “one must not be steadfast”: one must not hold rigidly to the full force of one's firm uprightness. This is the middle Way. How, moreover, could one make her accomplish something extraordinarily lofty? Likewise, with a yielding, weak ruler, it is enough to expend one's sincerity and loyalty in bringing him to the middle Way. How could one make him undertake great deeds? Even the Duke of Zhou, with all his sagacity, assisted King Cheng—and King Cheng was not especially weak—yet could only make him King Cheng. Preserving the established achievement without losing the Way was enough; he certainly could not make him accomplish the deeds of Fuxi, the Yellow Emperor, Yao, or Shun. The second belongs to Xun and attains the center: it can act deferentially and compliantly while attaining the middle Way. This accords with “one must not be steadfast” and attains the Way of setting right the mother's decay.

**Collected Explanations.**

### Top Nine: Do not serve kings or lords. Hold one's undertaking high.[^j03-withdrawal-service]

**Original Meaning.** Yang firmness occupies the top, outside the affairs at hand: hence this image. Prognostication and admonition are both contained within it.

**Cheng's Commentary.** The top nine stands at Gu's end, with no binding correspondence below. It is outside the affairs at hand, in a place without service to perform. They preserve their lofty integrity and are not burdened by worldly business. Hence “do not serve kings or lords; hold one's undertaking high.” Among the ancients, Yi Yin and Taigong Wang in their early lives, and people such as Zengzi and Zisi, practiced this. They did not bend the Way to accommodate the times. Unable to put it into effect throughout the world, they honored their own persons, esteemed and cherished their undertakings, and simply preserved their resolve and integrity. There is more than one way for a scholar to hold an undertaking high. Some embrace the Way and virtue but, not meeting a suitable time, preserve their lofty integrity. Some know when to stop and what is sufficient, and withdraw to preserve themselves. Some assess their abilities and proper share and rest content without seeking recognition. Some maintain purity and independence, disdain the world's affairs, and keep only themselves unsullied. When the Image says, “The intention may be taken as a model,” it refers to advance and withdrawal that accord with the Way.[^j03-self-honoring-variant]

## 19. Lin 臨 — Approach

<!-- Source: https://www.eee-learning.com/book/4567 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4567:001 -->

![Lin: Dui below, Kun above](../assets/eee-learning/1c4750a2c8cb-yi19b.png) **Lin 臨 — Approach** — Dui below; Kun above.[^j03-approach]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4567:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4567:002 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Concerning Approach, the *Sequence of the Hexagrams* says: “Only after there are affairs can there be greatness; therefore Gu is followed by Approach. Approach means greatness.” Gu means affairs; with affairs, greatness becomes possible. Thus it is followed by Approach. Han Kangbo says: “An enterprise capable of greatness arises from affairs. The two yang are now growing and becoming strong and great; hence Approach.” The hexagram is earth above a marsh. Earth above the marsh forms a bank, adjoining and drawing close to the water; hence Approach. Nothing in the world draws closer or approaches more intimately than earth and water. Thus water upon the earth makes Holding Together, while earth above the marsh makes Approach. Approach includes overseeing people, attending to affairs, and whatever else one approaches. In the hexagram, the meaning is approaching the lower from above, as in overseeing the people.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4567:002 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4567:003 -->

### Approach: great success. It is beneficial to be steadfast in what is right. By the eighth month, there is misfortune.[^j03-eighth-month]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4567:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4567:004 -->

**Original Meaning.** Approach means advancing and pressing close upon something. Two yang gradually grow and press upon yin; hence Approach, the hexagram of the twelfth month. Moreover, the hexagram has Dui's pleasure below and Kun's compliance above; the second nine, firm in the center, corresponds with the fifth six above. Thus the diviner has great success, with benefit in correctness. Yet by the eighth month there should be misfortune. The eighth month means counting from Return's month of one yang to Retreat's month of two yin, when yin grows and yang withdraws. Some say it means the eighth month of the Xia calendar, whose hexagram is Viewing, Approach's inverted counterpart. The prognostication thereby also provides a warning.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4567:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4567:005 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** This speaks in terms of the hexagram's capacities. If the Way of Approach accords with those capacities, there is great success and correctness. The two yang are just growing below; yang's Way is moving toward strength. The sages warn in advance: although yang is now growing, by the eighth month its Way will decline, bringing misfortune. Generally, the sages give warnings precisely when things are growing strong. Considering decline while still flourishing allows one to guard against excessive fullness and plan for endurance. A warning given only after decline comes is too late. From antiquity, the world's peace and order have never lasted indefinitely without disorder, because people could not remain watchful amid flourishing. Not knowing to be watchful when strong, they become accustomed to peace and wealth, and pride and extravagance arise; delighting in ease and license, they let governing principles fall apart; forgetting disaster and disorder, they allow the seeds of trouble to sprout. Thus disorder approaches gradually and imperceptibly.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4567:005 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4567:006 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4567:006 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4567:007 -->

**Master Zhang:** Approach speaks of misfortune because, in the *Changes*' transformations of the lines, once yang reaches two, a warning is already given. One is warned before passing the middle, as in the meaning of treading on frost and knowing that solid ice is coming. Peace's third line, “No level ground without a slope; no going without a return,” is a warning about passing the middle.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4567:007 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4567:008 -->

**Classified Conversations of Master Zhu:** A question: “Does Approach mean not only the upper approaching the lower, but anything advancing and drawing close?” The reply: “Yes. Here the two yang advance upward from below; thus anything drawing close to another is Approach.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4567:008 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4567:009 -->

**Cheng Jiong:** Yang reaches its extreme at nine, and young yin is born at eight; yin's meaning is paired with the month. Yin reaches its extreme at six, and young yang returns at seven; yang's meaning is paired with the day.[^j03-seven-eight]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4567:009 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4567:010 -->

**Wang Yinglin:** There are three explanations of Approach's “eighth month.” One counts from chou to shen, reaching Obstruction; one from zi to wei, reaching Retreat; one from yin to you, reaching Viewing. The *Original Meaning* accepts both the Retreat and Viewing explanations. Return's “seven days” likewise has three explanations. One says that the hexagram-qi sequence begins with Inner Sincerity and reaches Return after six days and seven parts. One says that passing through Kun's six positions and reaching Return makes seven days. One says that the count runs from the birth of one yin in the fifth month, Coming to Meet, to the birth of one yang in the eleventh month. The *Original Meaning* takes the explanation from Coming to Meet to Return.[^j03-hexagram-qi]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4567:010 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4567:011 -->

**Hu Bingwen:** The various commentators define *lin* as nearness or greatness, seeing only the upper approaching the lower, not the firm approaching the yielding. The *Original Meaning* follows the use of lin in “as though approaching a deep abyss,” meaning advancing and coming close to the abyss. Here it means the firm advancing and pressing close upon the yielding. Return is yin at its extreme and yang's first arrival; Approach is two yang arriving and pressing upon yin. Thus Return has success and Approach great success. Return does not speak of benefit in steadfastness because it is the first sprouting of yang, with nothing not good. In Approach, the two yang gradually grow strong and can easily become unrestrained; hence the warning.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4567:011 -->

### First Nine: All-embracing approach. Steadfastness in what is right brings good fortune.[^j03-xian-approach]

**Original Meaning.** The hexagram has only two yang, which approach all four yin. Thus both lines have the image of all-embracing approach. The first nine is firm and correctly placed; hence its prognostication of correctness and good fortune.

Thus, through the correct Way, it gains the trust of one in office and can carry out its purpose. Accepted by the one above and able to practice its correct Way, it has good fortune. In other hexagrams, the first and top lines are not discussed as correctly or incorrectly placed, because beginning and ending carry greater weight. In Approach, however, the first's proper placement and correctness are emphasized. “Steadfastness in what is right brings good fortune” may mean already correct and also fortunate, fortunate if one attains correctness, or fortunate if one holds firmly to something. <!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4567:025 -->

Because that influence is great, one's virtue and undertakings should likewise be great: this is the intention of the hexagram and line statements. The first and second influence others through virtue, hence *xian*. Those who influence others through virtue regard their growing influence with concern, never with delight. The third six governs pleasure and lacks centered and correct virtue. It delights in its influence: hence sweet approach. What benefit can there be in indulging one's feelings through power and position? Yet pleasure at its extreme contains the possibility of anxiety. Once anxious, one recognizes that power and position are not occasions for pleasure, and the fault does not endure. This line exactly resembles the lament over failing to keep limits at Limitation's third line. Both belong to Dui.

The Way of Approach values nearness, so adjacency is called the utmost. The fourth occupies its correct place and responds below to the yang firmness of the first. Standing close to the ruler, maintaining correctness and employing the worthy, it approaches those below intimately. Thus there is no blame: its position and conduct are fitting.

### Six in the Fifth Place: Wise approach. What befits a great ruler. Good fortune.[^j03-wise-approach]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4567:034 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4567:035 -->

**Original Meaning.** Yieldingness dwells in the center and responds below to the second nine. Not relying upon oneself alone but entrusting others is the work of wisdom, what befits a great ruler, and a way to good fortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4567:035 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4567:036 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The fifth, yielding and centered within the trigram of compliance, occupies the honored position and responds below to the firm, centered minister at the second. It can rely upon the second, governing without laboring itself: approaching those below through wisdom. How could one person overseeing the vastness of the world attend to every affair by relying narrowly on oneself? Those who depend solely upon their own knowledge thereby reveal their lack of knowledge. Only by drawing upon the world's goodness and employing the world's perception and intelligence can one attend to everything. Not relying solely upon one's own knowledge makes that knowledge great. The fifth responds compliantly to the worthy person of centered firmness at the second nine and entrusts him with overseeing those below. Thus the fifth itself approaches the world through clear wisdom. This is what befits a great ruler, and its good fortune is evident.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4567:036 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

## 20. Guan 觀 — Viewing

<!-- Source: https://www.eee-learning.com/book/4568 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4568:001 -->

![Guan: Kun below, Xun above](../assets/eee-learning/8e2cab40f160-yi20b.png) **Guan 觀 — Viewing** — Kun below; Xun above.[^j03-viewing]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4568:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4568:002 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Concerning Viewing, the *Sequence of the Hexagrams* says: “Approach means greatness. Only when things are great can they be viewed; therefore Approach is followed by Viewing.” This is why Viewing follows Approach. Looking at something is *guan*; becoming an object of viewing for those below is also *guan*. A lookout tower is called guan because it is seen from below. A ruler looking upward at Heaven's Way and downward at the people's customs practices viewing; cultivating virtue and administering government so that the people look up to him makes him an object of viewing. Wind moves over the earth, touching every kind of thing: the image of comprehensive viewing. Two yang stand above and four yin below. Yang firmness occupies the honored place and is looked up to by the multitude beneath: this is Viewing's meaning. The individual lines, however, take only the meaning of looking and seeing, according to their particular circumstances.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4568:002 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4568:003 -->

### Viewing: washing the hands, without presenting the offering. Sincerity and reverent regard.[^j03-washing-offering]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4568:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4568:004 -->

**Original Meaning.** Viewing means having something to show others, becoming an object of their respect. The fifth nine dwells above, and four yin look up to it. Moreover, there is compliance within and deference without, while the fifth nine displays centeredness and correctness to the world. This is why the hexagram is Viewing. Washing the hands means cleansing them before sacrifice. Presenting the offering means bringing wine and food for sacrifice. *Yongran* describes a dignified, imposing bearing. The meaning is that, by perfecting one's purity and not lightly putting oneself forward, one preserves sincerity within and becomes worthy of reverent regard. The diviner is admonished to be like this. Some say that “sincerity and reverent regard” means those below trust and look up to the person. This hexagram has four yin growing and two yang declining, properly making it the hexagram of the eighth month. Yet its name and attached statement take another meaning. This too expresses support for yang and restraint of yin.[^j03-reverent-subject]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4568:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4568:005 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** I heard Master Hu Yizhi say: “The noble person above serves as the world's pattern and model. Such a person must bring solemnity and reverence to the utmost; then those below look up and are transformed.” Thus, to be an object of viewing for the world, one should be like the beginning of washing at an ancestral-temple sacrifice, not like the time after the offerings have been presented. Then the people below bring forth their utmost sincerity and look up reverently. Washing refers to the beginning of sacrifice, when one washes the hands and pours fragrant sacrificial liquor onto the earth in seeking the spirits. Presenting refers to the offering of raw and cooked meats. Washing is the beginning of the affair, when the heart fully exerts its pure sincerity and solemnity is at its utmost. After the offerings have been presented, the ceremonies are numerous and intricate; the heart becomes dispersed, and its concentrated purity is no longer what it was at the initial washing. Those above who make their conduct upright as a pattern for the people's viewing should remain solemn as at that first washing. They must not let sincerity scatter in the least, as after the presentation of offerings. Then everyone under Heaven will fully exert sincere trust and look up reverently. *Yong* means looking upward with respect.[^j03-guan-readings]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4568:005 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4568:006 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4568:006 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4568:007 -->

**Classified Conversations of Master Zhu:** Showing something from above to those below is *guàn*; looking upward from below is *guān*. Hence the hexagram's name has the departing tone, while guan in all six lines has the level tone.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4568:007 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4568:008 -->

**Someone asked:** “Yichuan holds that sincerity remains at the initial libation, but the concentrated intention slackens after the offerings are presented. The *Original Meaning* speaks of perfecting one's purity and not lightly putting oneself forward. These meanings differ.” The reply: “Washing is simply washing the hands, not pouring the libation. Yichuan inherited the error of earlier scholars. To say that sincerity slackens after the offerings are presented would mean that, in the former kings' sacrifices, there was sincerity only at the initial libation, and that everything after the presentation failed to fulfill the rite.” A question: “Then is the meaning that the sage above must make every act of seeing, hearing, speaking, and moving a model for the world, not daring to be careless—just as in sacrifice one perfects purity and does not dare put it lightly into use?” The reply: “Yes.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4568:008 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4568:009 -->

**He also said:** “No sacrifice lacks the presentation of offerings. This is a hypothetical way of speaking. Presenting is already putting something into action; washing is the beginning, before the action. ‘Without presenting’ means constantly holding to this sincerity and reverence, as though the intention of washing were always present. Presenting would be putting it forth; once that action was finished, it would have passed, and the original intention would no longer remain.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4568:009 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4568:010 -->

**A question:** “‘Sincerity and reverent regard’ follows ‘washing the hands, without presenting the offering.’ Perfecting one's purity and not lightly putting oneself forward leaves sincerity within and makes one worthy of reverent regard. Another explanation says that those below trust and look up to the person. Which is preferable?” The reply: “The latter accords with the Judgment's meaning that those below view and are transformed.” The questioner said: “The first seems good.” The reply: “The Judgment must determine the interpretation.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4568:010 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4568:011 -->

**Feng Yi:** The hexagram repeats Gen's strokes, giving an image of gateways and towers one behind another. Hence the image is taken from a lookout tower.[^j03-lookout-image]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4568:011 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4568:012 -->

**Gong Huan:** The *Changes* names hexagrams with yang as the governing concern. Where yang grows, the account naturally centers on yang; where yin grows, it likewise centers on yang. Centering the account on yang is a way of supporting yang. Thus this hexagram of four yin is not called Small Strength, but Viewing. Why, then, is a four-yang hexagram called Great Excess and a four-yin hexagram called Small Excess? Yin may be spoken of as excessive, but not as strong. Yet Great Excess's four yang are excessive while dwelling centrally; Small Excess's four yin are excessive while dwelling outside. This too expresses honoring yang and restraining yin.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4568:012 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4568:013 -->

**Liang Yin:** “Washing the hands, without presenting the offering” is a constructed expression, simply showing purity at its utmost and an unwillingness to put oneself lightly into action. It resembles the *Doctrine of the Mean*'s “reverent without moving, trustworthy without speaking.” The sage is not literally without speech or movement. Rather, reverence and trustworthiness are especially present before speech and movement. Thus, when the sage governs the world, the people are certainly transformed by the administration of government and teaching. Yet even before these are put into practice, there is a still subtler, wordless power of transformation. Profound reverence at its utmost is like attendance at a great sacrifice: sincere intention abides within, and a reverent bearing appears without. As the people look up, trust, follow, transform, and submit, they do so without knowing how it has come about.[^j03-wordless-teaching]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4568:013 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4568:014 -->

**Cai Qing:** Master Xiang of Ping'an says: “This simply uses washing without presenting the offering to image reverent self-conduct and nonaction.” I suggest that “reverent self-conduct” is intelligible, but “nonaction” is difficult here. Nonaction means the sage's virtue is so abundant that the people transform themselves without awaiting any particular action. It is not the meaning of refraining from lightly putting oneself forward. Could one deliberately set one's mind on nonaction? Even Yao and Shun could not promise themselves nonaction. As for establishing teaching through the numinous Way and securing the world's submission, that is the utmost fulfillment of Viewing and the sage's full accomplishment. That is what is called nonaction.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4568:014 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4568:015 -->

**Lin Xiyuan:** One washes in preparation for presenting the offering. How could there be a principle of not presenting it? The answer: washing without presenting simply makes clear that reverence should remain constantly present. If washing without presenting speaks in terms of sacrifice, then sincerity and reverent regard likewise speak in terms of sacrifice. The meaning of being an object of viewing lies beyond the words themselves.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4568:015 -->

### First Six: A child's viewing. For an ordinary person, no blame; for a noble person, humiliation.[^j03-child-woman]

**Original Meaning.** The hexagram concerns showing oneself for viewing, taking the fifth nine as its ruler. The lines concern looking upward: all view the fifth nine. The first six, yielding yin below, cannot see far: the image of a child's viewing. This is the way of an ordinary person, but a noble person's shame. The first is far away and sees indistinctly, like the viewing of an unformed child. “Ordinary person” means the common people below. Their view is obscure and shallow, unable to recognize the noble person's Way: that is their usual condition, not something to be called a fault. **Wang Bi:** Viewing takes excellence in what is seen as its meaning. Thus nearness to the honored one is valued, and distance from it brings humiliation.

### Six in the Second Place: Viewing through a narrow opening. A woman's steadfastness is beneficial.

Viewing what one produces and advancing or withdrawing as appropriate, one has not yet lost the Way despite the incorrect position. Following the time in advance and withdrawal, seeking not to lose the Way, leaves no regret or blame, because one can be compliant.

## 21. Shihe 噬嗑 — Biting Through

<!-- Source: https://www.eee-learning.com/book/4574 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4574:001 -->

![Shihe: Zhen below, Li above](../assets/eee-learning/a2c697b30351-yi21b.png) **Shihe 噬嗑 — Biting Through** — Zhen below; Li above.[^j03-biting-through]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4574:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4574:002 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Concerning Biting Through, the *Sequence of the Hexagrams* says: “Only after there is something worthy of viewing can there be joining; therefore Viewing is followed by Biting Through. He means joining.” Once there is something worthy of viewing, others come to join it. This is why Biting Through follows Viewing. *Shi* means biting; *he* means joining. Something intervenes within the mouth, and only after it is bitten through can the jaws join. The hexagram has firm lines at the top and bottom with yieldingness between: firm outside and empty within, the image of human jaws and a mouth. Within that inner emptiness is another firm line, imaging something in the jaws. A thing in the mouth separates upper and lower and prevents them from joining. One must bite it through before they can join: hence Biting Through. The sages extend this hexagram's image to the world's affairs. In the mouth, an intervening thing prevents closure; in the world, stubborn resistance or slanderous wickedness intervenes and prevents affairs from coming together. Penal law must be employed to remove it: admonitory punishment for lesser offenses, execution for greater ones. Only then can the world's order be accomplished. From the whole world to one state or household, and throughout the myriad affairs, failures of harmony and union all arise from something intervening. Without an interval, there is union. Even Heaven and Earth's generation and the myriad things' completion require union before they can come to fulfillment. Wherever union is absent, something intervenes. Estrangement, divided allegiance, resentment, and breaches between rulers and ministers, fathers and sons, kin and friends arise because slander and wickedness intervene. Remove them, and harmony returns. Thus obstruction is a great harm to the world. The sages observe Biting Through's image and extend it to every affair, removing what intervenes and bringing things together until all is harmonious and ordered. Biting Through is a great instrument for governing the world. Removing the world's obstructions depends on punishment; thus the hexagram takes the administration of punishment as its meaning. In the two trigrams, illuminating clarity and awe-inspiring thunder give the image of administering punishment.

### Biting Through: success. It is beneficial to adjudicate cases.[^j03-judicial-cases]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4574:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4574:004 -->

**Original Meaning.** Shi is biting, and he is joining. Where something intervenes, bite through it to bring things together. The hexagram has two yang at the top and bottom with emptiness within: the image of jaws and a mouth. One yang, the fourth nine, intervenes inside; it must be bitten through before the jaws can close. Hence Biting Through. Its prognostication promises free passage because obstruction prevents passage, whereas biting through the obstruction and joining restores it. Moreover, there are three yin and three yang, firmness and yieldingness in equal measure; movement below and clarity above, thunder below and lightning above. It originally comes from Increase, the yielding fourth six moving upward to the fifth and attaining the center. Thus we know that although yin in a yang place is not correctly positioned, it is beneficial for adjudicating cases. The Way of adjudication values authority and clarity that attain the mean. Therefore, whoever receives this hexagram in divination fulfills its prognostication if endowed with these virtues.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4574:004 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** “Biting Through: success”: the hexagram inherently contains the meaning of success. Affairs fail to find free passage because something intervenes. Bite through and bring them together, and passage becomes free. “It is beneficial to adjudicate cases”: the Way of biting through and joining appropriately employs legal proceedings and punishment. Without these, how can the world's obstructions be removed? The text does not say that punishment is beneficial, but that adjudicating cases is beneficial, because the hexagram has an image of illuminating clarity, favorable for examining cases. Judicial inquiry investigates truth and falsity. Once the facts are obtained, one knows how the obstruction operates; only then can one establish safeguards and impose punishment.

**Collected Explanations.**

## 22. Bi 賁 — Adornment

<!-- Source: https://www.eee-learning.com/book/4575 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4575:001 -->

![Bi: Li below, Gen above](../assets/eee-learning/6c7514eff6f8-yi22b.png) **Bi 賁 — Adornment** — Li below; Gen above.[^j03-adornment]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4575:001 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4575:002 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Concerning Adornment, the *Sequence of the Hexagrams* says: “He means joining. Things cannot merely join indiscriminately; therefore Biting Through is followed by Adornment. Adornment means embellishment.” When things come together, there must be pattern, and pattern is adornment. When people gather, there are dignified forms of conduct and distinctions of higher and lower; when things assemble, there are sequences, orders, and ranks. Joining necessarily entails pattern. This is why Adornment follows Biting Through. The hexagram is fire beneath a mountain. The mountain gathers grasses, trees, and the hundred kinds of things. Fire below illuminates what is above; all the plants and their various kinds receive its light and color. This gives the image of embellishment, hence Adornment.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4575:002 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4575:003 -->

### Adornment: success. A little benefit in having somewhere to go.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4575:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4575:004 -->

**Original Meaning.** Bi means adornment. When the hexagram comes from Decrease, the yielding comes from the third place to adorn the second, and the firm rises from the second to adorn the third. When it comes from After Completion, the yielding comes from the top to adorn the fifth, and the firm rises from the fifth to adorn the top. Moreover, Li is within and Gen without, giving the image of patterned clarity in which each thing attains its proper share. Hence Adornment. Because the yielding comes to adorn the firm, yang receives yin's assistance, and Li's clarity is within, the diviner obtains success. Because the firm rises to adorn the yielding, with Gen's stillness outside, there is only a little benefit in having somewhere to go.[^j03-bi-transformation]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4575:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4575:005 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Things can find free passage only after they are adorned. Hence the sayings, “Without a root, nothing stands; without pattern, nothing proceeds.” When substance is present and adornment is added, success becomes possible. The Way of patterned adornment can increase brilliance; hence it provides a little benefit in advancing.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4575:005 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4575:006 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4575:006 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4575:007 -->

**Wang Shenzi:** Bare substance cannot find free passage. With pattern added to adorn it, it can: hence “Adornment: success.” Yet when pattern grows excessive, substance must decline. If one advances while valuing pattern alone, one goes adrift; hence only a little benefit in having somewhere to go. “A little” means not carrying it so far as to extinguish substance.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4575:007 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4575:008 -->

**Liang Yin:** Adornment is the Way of giving things patterned embellishment. When substance is present and pattern is added, free passage becomes possible. A court is patterned by ceremonial institutions and thereby functions; host and guest are patterned by ritual manners and thereby associate; a household is patterned by the order of relationships and thereby prospers; government offices are patterned by instruction and ordinances and thereby operate. Extend this to affairs and things: whatever has substance awaits pattern, and through pattern finds free passage. Yet, having said “success,” why say “a little benefit in having somewhere to go”? Patterned adornment merely adds color and form; it cannot change the underlying substance. Excessive ornament is therefore not beneficial: there is only a little benefit in advancing. Those who do not understand the root sometimes forget their urgent responsibilities and fuss over ornament. Though they desire success, how could they obtain it?

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4575:008 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4575:009 -->

**Zhang Zhenyuan:** Li's virtue of patterned clarity cannot be obscured, preventing the fault of simply acting out one's feelings without restraint. Conduct can therefore proceed: hence success. Gen's virtue stops without excess and also images not adorning everything completely. Thus, in employing pattern, one should add only a little embellishment, not strive for exhaustive ornament and thereby injure the original truth. Hence a little benefit in having somewhere to go.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4575:009 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4575:010 -->

### First Nine: Adorn the toes. Leave the carriage and walk.[^j03-carriage-walking]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4575:010 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4575:011 -->

**Original Meaning.** Firm virtue within the trigram of clarity adorns itself below: an image of leaving a carriage obtained contrary to the Way and resting content with walking. The diviner should conduct oneself like this.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4575:011 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4575:012 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The first nine, yang and firm within the trigram of clarity, dwells below: a noble person with firm, discerning virtue in a low position. Holding no office, the noble person has no means of putting anything into effect throughout the world and can only adorn personal conduct. The toes are taken because they are below and are what one walks with. The noble person's Way of cultivation and adornment is to make conduct correct, preserve integrity, and dwell in rightness, never acting carelessly. If accepting a carriage would be inappropriate, one leaves it and prefers to walk. What the multitude considers shameful, the noble person regards as adornment. The meaning of leaving the carriage and walking is also taken from adjacency and correspondence. The first adjoins the second and corresponds with the fourth. Corresponding with the fourth is proper; joining with the second is not. Firm, discerning, and faithful to rightness, the nine does not join the nearby second but corresponds with the distant fourth, abandoning the easy for the difficult, like leaving a carriage to walk. Preserving integrity and rightness is the noble person's adornment. Thus what adorns the noble person is what the world finds shameful; what the world prizes is what the noble person holds cheap. Carriage and walking are mentioned because the meaning is taken from toes and movement.

### Six in the Second Place: Adorn the beard.[^j03-beard]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4575:013 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4575:014 -->

**Original Meaning.** The second, yielding yin, dwells centrally and correctly; the third, firm yang, is also correctly placed. Neither has correspondence, so the second attaches to the third and moves with it, giving the image of adorning the beard. The diviner should follow the yang firmness above in acting.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4575:014 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4575:015 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** Although the hexagram becomes Adornment through the alteration of two lines, the meaning of patterned clarity carries greater weight. The second truly governs Adornment, so its statement chiefly concerns the Way of adornment. Embellishment cannot greatly alter a thing's substance; it merely adds ornament in accordance with that substance. Hence the beard is taken as the image. The beard moves with the jaw: its movement and stillness depend entirely on what it is attached to. Likewise, goodness and badness do not arise from adornment itself. The second's patterned clarity merely provides embellishment; goodness and badness depend on the substance.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4575:015 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4575:016 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4575:016 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4575:017 -->

**Wang Bi:** It occupies its proper place but has no correspondence; the third likewise has none. Both lack correspondence and adjoin one another: near, they find accord. A beard is something attached upward; hence “adorn the beard.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4575:017 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4575:018 -->

**Zhu Zhen:** Hair on the chin is called a beard, above the mouth a mustache, and on the cheeks whiskers. From the third to the top there is a jaw-like figure; the second lies beneath it, giving the image of a beard. The second and third, yielding and firm, adorn one another: adorning the beard. Pattern does not arise from emptiness. The beard grows from the chin and adorns it.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4575:018 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4575:019 -->

**Yu Yan:** The second lacks correspondence and adjoins the third; the third likewise lacks correspondence and adjoins the second. Thus they adorn one another. Adornment succeeds through the yielding coming to ornament the firm. Pattern should follow substance: without substance it cannot adorn itself. Yin must follow yang: without yang it cannot advance by itself. The second six is purely yielding and must await the third nine's movement before moving; hence “adorn the beard.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4575:019 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4575:020 -->

**Jiang Tisheng:** A six occupies the central second place, giving the image of adorning the beard. In the human body, the beard neither adds to nor subtracts from the bodily frame; it simply adorns one's bearing. In turning, bowing, yielding, advancing, withdrawing, lowering or raising oneself, it moves with the face, making one's carriage, cultivated appearance, and deportment worthy of view. Hence the Image says, “It rises with the one above.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4575:020 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4575:021 -->

**He Kai:** The beard is a form taken by yin blood, and yieldingness thus ornaments firmness. Yet yielding yin cannot move by itself: it must attach to yang, just as the beard, though beautiful, must attach to the jaw. Generally, firmness is substance and yieldingness pattern. Without attachment to substance, how can pattern be pattern? Thus the second must adorn the beard to follow the third, and the fifth must find adornment in hills and gardens to follow the top. Here one can see the sages' intention to give precedence to substance over pattern.[^j03-beard-pattern]

### Nine in the Third Place: Adorned and glistening. Lasting steadfastness in what is right brings good fortune.[^j03-glistening]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4575:022 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4575:023 -->

**Original Meaning.** One yang stands between two yin, receiving their adornment and luster. Yet one must not become immersed in what brings comfort; hence the warning to remain steadfast for the long term.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4575:023 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4575:024 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The third occupies the utmost of patterned clarity. Between the two yin at the second and fourth, it joins with them in mutual adornment: adornment in abundance. Hence “adorned.” Ru is a grammatical particle. At adornment's height, brilliance is moist and lustrous; hence “glistening.” Abundant brilliance has luster. The *Odes* says, “Sleek are the does.” “Lasting steadfastness in what is right brings good fortune”: the third does not properly correspond with either the second or fourth. Their mutual adornment comes through adjacency, so it is warned to remain constantly steadfast and correct. Adornment is embellishment, and constancy is difficult in matters of embellishment. Hence lasting steadfastness brings good fortune. The third adorns and is adorned by the fourth, and also adjoins the second below. Two yielding lines ornament one firm line, with mutual adornment above and below: adornment at its height.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4575:024 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4575:025 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4575:025 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4575:026 -->

**Hu Bingwen:** The interlocking Kan trigram has the meaning of moistening and also of sinking into danger. After Completion and Before Completion speak of wetting the head or tail: becoming wet and sinking. The third nine is not without correctness. If it can make that correctness endure, the two yin give it the moistness of luster, while in relation to them it does not suffer the moistness of immersion and entrapment.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4575:026 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4575:027 -->

**Yu Yan:** The third nine stands between the second and fourth sixes; hence adorned and glistening. Excessive pattern destroys substance, and the loss of substance corrupts pattern. One must maintain firm and correct virtue enduringly: then there is good fortune.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4575:027 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4575:028 -->

**Pan Shizao:** The third is originally firm and correct. The particular concern is that the two yin may engulf it, leaving it exposed to the loss of substance. Hence the warning to remain steadfast for the long term.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4575:028 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4575:029 -->

**He Kai:** One firm line between two yielding ones is adornment in abundance. “Glistening” resembles the *Odes*' “six reins as though moistened”: the ornament's pattern and color are fresh and lustrous. Yet receiving adornment from others brings the danger of being engulfed by them. Hence the warning: lasting steadfastness brings good fortune. If one keeps the correctness of yang firmness over time and is not immersed in yin yieldingness, pattern will not extinguish substance.

### Six in the Fourth Place: Adorned—or plain white? A white horse, swift as wings. Not a raider, but a marriage partner.[^j03-white-horse]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4575:030 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4575:031 -->

**Original Meaning.** *Po* means white. A horse is what a person rides; when the person is white, the horse is white as well. The fourth and first adorn one another, but the third nine intervenes and prevents fulfillment: hence plain white. Yet the heart's desire to go and seek its partner is as swift as flying wings. The third nine, however, is firm and correct: it is not a raider, but seeks marriage. Hence this image.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4575:031 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4575:032 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The fourth and first properly correspond and adorn one another. They should be adorned, but the third intervenes. Unable to adorn one another, they remain white. Po is white: not yet obtaining adornment. A horse is below and moves; because mutual adornment has not been attained, the horse is called white. The intention to follow the proper correspondent is like flight; hence “swift as wings.” Were it not for the third nine's hostile obstruction, the marriage partners would fulfill their closeness. What one rides, and what moves below, provide the horse's image. The first and fourth properly correspond and will ultimately attain closeness; they are merely separated at the beginning.[^j03-raider-marriage]

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4575:033 -->

**Classified Conversations of Master Zhu:** The fourth six's white horse, swift as wings, means that this line has nothing adorning it, and its horse too is white. This is how the image expresses the absence of ornament.

**Hu Bingwen:** Difficulty at the Beginning's second responds to the fifth: the lower seeking the upper must not be hasty. Adornment's fourth responds to the first: the upper seeking the lower must not delay.

**Yu Yan:** White hair is called po, and a white horse is called han. The *Record of Ritual* says, “The Shang people valued white; in military affairs they rode white horses.” Zheng's commentary says that han is a horse's white color. The fourth stands at the turning of Adornment's Way, where pattern returns to substance; hence this image.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4575:036 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4575:037 -->

**Liang Yin:** The fourth six is outside Li's clarity and at the beginning of Gen's stillness. Adornment has reached its greatest fullness, and the time has come to return to plain substance. **Su Jun:** This fourth-six line should be understood through the meaning of white adornment. The fourth and first adorn one another. Seeking the first with a substantial, sincere heart, the fourth uses no empty ornament. **Editorial Judgment.** Cheng's *Commentary* follows the explanation of the earlier *Commentary and Subcommentary*, and the *Original Meaning* follows Cheng. Both say that the first and fourth adorn one another but are separated by the third, so that, not yet attaining adornment, they remain white. Yet the *Classified Conversations of Master Zhu* speaks of absence of ornament, showing that Zhu had already revised his explanation. Thus later scholars all take whiteness to mean esteeming plainness and returning to substance. This truly accords with the hexagram's meaning.[^j03-bi-readings]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4575:039 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4575:040 -->

**Further Editorial Judgment.** Wherever the *Changes* repeats ru, it expresses indecision between two possibilities. Thus “difficulty and hesitation” means wishing to advance without advancing directly. This hexagram's third line, “adorned and glistening,” means receiving yin's adornment while also fearing immersion in it. This line's “adorned—or plain white?” means that in a time of Adornment one outwardly values ornament, yet, responding below to the first line's firmness, inwardly esteems plain substance. One cannot yet decide between the two. This is what the *Commentary on the Images* calls doubt. The white horse, swift as wings, refers to the first nine. Because one has a heart inclined toward plain white, one knows that the rider coming on the white horse is no raider, but one's marriage partner. Whenever the text says “not a raider, but a marriage partner,” it refers to the figure mentioned immediately before. Difficulty at the Beginning's second and Opposition's top are exactly the same.[^j03-repeated-ru]

### Six in the Fifth Place: Adornment in hills and gardens. A bundle of silk, meager and small. Humiliation; in the end, good fortune.[^j03-hills-silk]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4575:041 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4575:042 -->

**Original Meaning.** The fifth six is yielding and centered and governs Adornment. Deepening the root and valuing substance, it attains the Way of adornment: hence the image of hills and gardens. Yet yin's nature is parsimonious, giving the image of a meager bundle of silk. A bundle of silk is a modest object; *jianjian* means shallow and small. Though such conduct may appear shamefully mean, in ritual it is better to be frugal than extravagant. Hence good fortune in the end.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4575:042 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4575:043 -->

**Cheng's Commentary.** The fifth six, yielding yin in constitution, stands close to the worthy person of yang firmness at the top nine. Yin adjoins yang and has no other binding correspondence, so it follows the top and receives adornment from it. From antiquity, natural obstacles have been used to defend states, so walls and fortifications commonly follow hills and slopes. “Hills” means something outside, nearby, and high. Gardens and plots lie nearest the walled settlement, likewise outside and nearby. Hills and gardens mean what is outside and nearby, referring to the top nine. Though the fifth six occupies the ruler's position, its yielding ability is insufficient to preserve itself. Adjoining the yang firmness above, it intends to follow it and receives adornment from that worthy neighbor outside: adornment in hills and gardens. If it can receive adornment from the top nine and accept its shaping and regulation, like a bundle of silk cut and fashioned, then, although its weakness and inability to act alone are contemptible and humiliating, it can follow another and accomplish adornment's work, ultimately obtaining good fortune. Jianjian describes cutting, tailoring, and dividing. Before use, silk is bundled; hence a bundle of silk. When made into clothing, it must be cut and divided. The bundle of silk represents the fifth six's original substance; jianjian means accepting another's cutting and shaping in order to become useful. Its reliance on another is like Unformed Understanding. Why does that hexagram not speak of humiliation? Because dependence on another is appropriate for a child still unformed. Relying on another for adornment when no longer a child is humiliating. Yet one enjoys the accomplishment, and in the end there is good fortune.[^j03-tailored-silk]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4575:045 -->

**General Discussion.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4575:060 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4575:061 -->

**Qiu Fuguo:** Among yin and yang, those with correspondence adorn one another through correspondence; those without it adorn one another through adjacency. The fourth corresponds with the first and seeks adornment there: hence the first adorns its toes and the fourth has the winglike horse. The second adjoins the third and adorns it: hence the second adorns the beard and the third glistens. The fifth adjoins the top and receives adornment there: hence the fifth is adorned in hills and gardens, and the top has white adornment. <!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4575:061 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4575:062 -->

**Gong Huan:** Adornment means embellishment through pattern and brilliance. Yet examine the six lines: the first leaves the carriage and walks, the fifth has hills and gardens, and the top has white adornment. All value real substance rather than ornamental brilliance. The fourth, plain white, receives adornment from the first; the second's adornment of the beard attaches to the third. Only the third's adornment and glistening represent embellishment at its height, and it is immediately warned to remain steadfast, from fear of immersion in ornament. Thus what the ancients called adornment was never merely attending to brilliance. It was simply attending to the root and substance. Once root and substance are established, pattern and brilliance are not external to them. Attending only to brilliance while neglecting root and substance is not what the ancients called adornment.

## Translator's Endnotes

These notes distinguish the translator's decisions from the imperial compilers' **Editorial Judgments**. **B** is the repository's unchanged [YiXueWang base transcription][B]; **P** is the [parallel *Siku Quanshu* transcription, fixed revision 528531][P]. Doubtful passages were compared selectively. P is another transcription, not a facsimile, and sometimes shares B's apparent errors. An attested alternative is not automatically a better reading. This translation is not a newly constituted critical edition, and neither independent bilingual review nor systematic leaf-by-leaf facsimile collation has been completed.

The oracle headings offer a readable entry into each passage, not a verdict that cancels the different interpretations following them. In particular, 貞 may express correctness, firmness, or persistence in a course: the commentators themselves distinguish these uses. Historical institutions and social assumptions remain in the translated voices; the notes do not recast them as present-day prescriptions.

[^j03-modesty]: **Modesty, 謙.** This is *qiān*, not *qián* 乾, the first hexagram; both appear as Qian when tones are omitted. The defining phrase 有而不居 means possessing virtue or accomplishment without appropriating its honor. It is not an injunction to pretend that one has no ability. “Modesty” also preserves the title already used in the preceding juans. “Having an end,” 有終, is interpreted both as eventual outward fulfillment and as carrying modest conduct through one's entire life.

[^j03-governing-line]: **The governing third line.** Reversing Modesty's six-line order gives Enthusiasm: its single yang at the third becomes the fourth. The editors use this pair to supplement explanations based only on the two trigrams. A line governing a hexagram's formation need not occupy the fifth, conventionally the ruler's place. Here “the Master” is Confucius in the compilation's traditional attribution of the *Wings*; that attribution is translated as part of the received commentary, not independently established by this edition.

[^j03-sounding-modesty]: **鳴, sounding forth.** Zhu Xi emphasizes becoming known; Cheng Yi emphasizes inner virtue becoming perceptible in voice and countenance; Su Shi emphasizes an answering call between related lines. The English keeps a common expression that can carry all three. These explanations do not make “sounding forth” mere boasting. The comparison with Enthusiasm's first line makes the different moral character of what sounds forth decisive.

[^j03-qian-textual]: **Two readings in Su Shi's gloss.** B prints 嗚 in 寄之於嗚; P has 鳴, matching the line's word and the gloss's call-and-response argument. The translation follows 鳴. Both B and P then print 上九其配也, “the top nine is its counterpart,” although Modesty's top line is a six. The English says “the top line”; the implied reading 上六 is a contextual correction, not one attested in the two witnesses consulted. The source remains unchanged. See [P], Modesty, second six.

[^j03-labor-modesty]: **勞謙 and punctuation.** 勞 includes meritorious labor, service, and accomplishment; “modest in accomplishment” keeps the contrast between having earned honor and not claiming it. The usual division is 勞謙／君子有終／吉. Wu Yueshen proposes 勞謙君子／有終吉, “The noble person modest in accomplishment: successful completion and good fortune,” by analogy with the first line's 謙謙君子. His disagreement is preserved rather than resolved through the heading.

[^j03-duke-zhou]: **The Duke of Zhou.** Cheng invokes the traditional image of the Duke bearing responsibility for the realm while serving the young ruler. The point is not modesty without achievement, but modesty under the greatest responsibility. 夔夔如畏然 describes vigilant reverence, as though in fear. The following reference to the line's *Image* concerns the separate commentarial text, not an additional oracle statement missing from this juan.

[^j03-wielding-modesty]: **撝謙 and the fourth line's syntax.** 撝 can suggest an outward movement of the hand, hence expressing or extending modesty in conduct. Zhu divides 无不利／撝謙: “Nothing is unfavorable; give expression to modesty.” Cheng reads the words together, with every undertaking finding benefit in expressing modesty. The imperial editors prefer Cheng's continuous construction. Neither account recommends theatrical self-deprecation: Liang Yin specifically warns against accepting a title or income unsupported by real service.

[^j03-modesty-force]: **Modesty and force.** 不富以其鄰 means winning or employing neighboring support without relying on wealth, with 以 understood verbally rather than as a simple “because.” The fifth line genuinely speaks of invasion and punitive campaigning, 侵伐. Its commentators connect moral influence and coercive authority within their conception of rule. The English retains that historical claim; it does not replace the military language with purely inward self-improvement, which belongs more particularly to Cheng's reading of the top line.

[^j03-miao]: **Yu, Boyi, and the Miao.** Hu Bingwen alludes to the received *Documents*, “Counsels of the Great Yu” (大禹謨), where Yi tells Yu that fullness invites loss and modesty receives benefit, in the account of the Miao campaign. See the [classical text](https://ctext.org/shang-shu/counsels-of-the-great-yu). Hu's claim that the word was first brought forth there belongs to his reading of the transmitted canon; it is not proof of the word's historical first occurrence.

[^j03-own-domain]: **邑國, one's own domain.** Zhu permits military action only within one's own domain because the top line has no sovereign office. Cheng and several collected commentators make the domain an image of what is privately one's own and read the campaign as self-discipline. These are distinct readings. “No official position” is not the claim that a line has no place in the diagram: it distinguishes the top from the fifth's conventional position of rule.

[^j03-laozi]: **Laozi's military comparison.** The two allusions correspond to chapters 61 and 69 of the *Daodejing*: great and small states gaining through lowering themselves, and the grieving side prevailing when armies confront one another. Compare the [received chapter sequence](https://zh.wikisource.org/zh-hant/道德經古本篇). The compilation applies these to modesty without making all the texts identical in doctrine. The final saying about approaching a task apprehensively is a Confucian allusion, not a third quotation from Laozi. “The one that grieves” retains 哀 rather than substituting superior aggression or confidence.

[^j03-enthusiasm]: **豫, delight and ease.** “Enthusiasm” is the established project title, but the Chinese ranges from shared harmony and joy to personal ease, pleasure, and indulgence. The *Original Meaning* explicitly distinguishes the hexagram's collective joy from most lines' self-directed delight. Thus an unfavorable line need not contradict the hexagram's favorable public activity. Neither “enthusiasm is always good” nor “pleasure is always bad” would preserve this structure.

[^j03-stone-beginnings]: **Firmness and swift discernment.** 介 suggests integrity and a clear moral boundary; 幾 are the barely visible beginnings of change. Being firm as stone therefore does not mean refusing to act. It permits recognizing the first sign and acting without waiting out the day. Cheng quotes the *Appended Statements* at length, including its account of relationships without flattery or overfamiliarity. Zhu's *Great Learning* quotation links settled attention, deliberation, and attainment. The two accounts make steadiness the condition of responsiveness, not its opposite.

[^j03-looking-up]: **盱豫 and the two regrets.** 盱 is looking upward with expectation. Zhu reads the warning as a call to prompt repentance: waiting to change produces renewed regret. Cheng makes both advance toward the fourth and delay in doing so regrettable because the third's own conduct is incorrect. The oracle's compressed wording is not expanded into just one of these explanations. Its focus is the condition of the person looking for delight, not a rule that looking upward itself is blameworthy.

[^j03-hairpin]: **朋盍簪.** The image joins companions gathering with the pin that gathers hair and secures ceremonial headgear. The translators' heading makes that comparison explicit; the individual commentaries show how it is derived. Zhu also records a sense of swiftness for 簪. He Kai's question about the antiquity of hairpins is part of the received discussion and is translated as such; the citation of Yu losing a hairpin is not independently verified archaeological evidence.

[^j03-yu-readings]: **Corrections under the fourth nine.** Hou Xingguo's 為豫為主 in B is 為豫之主 in P, “governing Enthusiasm.” Cai Qing's 勿疑明盍簪 is 勿疑朋盍簪 in P, restoring the oracle's “companions.” In the same gloss, 俊又吉人 is 俊乂吉人 in P, referring to outstanding and good people, not “outstanding and also good people.” The English follows these attested readings. The Eight Primordials and Eight Excellent Ones are the two named groups invoked by Cai; no additional biographical claims are introduced. See [P], Enthusiasm, fourth nine.

[^j03-persistent-illness]: **貞疾恆不死.** The heading leaves the image as an enduring illness without death. Cheng distinguishes the correct ruler's position from the illness of losing effective authority; Wang Zongchuan treats corrective opposition as protection against ruin; He Kai makes the image habitual apprehension rather than literal disease. These are historically situated interpretations of the oracle, not medical diagnoses or assurances of survival. The reference to the end of the Han and Wei is Cheng's example of authority separated from title.

[^j03-changing-delight]: **成有渝.** The condition may already be established, even ingrained, yet change remains possible. The editors connect this line to the fifth: apprehension has value because it can lead to reform. “Without blame” is not a declaration that earlier indulgence never occurred. It marks the possibility of repairing the fault. The text therefore does not treat the last line's extremity as an irreversible fate.

[^j03-yu-top-position]: **Cheng's 不正 at the top.** Both B and P say that yin occupying the top is “not correct,” even though the sixth place is even and therefore formally a yin position in the usual scheme. The passage concerns propriety of conduct and standing as well as diagrammatic position. The English retains the judgment instead of changing the line to yang. Readers should not force every instance of 正 into a single mechanical test of odd and even positions.

[^j03-sui-transformations]: **Different derivations of Following.** Cheng's exchange of Qian's top and Kun's bottom describes a derivation from Obstruction by exchanging positions one and six. Zhu gives three others: Oppression, exchanging one and two; Biting Through, exchanging four and five; Before Completion, making both exchanges. These are interpretive accounts of the completed figure, not instructions that a diviner must cast these changes or treat the named hexagrams as an actual prior reading. The approaches are preserved side by side.

[^j03-mujiang]: **Mu Jiang's refusal of an easy favorable reading.** As quoted here from the *Zuo Tradition*, she judges the favorable words against her own lack of the required virtues rather than treating them as an unconditional prediction. Zhu rejects the four-virtue analysis as the phrase's original meaning while approving the application to divination. This distinction is the point of the quotation: disagreement about original wording does not cancel agreement about the diviner's responsibility. The full narrative is not part of this juan's excerpt.

[^j03-following-direction]: **Who follows whom?** Zhu includes both following others and attracting their following. The compilers argue that lowering oneself and following others is primary, with being followed its result. Their statement that this configuration is unique is part of their paired reading of Following and Gu, combining trigram relations and line arrangement. It is not an instruction to identify every firm line anywhere in the figure as the same ruler, or every yielding line as the same subordinate.

[^j03-office-changes]: **官有渝.** 官 can name office or governance, and the commentators extend it to what governs the heart and the position or principle one maintains. “What governs changes” keeps these possibilities available. The phrase need not foretell a change of employment. Going beyond the gate is likewise interpreted as going beyond private attachments and hearing others fairly, not as a requirement merely to change physical location.

[^j03-sui-relations]: **Adjacency and correspondence.** Formal correspondences pair one with four, two with five, and three with six; adjacency concerns neighboring positions. Yu Yan emphasizes adjacency throughout Following, while Zhu and Cheng repeatedly use correspondence as well. Their explanations should not be silently combined into one uniform rule. The same first yang can govern the hexagram's formation in one account and appear as a distracting “boy” from the second line's standpoint in another.

[^j03-boy-man]: **Boy and grown man.** 小子 and 丈夫 are retained as concrete figures, but their referents depend on the line being explained. At the second six, the boy is the nearby first nine and the grown man the proper correspondent at the fifth. At the third six, the grown man is the nearby fourth nine, while the boy remains the first. The contrast is not a freestanding rule about the age or sex of someone the reader should follow.

[^j03-gaining-followers]: **Gain and dangerous persistence.** In the third line, 得 concerns obtaining what one seeks through following. In the fourth, 獲 concerns gaining another's following. Cheng sees the danger of a minister attracting allegiance that should belong to the sovereign; Guo and Gong warn against persisting in such a situation. Zhu allows that even a correct position can become dangerous. Accordingly, 貞凶 is not forced into the claim that ethical rightness itself is wrong: correctness and obstinate persistence are distinct possibilities within this commentary.

[^j03-sui-readings]: **Three corrected characters.** In Cheng's fourth-nine commentary, B's 復伺過咎之有 is P's 復何過咎之有, the rhetorical question “What further fault or blame could there be?” At the fifth nine, B's 陽剛中止 is P's 陽剛中正, “yang firmness, centered and correct.” In Xiang Anshi's top-line gloss, B's 於用亨於岐山 is P's 王用亨於岐山, restoring “the king” in the citation of Pushing Upward. The translation follows P in these places; B is not altered. See [P], Following, fourth, fifth, and top lines.

[^j03-binding-offering]: **Binding and sacrifice.** The terse 拘係之乃從維之 does not name every agent or object. The heading keeps the concrete act of binding without pretending that its participants are unambiguous. Zhu reads 亨 as 享, sacrificial offering; Cheng reads flourishing or success and applies it to the royal enterprise. The editors instead understand the king to employ a worthy person as celebrant. These are substantive differences, not interchangeable English paraphrases. The heading's “uses this” leaves their explanations to identify what is used.

[^j03-great-king]: **The Great King and the western mountain.** Cheng's 太王 is the Great King associated in the received Zhou narrative with the move from Bin to Qi. He is not silently replaced by King Wen. Mount Qi is the Western Mountain in this interpretation. Cheng's story explains loyal following and the flourishing of the royal enterprise; Zhu's interpretation concerns mountain sacrifice. Both accounts are translated as the commentators present them, not as a reconstructed historical narrative.

[^j03-tethered-guest]: **The guest's horse.** Lü Zuqian cites the *Odes*, “A Guest” (有客) and “The White Colt” (白駒), where tethering the guest's horse expresses the wish to prolong the guest's stay. Within his interpretation, binding thus figures affectionate unwillingness to part. This does not erase the harder possibility of coercion in the bare oracle; it explains why the received commentary need not understand every bond as captivity.

[^j03-king-worthy]: **Who conducts the royal offering?** The compilers read 王用 as the king employing the worthy person represented by the line, not the line itself becoming king. Their argument makes the top a person whose service Heaven accepts and who remains attached when service is fitting. Gu's top line, by contrast, does not serve. The conclusion explicitly rejects explaining the first as mere dependence on salary and the second as mere flight from society: appropriate service and appropriate withdrawal form the contrast.

[^j03-decay]: **蠱 and affairs requiring repair.** The character's insects-in-a-vessel image supplies decay and corruption. Cheng expressly rejects treating 事, “affairs,” as a literal dictionary definition of 蠱: decay creates work to be done, and remedying it is itself an undertaking. “Decay” retains the condition from which that work arises. The family imagery extends the scope to inherited failure and responsibility without making the whole hexagram merely a prediction of family conflict.

[^j03-jia-days]: **Three days before and after jia.** The ten-day sequence is jia, yi, bing, ding, wu, ji, geng, xin, ren, gui, then jia again. Three steps before jia is xin; three after is ding. Zhu links xin 辛 with renewal 新 and ding 丁 with earnest attentiveness 丁寧, making prevention and continued care the practical meaning. Cheng instead emphasizes investigating causes and anticipating consequences. Neither reading warrants reducing the whole instruction to a modern three-day waiting period.

[^j03-gu-transformations]: **Gu's alternative derivations.** From Adornment, exchange positions one and two; from The Well, exchange five and six; from After Completion, make both exchanges. In each, firm lines move upward and yielding lines downward. This illustrates the separation invoked in the interpretation. It is distinct from the sequence relation in which Gu follows Following, and from a divinatory change produced in an individual consultation.

[^j03-gu-readings]: **Two transcription corrections.** Kong Yingda's 大得串通 in B is 大得亨通 in P, “obtaining great free passage.” Hu Bingwen's 蠱猶末深 is 蠱猶未深 in P, “decay is not yet deep.” The English follows these attested corrections. Their graphic similarity explains the practical need for comparison, but the notes do not claim to identify the precise stage at which the errors entered the transcription. See [P], Gu, general commentary and first six.

[^j03-master-ji]: **The unidentified Master Ji, 集氏.** This attribution looks unusual but is not corrected to another surname. Both B and P read 集氏. More importantly, the book's own [list of cited commentators](../source/front-matter.md#eee-4534) groups this name with Xue Wenqi and Master Lu, stating that their generation or chronology is uncertain and that they occur in Fang Shenquan's *Yihai* (義海). The identity remains unresolved; “Master Ji” translates the recorded attribution without inventing a given name.

[^j03-fathers-decay]: **The father's undertaking.** 幹 is linked with the trunk that supports branches, hence taking responsibility and setting matters upright. 蠱 includes work already spoiled by predecessors; the parent's affairs therefore supply a model for inherited duties more generally. 考 can name a deceased father, but the local commentaries do not require the reader to assume a death in every application. “Having such a son” means having someone equal to the task, not merely the biological fact of parenthood.

[^j03-mothers-decay]: **不可貞.** Here the warning is against rigid insistence, not against being ethical. The commentators require correction that preserves affection and enters the situation through deference. Their account is framed by inherited assumptions about maternal yieldingness, filial duty, and ruler–minister relations; those assumptions remain in their own voices. The interpretation should not be recast as a universal claim about women's abilities, nor flattened into the notion that one must never disagree with a parent.

[^j03-regret-humiliation]: **悔 and 吝.** Zhu explicitly contrasts regret that turns a person away from danger with complacency that moves toward humiliation. The third's overfirmness at least repairs the decay; the fourth's leniency may look harmless while allowing it to deepen. “Regret” and “humiliation” therefore remain distinct rather than becoming interchangeable unfavorable predictions. Cheng's fourth-line account also differs from Zhu's: he allows maintenance of ordinary affairs but doubts the capacity for work beyond the ordinary.

[^j03-repair-praise]: **Whose reputation is restored?** Zhu and Cheng chiefly explain the good reputation of a successor who successfully repairs inherited affairs. Zheng Weiyue shifts attention to the parent: conspicuous praise for the son's repairs might expose the parent's failure, so the finest repair also preserves the parent's good name. The short 用譽 can thus support praise as a result and preservation of praise as a method. The translation retains the difference.

[^j03-withdrawal-service]: **An undertaking outside office.** 不事王侯 is refusal or absence of royal service, not the absence of all meaningful work. Zhang Zhenyuan makes precisely this distinction. Cheng lists several kinds of withdrawal without treating them as equal in breadth or value; Hu Bingwen distinguishes the chosen bond of ruler and minister from filial responsibilities that cannot simply be disclaimed. The conclusion concerns appropriate service and withdrawal, not withdrawal as an invariably superior spiritual status.

[^j03-self-honoring-variant]: **A meaningful variant retained.** In Cheng's top-line commentary, B reads 則自尊其身, “then one honors one's own person.” P reads 則自善其身, “then one makes one's own person good.” Both fit the surrounding discussion of integrity outside office. The English follows B's 自尊 rather than silently replacing it with the parallel's 自善. This is recorded as a variant, not classified as a proven typographical error. See [P], Gu, top nine.

[^j03-approach]: **臨 has more than one direction.** Cheng emphasizes overseeing or approaching those below from above; Zhu emphasizes the growing yang advancing upward toward yin. Both draw on coming close and bringing one's presence to bear. “Approach” preserves the title without forcing every line to describe geographical movement. Overseeing people and attending to affairs remain explicit where the commentators introduce them.

[^j03-eighth-month]: **Three reckonings, not one modern deadline.** Wang Yinglin records three inclusive counts: from chou, the twelfth month, to shen, the seventh, reaching Obstruction; from zi, the eleventh month, to wei, the sixth, reaching Retreat; and from yin, the first month, to you, the eighth, reaching Viewing. Zhu accepts the latter two explanations. In the first two, “eighth” counts eight months inclusively from a chosen starting point; in the third, it is the eighth month of the Xia calendar. None means Gregorian August by default.

[^j03-seven-eight]: **The numbers and their symbolic associations.** Cheng Jiong relates nine to mature yang, eight to young yin, six to mature yin, and seven to young yang, associating yin with months and yang with days. This is his account of the wording's numerical symbolism, not an instruction to change a cast nine into an eight. The following commentator's several calendar schemes are separate explanations and should not be collapsed into this one.

[^j03-hexagram-qi]: **六日七分, six days and seven parts.** In the calendrical scheme cited here, the parts are eightieths of a day: sixty hexagrams each govern six days and seven-eightieths, totaling 365¼ days. A primary discussion preserving the formula is [Hui Dong's *Yi Hanxue*, juan one](https://ctext.org/wiki.pl?chapter=607689&if=en). Wang contrasts this account of Return's seven days with a count through Kun's six positions and a month sequence from Coming to Meet to Return. The translation leaves all three accounts visible rather than making one the unqualified practical rule.

[^j03-xian-approach]: **咸: all or influence?** Zhu's 遍臨四陰 takes 咸 as comprehensive approach: the two yang approach all four yin. Cheng explicitly glosses it 感, influencing and eliciting response. The heading's “all-embracing” follows Zhu's explanation, while Cheng's commentary uses “influence.” This is not the importation of an unrelated keyword from hexagram 31; Li Shunchen himself compares the two figures through the reciprocal influence of yin and yang.

[^j03-initial-position]: **Different uses of position.** Cheng distinguishes a line's formal position, its function as beginning or end, and the rank or responsibility it represents. His claim that Approach especially emphasizes the first line's correct placement is part of that distinction. The paired lines one–four and two–five should not be mistaken for moving-line instructions. Similarly, 貞吉 can describe existing correctness or require correctness as a condition of good fortune; Cheng explicitly records several possibilities.

[^j03-sweet-approach]: **甘 is not merely pleasantness.** Cheng reads sweetness as ingratiating conduct; the editors emphasize pleasure taken in power and status. Both contrast it with influence grounded in virtue. Their warning is not that kindness is suspect. Anxiety matters because it can interrupt self-indulgence and bring reform. The comparison with Limitation's third line is an interpretive parallel based on Dui, not an assertion that the hexagram must transform into Limitation.

[^j03-wise-approach]: **知, wisdom through delegation.** Here 知 is read in the sense of 智, wisdom or discernment. The commentators make breadth of understanding depend on employing others' abilities, not pretending to personally comprehend and decide everything. Wang Shenzi's citation is from the *Doctrine of the Mean*. “Great ruler” retains the institutional image rather than converting it silently into a modern manager or an individual's higher self.

[^j03-generous-approach]: **敦, depth and generosity.** 敦 carries thickness, solidity, and accumulated sincerity, hence generous or deeply grounded approach. Cheng does not make the top line excessive simply because it is last: here culmination is a fullness of compliance and respect for worthiness. Zhu compares 敦 in Gen and Return to show that the same term can appear at different positions according to the local meaning. The analogy does not add a missing seventh line to the hexagram.

[^j03-viewing]: **觀: viewing and being viewed.** The traditional distinction is guān, looking, and guàn, a spectacle or example presented for viewing; the latter also names an elevated lookout. Zhu marks this through the level and departing tones. Modern English cannot reproduce the tonal distinction, so the prose identifies the direction of attention wherever consequential. The hexagram may concern exemplary conduct above while its individual lines concern the observers' differing capacities to see.

[^j03-washing-offering]: **盥 is handwashing; 灌 is libation.** Cheng's account joins washing the hands with pouring fragrant liquor and contrasts initial concentration with later ceremonial dispersal. Zhu explicitly objects: 盥 is washing, not 灌, and proper sacrifice cannot be reduced to its opening moment. For him, “without presenting” is a hypothetical image of continuously preserved reverence, not advice to omit the offering. The disagreement is present in both witnesses and is not harmonized away.

[^j03-reverent-subject]: **Who has sincerity and reverence?** Zhu first reads 有孚顒若 as the exemplary person's inward sincerity and outward dignified bearing. He also records the alternative that those below trust and gaze reverently. In the quoted conversation he prefers the latter because it matches the Judgment's “those below view and are transformed.” The heading leaves the subject unstated so that both readings can be heard. These are interpretive alternatives within the compilation, not a difference between the Chinese texts collated.

[^j03-guan-readings]: **Opening pronoun and punctuation.** B's 子聞之胡翼之先生 is P's 予聞之胡翼之先生: Cheng says “I heard it from Master Hu Yizhi.” The English follows 予. B also places a stop after 勿使誠意少散, before 如既薦之後. Read continuously, the passage warns against letting sincerity scatter *as after the presentation*, before describing the people's sincere regard. The translation follows that syntax, supported by P's continuous text and the preceding argument; it does not make scattered sincerity the cause of good transformation. See [P], Viewing, general commentary.

[^j03-lookout-image]: **Repeated Gen strokes.** Feng's 疊艮之畫 need not mean that the two actual trigrams are both Gen: they are Kun below and Xun above. It can be understood graphically by doubling each stroke of Gen—two broken strokes, two broken strokes, then two unbroken strokes—to obtain Guan's six-line arrangement. This explains the visual comparison without replacing the hexagram's constituent trigrams. Feng associates that pattern with repeated gateways or towers.

[^j03-wordless-teaching]: **Reverence before expression.** The comparison with being reverent without moving and trustworthy without speaking invokes the *Doctrine of the Mean*. Liang Yin does not deny that a sage speaks and acts; he locates the root of effective action before outward expression. Cai Qing then distinguishes this disciplined reserve from 無為, nonaction understood as transformation through abundant virtue. Their distinction is preserved rather than translated indiscriminately as passivity, mindfulness, or doing nothing.

[^j03-child-woman]: **Historical limits of the observer imagery.** In the first line Cheng expressly glosses 小人 as 下民, the common people; “ordinary person” therefore replaces the otherwise usual “petty person” in this passage. The second line's distinction between a woman's proper conduct and a grown man's obligations reflects the commentators' social assumptions. It remains visible in the translation but is not presented as a universal judgment about present-day capacities. Zhu's emphasis is on a narrow view that does not see the whole.

[^j03-viewing-life]: **我生 is conduct and what it produces.** Zhu glosses it 我之所行, what I do; Cheng expands it into actions and undertakings issuing from oneself. “What I bring forth” retains 生's productive sense without suggesting that the passage concerns a birth chart or merely watching one's feelings. Zhu's examples ask whether counsel is heard and whether government and beneficence take effect. The decision to advance or withdraw depends on examining such evidence.

[^j03-royal-guest]: **賓于王.** The literal guest relationship supplies the image of being honorably received at court. Cheng explicitly links it with worthy people's entry into service. “The state's radiance” likewise means more than a splendid palace or personal charisma: it is the ruler's virtue made visible through government and the people's condition. The translation preserves both the guest image and its institutional explanation.

[^j03-ruler-reflection]: **Seeing oneself through the people.** Zhu emphasizes whether one's own conduct is that of a noble person; Cheng and Kong emphasize whether the people's customs have become those of noble people. The perspectives overlap but are not identical. The latter is the commentary's account of a ruler's moral responsibility for public life, not a modern empirical claim that every social outcome has only one cause. Its practical contrast is between self-approval and examining the effects of one's conduct.

[^j03-top-viewing]: **“I” and “that person.”** The shift from 我 to 其 permits different readings. Zhu and Cheng describe an exemplary person above official responsibility who must still examine the conduct others observe. The compilers prefer taking the last line as completing the ruler's self-examination in the fifth, without making the top itself the ruler. “That person” avoids fixing a gender or falsely settling the disputed referent. The concluding 志未平 is an intention not yet at rest, not a diagnosis of mental disturbance.

[^j03-biting-through]: **Biting so the jaws can close.** 噬 is the bite; 嗑 is joining. “Biting Through” therefore includes removing what prevents union, not violence for its own sake. The middle obstruction supplies the hexagram's overall image, but individual middle lines represent those who remove obstructions. Cheng's analogy genuinely extends to punishments including execution. The translation neither conceals that historical penal framework nor turns it into a present-day recommendation to punish personal adversaries.

[^j03-judicial-cases]: **獄 is adjudication, not merely prison.** Cheng explicitly distinguishes examining cases from administering punishment, 刑: the truth and falsity of the matter must be investigated before safeguards and penalties are set. Accordingly, “it is beneficial to adjudicate cases” is more exact here than “it is beneficial to use prisons.” The text's pairing of authority with clarity likewise concerns responsibility in judgment, not force without inquiry.

[^j03-stocks-toes]: **滅: hidden or injured?** Yu Yan and Jiang Bao explain the toes as concealed by the wooden stocks, expressly rejecting destruction of the toes. Cheng's wording includes injury, and Zhu speaks of a small hurt. The heading follows the covering image, while the individual comments retain their own language. This is an interpretive disagreement, not an error to be erased from the witnesses. 足 and 趾 are kept in their immediate foot-and-toe imagery rather than replaced by an abstract obstruction.

[^j03-positions-punishment]: **No office is not no line position.** The first and top are interpreted as recipients of punishment, while the four middle lines administer it. Cheng rejects Wang Bi's explanation in terms of lacking a yin or yang position, distinguishing office or rank from odd and even places. This repeats an issue already encountered in Approach. It prevents the administrative hierarchy of this hexagram from being mistaken for a universal scheme in which first and sixth positions have no polarity.

[^j03-biting-nose]: **Whose nose, and what happens to it?** The bare image is retained without adding a named offender or judge. Zhu speaks of injury incurred in overcoming resistance; Cheng reads the nose as disappearing into a deep bite. His phrase 人之肌膚 explicitly uses human skin and flesh as the comparison and has not been silently turned into an animal meal. The later comments debate the depth of punishment and the significance of concealment. None of these distinctions is resolved by simply translating 滅 as amputation everywhere.

[^j03-shihe-readings]: **易人 corrected to 易入.** In Hu Bingwen's second-six gloss, B has 如噬膚之易人. P reads 如噬膚之易入, “as easy to penetrate as biting tender flesh,” which agrees with the preceding explanation. The English follows P. This is a character correction, unlike the substantive dispute over whether 滅 implies covering or injury. See [P], Biting Through, second six.

[^j03-dried-meat]: **Different textures, different resistance.** 膚 is tender flesh or skin-on meat; 腊肉 here is the animal dried whole with bones; 乾胏 is dried flesh attached to bone; 乾肉 is dried flesh. The distinctions matter to the commentators' comparison of difficult cases. “Poison” retains 毒's force even when Cheng interprets it as the resentment and injury provoked by an improperly situated judge. It is not silently reduced to an unpleasant flavor alone.

[^j03-metal-arrows]: **Judicial deposits and symbolic firmness.** Zhu cites the *Rites of Zhou*: a bundle of arrows is deposited for 訟, and a jun-weight of metal for 獄, before the case is heard. See the [primary passage, *Offices of Autumn*](https://ctext.org/text.pl?if=gb&node=37230&show=parallel). Cheng instead emphasizes metal's firmness and the arrow's straightness. 金 is rendered “metal,” not assumed to be chemically pure gold; 鈞 remains a historical weight without a speculative modern conversion. The deposit interpretation is not the same as finding an arrowhead embedded in hunted meat.

[^j03-obstacle-remover]: **The fourth line's two functions.** In the whole mouth-like hexagram, the fourth yang is the obstruction. In the fourth line's own statement, it is the firm official who removes obstructions. Yang Shi and Wang Zongchuan explicitly defend this change of standpoint. The apparent reversal should therefore not be “corrected” into a single unvarying character in a six-person story. The compilation makes this distinction elsewhere as well.

[^j03-li-drying]: **乾 here is gān, dry.** Hu's 離為乾卦 does not identify Li with the Qian trigram. It says Li has a drying character, furnishing the image of dried meat. His subsequent contrast is between bone within flesh and flesh joined to bone, aligned respectively with yieldingness in a firm position and firmness in a yielding one. The physical metaphor and positional interpretation are both retained.

[^j03-yellow-metal]: **黃金 and the center.** Yellow is explained as the central color; metal supplies firmness and, in Zhu's account, the judicial deposit already mentioned. “Yellow metal” keeps these explanations available rather than implying a promised financial windfall. Cheng's statement that the fifth occupies a firm position refers to the odd-numbered place: the line itself remains yielding yin. The distinction is essential to his warning about firmness and apprehension.

[^j03-duty-variant]: **分 or 公 in Li Guo.** B reads 有司執法之分, the responsible official's assigned duty to enforce law. P reads 有司執法之公, the official's impartiality in enforcing law. Both make sense opposite the ruler's compassion. The English retains B's 分, “duty,” rather than treating the alternative as a proven correction. See [P], Biting Through, fifth six.

[^j03-judicial-caution]: **Before and after judgment.** Gu Jiajie distinguishes careful inquiry before a case is settled from reverent compassion afterward. 出入 literally concerns bringing within or letting out of penal liability; “conviction or acquittal” supplies the relevant sense without claiming equivalence to every modern procedural category. The order 艱貞 in the fourth and 貞厲 in the fifth is important to his explanation. It is not merely a stylistic variation in the English.

[^j03-collar-ears]: **何校 and mutilation.** 何 is read as 荷, to bear: the wooden restraint rests at the neck. Guo Yong argues that the toes, nose, and ears are concealed rather than severed, using the relative penalties described in earlier texts as his reasoning. The historical comparison with Han punishment is his assertion, retained in translation; this edition does not independently establish that penal history. His conclusion concerns what 滅 signifies in these images, not a denial that restraints and bodily penalties existed in the commentary's world.

[^j03-adornment]: **文 and 質.** “Pattern,” “form,” “cultivation,” and “ornament” translate 文 according to context; 質 is the underlying substance or material. The opposition is not simply good reality versus bad appearances. The opening commentators insist that substance requires appropriate form to function, while later lines warn against form overwhelming substance. 賁 is pronounced bì here, distinct from 比 bǐ, Holding Together. The Chinese title is therefore retained alongside the English.

[^j03-bi-transformation]: **Adornment's two derivations.** From Decrease, the second and third lines exchange places; from After Completion, the fifth and top exchange. These give the yielding coming to ornament firmness and firmness rising to ornament yieldingness. They are alternative accounts of the figure, not two simultaneous changes to be made in a consultation. Li's patterned clarity and Gen's ability to stop supply another, complementary account of the limited benefit in advancing.

[^j03-carriage-walking]: **Adorning one's conduct.** The toes image how one walks or acts. The abandoned carriage represents an easier or more prestigious course that would compromise rightness. Cheng expressly contrasts conventional status with the noble person's genuine adornment. His use of first–fourth correspondence against first–second adjacency belongs to this line; the following lines will legitimately use adjacency to explain mutual adornment. The text is not applying a universal rule that adjacent relationships are improper.

[^j03-beard]: **須, beard.** The beard is attached to the jaw and moves with it, furnishing the central relation between adornment and its supporting substance. Zhu Zhen differentiates chin hair, mustache, and cheek whiskers. His jaw-like figure from the third to the top is a graphic likeness, not a claim that these four strokes are the six-line Nourishment hexagram. The second line below the figure supplies the beard's position.

[^j03-beard-pattern]: **The dependence of ornament.** He Kai's account of the beard as a form of yin blood belongs to the commentary's traditional bodily cosmology, not a modern physiological explanation. His central analogy is that pattern requires substance, as the beard requires the jaw. 右質左文 gives priority to substance by placing it on the valued side of this particular contrast; it does not establish a universal rule about the superiority of right over left throughout Chinese ritual.

[^j03-glistening]: **濡: luster or immersion.** Moisture can give fresh brilliance or engulf what it touches. Hu Bingwen explicitly uses the interlocking Kan figure to connect these possibilities; here the relevant three-line group is positions two through four. The heading “glistening” follows the favorable surface image, while the commentary retains the risk of being submerged in ornament. The warning is to preserve firm correctness over time, not merely enjoy a favorable appearance.

[^j03-white-horse]: **皤 and 翰.** Po is white or unadorned. Zhu and Cheng read han through winglike swiftness; Yu Yan reads it as a white horse, supported by his ritual citation and Zheng's gloss. The heading follows the flying image but does not cancel the alternative. Whiteness itself is also disputed: for one account it is adornment not yet achieved; for later commentators it is a deliberate return to plain substance. These are different valuations of the same image.

[^j03-raider-marriage]: **Three accounts of the encounter.** Zhu identifies the third nine as an apparent obstruction that is actually seeking marriage. Cheng makes the third a hostile obstacle between the proper first–fourth pair. The editors take the white horse as the first nine, whose arrival is recognized as that of the proper partner rather than a raider. Their explanation continues the discussion already encountered in Difficulty at the Beginning, juan one. The relationships must not be silently blended into one narrative.

[^j03-bi-readings]: **Corrections, not suppressed revisions.** In the first Editorial Judgment on the fourth six, B's 故末得其賁 is P's 故未得其賁, “not yet attaining adornment.” In Zhu's conversation under the top nine, B's 復於無飾矢 ends 矣 in P, the sentence-final particle, not “an arrow.” The English follows P in both places. Separately, the editors' claim that Zhu revised his interpretation of whiteness is part of their own comparison of his writings; the earlier explanation remains translated in full. See [P], Adornment, fourth and top lines.

[^j03-repeated-ru]: **The editors' paired possibilities.** Cheng often treats 如 as a grammatical particle that describes a condition. The editors instead argue that repeated 如 can hold two possibilities in suspense. The fourth-line heading “adorned—or plain white?” makes that tension readable. The third-line heading remains “adorned and glistening,” with the adverse possibility of immersion explained in the comments. Neither English conjunction can reproduce every commentator's analysis simultaneously.

[^j03-hills-silk]: **Hills, gardens, and modest means.** Zhu reads 丘園 as turning toward roots and agricultural simplicity, and 戔戔 as meager or small. Cheng identifies the hills and gardens with the worthy neighbor represented by the top line and reads the silk as material to be cut and shaped. The heading follows Zhu's smallness reading, while the full alternative follows immediately. The image does not unconditionally prescribe rural retirement or promise gain from stinginess.

[^j03-tailored-silk]: **Cheng's 剪裁 reading.** For Cheng, jianjian is cutting and dividing silk to make it useful. The fifth's original substance accepts another's shaping, just as a ruler accepts a worthy person's instruction. The humility of depending on another is potentially embarrassing but can produce accomplishment. Zhu's account differs, taking 戔戔 as smallness and frugality and explicitly rejecting the sense of abundant silk. These lexical alternatives materially change the image and are therefore preserved.

[^j03-jian-character]: **戔 and related written forms.** Zhu appeals to characters containing 戔: 淺, shallow; 賤, cheap or low in value; 錢, coin or money. The translation reproduces his argument from shared graphic form. It is not presented as an independently demonstrated modern etymology of every word. His examples of Duke Wen of Wei and Emperor Wen of Han function as traditional models of frugality within this interpretation, not as a separate historical assessment of their governments.

[^j03-silk-bundle]: **束帛 as a ceremonial gift.** He Kai cites a marriage-rite bundle of five pairs, glossed as ten lengths. The count is retained without imposing a modern textile length. He's point is the modest material gift accompanying a genuine accord of intentions. His interpretation also locates the worthy recipient in hills and gardens, making the passage more than a comment on personal wardrobe or decorative expense.

[^j03-white-adornment]: **白賁 is not simple rejection of culture.** Zhu emphasizes returning to the root and the absence of added ornament. Cheng explicitly says that valuing plain substance does not mean having no adornment: brilliance must not overwhelm what is real. Their difference is retained. The closing general discussions likewise insist that appropriate form can arise from established substance, while empty display cannot substitute for it.

[^j03-white-ground]: **Plainness as the ground of beauty.** 素以為絢 evokes plainness in relation to patterned beauty. “Sweetness receives blended flavors; white receives colors” echoes the *Record of Ritual*, 禮器; compare the [classical commentary preserving that passage](https://www.shidianguji.com/book/CADAL02043479/chapter/1l7q15tbzgc27). In this analogy, an unforced underlying quality allows cultivated forms to be received. Jiang Tisheng's preference for such a ground over an ostentatious display should not be confused with the claim that colors or ceremony are inherently worthless.
