## Chapter 7 · Elevating Virtue and Broadening Work

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:003 -->

### The Master said: “How consummate the Changes is! Through the Changes, the sage elevates virtue and broadens his work. Understanding is exalted; ritual practice is lowly. In its exaltation, it models Heaven; in its lowliness, it follows Earth.”[^j14-knowledge-practice]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:003 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:004 -->

**Original Meaning.** The Ten Wings were all composed by the Master; he should not have written “the Master said” himself. I suspect these words were all added by later people. Exhaustively investigating principle makes understanding exalted like Heaven, and so elevates virtue. Following principle makes ritual practice lowly like Earth, and so broadens one's work. Here again the correspondences are drawn in terms of clarity and turbidity.[^j14-master-said]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:004 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:005 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:005 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:006 -->

**Han Kangbo:** Bringing understanding's exaltation to its utmost models Heaven, which is high and embraces all things. Making the practice of ritual complete models Earth, which is broad and bears all things.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:006 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:007 -->

**Kong Yingda:** The Way of the Changes is consummate. The sage uses it to elevate his virtue and broaden his work.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:007 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:008 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu:** Understanding should be lofty and clear; practice should be firmly grounded. Once understanding is lofty and clear, one must lower oneself and carry it out in earnest.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:008 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:009 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu, further:** Understanding's exaltation is how virtue is elevated; ritual practice's lowliness is how one's work is broadened. If ritual practice falls short even slightly, something remains wanting, and one's work is not broad. Only by being utterly lowly, leaving nothing wanting, can it be broad.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:009 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:010 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu, further:** The lowliness of ritual practice means humble compliance. Lowliness itself makes for breadth: Earth is broad because it is low. Height narrows. If people choose only lofty things to do, they become narrow. One must work with both feet planted on the ground.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:010 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:011 -->

**Wu Cheng:** Elevating virtue means establishing the heart through ease, so that what one attains advances and renews itself daily. Broadening one's work means conducting affairs through simplicity, so that what one accomplishes grows daily fuller and richer. Virtue advances and renews itself through the exaltation of understanding, lofty and clear like Heaven. Work becomes full and abundant through the lowliness of practice, level and substantial like Earth.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:011 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:012 -->

**Zhang Zhenyuan:** Understanding is virtue's open clarity, shining within. Ritual practice is the measure and standard of work accomplished outwardly. Heaven moves above the myriad things, and the understanding of the sage's heart likewise transcends the countless images; hence “in its exaltation, it models Heaven.” Earth encompasses the smallest things without leaving one out, and the sage's ritual practice likewise neglects nothing, however fine or slight; hence “in its lowliness, it follows Earth.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:012 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:013 -->

### Heaven and Earth establish their positions, and change moves between them. Preserve the nature already complete, preserving it without cease: the gateway of the Way and rightness.[^j14-preserving-nature]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:013 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:014 -->

**Original Meaning.** Heaven and Earth establishing their positions and change and transformation proceeding between them is like understanding and ritual practice preserving nature, from which the Way and rightness issue. “The nature already complete” is nature as originally complete. “Preserving it without cease” means preserving it, then preserving it again: never stopping.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:014 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:015 -->

**Original Meaning — Chapter Summary.** This is Chapter Seven.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:015 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:016 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:016 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:017 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu:** Insight is lofty above, practice substantial below; between them there is generation without end. Hence “change moves between them,” and “preserve the nature already complete, preserving it without cease: the gateway of the Way and rightness.”

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:017 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:018 -->

**Yu Yan:** Human nature is a complete whole formed by Heaven, with nothing in it that is not good. Add the work of nurturing and preserving it, preserving it again and again, and wherever one goes there is the Way, wherever one goes there is rightness.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:018 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:019 -->

**Lin Xiyuan:** This continues “understanding is exalted; ritual practice is lowly; in its exaltation, it models Heaven; in its lowliness, it follows Earth.” When Heaven and Earth establish their positions, yin and yang change and transform, and change moves between them. When the sage's understanding and ritual practice reach the point of modeling Heaven and following Earth, his originally complete nature is preserved without cease, and the Way and rightness issue from it. Hence “the gateway of the Way and rightness.” What the heart attains of the Way and rightness renews itself daily and flourishes month by month: thus virtue is elevated. What appears in affairs accumulates day by day and month by month: thus work is broadened. This is how the Changes enables the sage to elevate virtue and broaden his work, and why the book of the Changes is consummate.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:019 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:020 -->

**Master Lu:** Heaven and Earth taking their positions and change proceeding between them is the fullness of Heaven and Earth's virtue and work. Understanding and ritual practice being preserved, and the Way and rightness issuing forth, is the fullness of the sage's virtue and work.[^j14-lu]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:020 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:021 -->

**Wu Yueshen:** The inexhaustible issuing forth of the Way and rightness resembles the Changes' unceasing generation. Yet unceasing generation has never been possible without unceasing preservation.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:021 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:022 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** “Gateway” must not be explained solely as an exit: it includes both going out and coming in. When understanding is exalted within, the myriad principles arise from it: this is the gateway through which the Way issues. When ritual practice is lowly without, the myriad actions are completed through it: this is the gateway through which rightness enters. In correspondence with the four virtues, understanding belongs to winter, ritual practice to summer; the Way here is humaneness and belongs to spring, while rightness belongs to autumn. Humaneness governs issuing forth and coming into operation. Yet unless the one heart is open and clear, illuminating all principles, there is no source for that operation. Rightness governs entering and gathering inward. Yet unless every action and every good is fully present and complete, there is no ground for that gathering. This is the subtlety of movement and stillness rooted in one another in creative transformation, of being manifest in humaneness and hidden in its working. In the human being, it is nature's virtue: the Way that unites inner and outer.[^j14-gateway-reading]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:022 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:023 -->

**General Discussion — Xiang Anshi:** This chapter speaks of the sage embodying the Changes in his person. Understanding exhausts the source of all principles, as Qian initiates the myriad things. Ritual practice follows the standards of all principles, as Kun completes the myriad things. The Way is the underlying constitution of rightness, what wisdom knows. Rightness is the activity of the Way, what ritual practice carries out.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:023 -->

## Chapter 8 · Images, Deliberation, and Conduct

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:024 -->

### The sages had the means to discern the world's intricate complexity. They modeled its forms and appearances, imaging what was fitting to each thing. Hence these are called images.[^j14-ze]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:024 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:025 -->

**Original Meaning.** “Intricate complexity” means a tangled multiplicity. “Images” are the images of the hexagrams, such as those listed in the Discussion of the Trigrams.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:025 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:026 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:026 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:027 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu:** Ze means a tangled multiplicity. In antiquity this character did not exist; it was simply the character for clamorous speech. Its present component, 𦣞, also means a mouth. It is like the word in the Zuo Commentary's “clamorous and numerous words”: much confused talk issuing from a mouth. This is why the following text says it must not be disliked. Earlier scholars often took ze to mean the utmost subtlety. But on that reading, why say it must not be disliked? Ze simply means something tangled, cumbersome, and noisy.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:027 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:028 -->

**Wu Cheng:** Images, rather than Judgments, are set against line statements because, before King Wen appended the Judgments, the name given to the doubled trigrams was “images.” Images precede Judgments; speaking of images therefore includes the Judgments within them.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:028 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:029 -->

**Hu Bingwen:** Modeling concerns the image before it is complete; an image is the model once determined. Consider Qian and Kun alone. Before drawing them, one models the forms and appearances of yin and yang, and so produces undivided and divided lines. Once drawn, these are images. After drawing them, one also takes as images Heaven and Earth, head and belly, ox and horse, and such things as metal, jade, cauldron, and cloth. All are images.[^j14-image-correspondences]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:029 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:030 -->

**Zheng Weiyue:** Modeling occurs in the heart; imaging occurs in the lines.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:030 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:031 -->

**Zhang Zhenyuan:** Modeling their forms and appearances means modeling yin and yang before the hexagrams are drawn. Imaging what is fitting to things is precisely the drawing of the hexagrams. Modeling models what is to be imaged; imaging images what has been modeled. To speak of what is fitting to a thing means not merely resembling its form, but also fully expressing its principle.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:031 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:032 -->

### The sages had the means to discern the world's movements. They observed their convergences and free passages, so as to carry out their constant rules of propriety. They appended statements to determine their good fortune and misfortune. Hence these are called line statements.[^j14-huitong]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:032 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:033 -->

**Original Meaning.** “Convergence” is where principles gather and none can be left out. “Free passage” is where principle can be put into practice without obstruction. In Cook Ding's cutting up an ox, the convergence is the tangled joint; the passage is the space within it.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:033 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:034 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:034 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:035 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu:** Convergence concerns where things gather; free passage concerns what is fitting in affairs. Convergence is where the many principles come together. Although there seem to be all sorts of difficulties and obstructions, within them one must find a principle that permits passage before one can act. In the principles of affairs, if one does not understand the point of convergence but sees only one side, how can one proceed freely? One must understand everything at the convergence; within it there will of itself be a way through. “Propriety” here is also used broadly: it includes all the constant principles of things and affairs.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:035 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:036 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu, further:** Convergence without free passage leaves one blocked and unable to act. Free passage without convergence leaves one ignorant of the many intricacies, straight and crooked, that are intertwined there.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:036 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:037 -->

**Wu Cheng:** Convergences and free passages mean the principles of the great mean and utmost correctness, unconfined by any one-sided or partial obstruction. The sages saw the diverse movements of the world and observed their supremely good principles in order to act. When principle is understood precisely, action is fitting. They made the methods of handling affairs into statements and appended them beneath each line, so that someone casting the stalks and encountering that line would meet good fortune by acting accordingly, and misfortune by acting otherwise.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:037 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:038 -->

**Hu Bingwen:** Without convergence, principles are omitted: how could there be free passage? Without free passage, principles are obstructed: how could there be action? Free passage is attaining the mean appropriate to the time; constant rules are the enduring norm.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:038 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:039 -->

**Cai Qing:** Observing convergences and free passages and carrying out constant rules of propriety still refers to movements in the world, not yet to their embodiment in the Changes. Attaching these principles to the Changes to determine good fortune and misfortune is what makes line statements line statements. They thus model the world's movements, and so are called yao.[^j14-yao]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:039 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:040 -->

**Zhao Guangda:** Free passage is the passage within convergence. Spoken of in terms of the principles of affairs, it is called free passage; spoken of in terms of the sages establishing it as a constant rule, it is called constant propriety. Dian means constant. Propriety is principle capable of being practiced.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:040 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:041 -->

**He Kai:** Convergence is like a provincial capital: people coming from elsewhere can reach it from every side. Free passage is like a city open to all routes: setting out from it, one can reach every direction.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:041 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:042 -->

**Qian Chengzhi:** At the convergence of tangled circumstances, people see obstruction; the sages, observing it, necessarily find free passage. This is not acting by a provisional expedient, but carrying out constant propriety: it is firmly grounded and cannot be altered.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:042 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:043 -->

### They speak of the world's utmost complexity, yet it must not be disdained; they speak of the world's utmost movement, yet it must not be thrown into disorder.[^j14-complexity-movement]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:043 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:044 -->

**Original Meaning.** “Disdained” means disliked or wearied of.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:044 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:045 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:045 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:046 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu:** People easily grow weary of tangled things. Yet these are all matters that properly belong within principle and must be understood; therefore they must not be disdained. Movement likewise properly belongs there. Each movement has its own principle; thus it must not be disordered.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:046 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:047 -->

**Wu Cheng:** The meanings of the sixty-four hexagrams reveal the world's most hidden meanings in fitting names and words. People find them easy to understand and so do not grow weary of their complexity. The statements of the three hundred and eighty-four lines encompass the world's countless affairs and settle them precisely. People find them easy to follow and so do not confuse their movements.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:047 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:048 -->

**Pan Shizao:** Utmost unity is present; therefore complexity need not be disdained. Utmost constancy is present; therefore movement need not be disordered.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:048 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:049 -->

### Model before speaking; deliberate before acting. Through modeling and deliberation, bring change and transformation to completion.[^j14-model-deliberate]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:049 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:050 -->

**Original Meaning.** Observe the images and ponder the statements; observe the changes and ponder the prognostications. Then put them into practice as one's model. The seven lines below are examples of this.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:050 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:051 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:051 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:052 -->

**Wang Zongchuan:** Modeling before speaking means modeling one's speech on these images. Speech modeled on them has substance. Deliberating before acting means considering these lines before acting. Action so considered takes place only at its proper time.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:052 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:053 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu:** Modeling and deliberation simply mean measuring one's own speech and action, making them agree with this principle: changing in order to follow the Way.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:053 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:054 -->

**Hu Bingwen:** The sages modeled images before completing them; how could students of the Changes fail to model before speaking? With the lines, the sages necessarily observed convergences and free passages in order to carry out constant propriety. How could students following their example fail to deliberate before acting? The change and transformation mentioned earlier were those of the Changes. Here, bringing change and transformation to completion concerns the students of the Changes themselves.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:054 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:055 -->

### “A crane calls in the shade; its young answers it. I have a fine cup of wine; with you I will drink it.” The Master said: “The noble person remains in his room, yet if the words he speaks are good, people a thousand li away respond to him—how much more those nearby! He remains in his room, yet if the words he speaks are not good, people a thousand li away turn from him—how much more those nearby! Words issue from one's person and reach the people; conduct begins nearby and becomes visible far away. Speech and conduct are the noble person's hinge and trigger. Their release determines honor and disgrace. Through speech and conduct the noble person moves Heaven and Earth. Can he fail to be careful?”[^j14-crane]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:055 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:056 -->

**Original Meaning.** This explains the meaning of Inner Sincerity's nine in the second place.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:056 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:057 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:057 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:058 -->

**Han Kangbo:** The crane calls in the shade; sameness of qi brings an answering call. Words spoken in one's courtyard may find a response a thousand li away. If this is true even of speech, how much more of greater things! If a response comes from a thousand li away, how much more from nearby! Thus concern over regret and humiliation rests in the slightest dividing point, and determining loss or gain requires care at the hinge and trigger. The noble person therefore acts only after modeling and deliberating, taking care over what is minute.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:058 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:059 -->

**Cai Yuan:** Remaining in one's room corresponds to being in the shade; speaking corresponds to calling; the response from a thousand li away corresponds to the answering call. What influences and responds is the heart. Speech is the heart's voice, conduct its trace. Speech and conduct are therefore the hinge and trigger of influence and response.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:059 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:060 -->

**Bao Ba:** “Words issue from one's person and reach the people; conduct begins nearby and becomes visible far away.” The hinge moves and the door opens; the trigger moves and the arrow flies. On a small scale they bring honor or disgrace; on a great scale they move Heaven and Earth. In each case something calls here and is answered there: influence and response at their swiftest.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:060 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:061 -->

**Wang Dizhi:** Read “remaining in his room” in light of “in the shade.” Inner Sincerity means sincerity accumulating within. In the shade, within one's room, one should be especially watchful over oneself when alone, cultivating speech and conduct to advance in sincerity.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:061 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:062 -->

### “Fellowship: first crying aloud, afterward laughing.” The Master said: “The noble person's Way may lead him out into service or keep him at home, may call for silence or for speech. When two people share one heart, its sharpness cuts through metal. Words from one heart are fragrant as lan.”[^j14-one-heart]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:062 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:063 -->

**Original Meaning.** This explains the meaning of Fellowship's nine in the fifth place. The noble person's ways may seem different at first, yet in the end there is truly nothing between them. Cutting metal and resembling lan mean that nothing can divide them and their words have savor.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:063 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:064 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:064 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:065 -->

**Han Kangbo:** In service or retirement, silence or speech, noble people do not depart from what is within. Though their outward courses differ, when their Way is the same, they respond to one another.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:065 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:066 -->

**Geng Nanzhong:** Service or retirement, silence or speech, may result from things coming between them and making their outward courses different. Though their courses differ, their hearts are one, and so things cannot divide them forever. Their sharpness cuts metal: what intervened is removed. Once it is removed, they unite. Thus the text adds, “Words from one heart are fragrant as lan”: their mutual affection does not weary.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:066 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:067 -->

**Conversations of Master Zhu:** The sharpness of one heart cuts through even the hardness of metal and stone. “Cuts” means cutting into two pieces.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:067 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:068 -->

**Yu Yan:** Service or retirement, speech or silence, express “first crying aloud, afterward laughing.” Two people sharing one heart, cutting metal, and the fragrance of lan express their meeting.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:068 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:069 -->

**Qian Zhili:** Cutting metal speaks of the firmness of their hearts and intentions: things cannot divide them. Resembling lan speaks of their unity of breath and savor: things cannot adulterate it.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:069 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:070 -->

### “First Six: Spread white cogongrass beneath it. No blame.” The Master said: “Merely placing it on the ground would have sufficed. When one spreads grass beneath it, what blame could there be? This is care at its utmost. Grass is a slight thing, yet its use can carry great weight. Proceed with care according to this method, and nothing will go amiss.”[^j14-white-grass]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:070 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:071 -->

**Original Meaning.** This explains the meaning of Great Excess's first six.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:071 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:072 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:072 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:073 -->

**Cheng Jingcheng:** Affairs succeed through care and fail through neglect. How much more in a time of Great Excess, when circumstances are difficult: wherever care fails to reach, something goes amiss. Hence the image of utmost care. One would rather err on the side of apprehensive caution.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:073 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:074 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** “Grass is a slight thing, yet its use can carry great weight” must be read against the hexagram's meaning. The ridgepole is its image because it bears a heavy burden. Compared with a ridgepole, grass is slight indeed. Yet although a ridgepole bears weight, it remains liable to sag; thus those undertaking great affairs constantly fear collapse. Spread grass on the ground, however, and even a heavy object need cause no fear of toppling. Is this not a slight thing whose use can carry great weight? From antiquity, planning great affairs has necessarily been founded on a careful heart. This is why Great Excess, though its meaning calls for firmness, takes the yieldingness of the first line as its foundation.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:074 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:075 -->

### “Modest in accomplishment. The noble person carries things through to the end. Good fortune.” The Master said: “To labor without boasting, to accomplish without claiming the merit: this is substantial virtue at its utmost. It speaks of placing oneself below others despite one's achievements. Virtue calls for fullness; ritual practice calls for reverence. Modesty is bringing reverence to its utmost so as to preserve one's position.”[^j14-modesty]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:075 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:076 -->

**Original Meaning.** This explains the meaning of Modesty's nine in the third place. “Virtue calls for fullness; ritual practice calls for reverence” means that virtue should be full and ritual practice reverent.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:076 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:077 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:077 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:078 -->

**Yang Wanli:** Whether a person is modest or arrogant depends on the depth or shallowness of virtue. Deep virtue shows no complacent expression; shallow virtue offers no humble words. It is like bells and chime-stones: the thicker they are, the more measured their sound; with thin ones it is the reverse. Thus only the most substantial virtue can labor and accomplish without boasting or claiming merit. The fuller one's virtue, the more reverent one's practice.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:078 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:079 -->

### “The overreaching dragon has regret.” The Master said: “Noble yet without a position, high yet without the people; worthy people hold positions below, yet give no assistance. Therefore movement brings regret.”[^j14-dragon]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:079 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:080 -->

**Original Meaning.** This explains the meaning of Qian's top nine. It belongs in the Commentary on the Words; its appearance here is probably a repetition.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:080 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:081 -->

**Collected Explanations.**

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:081 -->

**Zhang Zhenyuan:** “Called change” and “called free passage” mean that change and free passage arise through transforming and shaping, extending and carrying out. “Rests in change” and “rests in free passage” mean that transforming and shaping, extending and carrying out, are enacted through change and free passage.

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:371 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** Transforming and shaping, extending and carrying out, conclude “changed and carried through to realize benefit fully.” From “making it numinous and clear” onward, the text concludes “stirred and inspired to bring the numinous fully into play.” Earlier, transforming, shaping, extending, and carrying out spoke generally of principles between Heaven and Earth; hence “called change” and “called free passage.” Here they speak of what is contained in the book of the Changes; hence “rests in change” and “rests in free passage.” At the points of change in its Way, one sees the subtlety of the sages' transforming and shaping; at the points of free passage, one sees the goodness of their extending and carrying out. “Numinous” in making it numinous and clear is rooted in stirring and inspiring to bring the numinous fully into play. The statements' capacity to stir people certainly suffices to bring the numinous fully into play. Yet the numinous of the human heart must accord with that of the Changes before one is stirred and inspired without knowing how. This is making it numinous and clear. Completing it in silence and being trustworthy without words is how such numinous illumination becomes possible.

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:371 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:372 -->

**General Discussion — Hu Bingwen:** The upper Appended Statements has twelve chapters, yet at the end it says, “writing does not exhaust words; words do not exhaust meaning.” It seeks to have students realize for themselves what lies beyond writing and words. From establishing images to express meaning fully through “stirring the world's movements rests in the statements”, it has repeatedly expounded the Changes' writing and words to the fullest. Yet it ends, “completing it in silence, being trustworthy without words, rests in virtue and conduct.” Can the Changes, then, truly be exhausted by writing and words? What is attained in the heart is virtue; what is practiced in one's person is conduct. The Changes as present in people therefore has its presence in heart and person, not merely in writing and words.[^j14-hu-final-reading]

<!-- END TRANSLATION: eee-4705:372 -->

<!-- BEGIN TRANSLATION: eee-4705:373 -->

**Editorial Judgment.** This chapter gathers the meaning of the previous eleven and discusses it as a whole. Words do not exhaust meaning: thus images are established to express meaning fully, referring to Fuxi. Writing does not exhaust words: thus statements are appended to the images to express their words fully, referring to King Wen and the Duke of Zhou. Images suffice to express meaning because what words point out is limited, while what images encompass is inexhaustible. Statements appended to images suffice to express words because images are representative rather than literal and encompass without limit; their statements too are figurative expressions containing inexhaustible meaning. Changing and carrying through to realize benefit fully is principle already present in the images, through which good and ill fortune are determined. Stirring and inspiring to bring the numinous fully into play is the subtlety unfolded by the statements, through which persistent endeavor is completed. Speaking of Qian and Kun traces the source of images. Because Heaven and Earth exist, there is transformation. Seen while confined to form, they are simply formed things; seen beyond form, they are the source of the Way. Shaping Heaven and Earth's transformation is how human affairs change; extending and enacting their principle of free passage is how human affairs proceed freely. This alone is how sages from antiquity have determined the world's work. Therefore the sages who made the Changes observed Qian and Kun as formed things and established images, extended their activity of change and free passage, and set out statements. They made a place here for all later people who wish to shape transformation and carry it into practice. Their achievement may indeed be called great. Yet although images suffice to express meaning fully, there is the Changes before the lines were drawn: hence the value of completing it in silence. Although statements suffice to express words fully, there is meaning beyond words: hence the value of being trustworthy without speaking. This is what is meant by making it numinous and clear: learning not merely through the wording one observes and ponders, and understanding not merely through the superficial work of mouth and ear. Virtue and conduct mean attaining the principle of ease and simplicity.[^j14-end]

## Translator’s Endnotes

The base is the repository’s unchanged 易學網 text. Doubtful readings were compared selectively with [the Siku Quanshu transcription, fixed revision 528603](<https://zh.wikisource.org/w/index.php?title=御纂周易折中_(四庫全書本)/卷14&oldid=528603>). Corrections, repunctuation, supplementary material, and unresolved readings are distinguished below. This is not a systematic facsimile collation. Added descriptive chapter headings are navigational, not translations of source headings.

[^j14-knowledge-practice]: 知崇禮卑 contrasts elevated understanding with lowly, concrete practice, not superior knowledge with inferior ritual. 禮 retains its historical sense of ritual propriety while extending, in these commentaries, to fitting conduct in affairs. 清濁, “clarity and turbidity,” refers to the traditional differentiation of qi, not to a moral condemnation of practical work. 易簡 continues the “ease and simplicity” of Juan Thirteen.

[^j14-master-said]: Zhu Xi’s attribution of all Ten Wings to Confucius and his consequent doubt about 子曰 are translated as his historical position. His doubt does not warrant deleting the formula from the received text. “The Master said” is retained wherever the base gives it; this translation does not independently authenticate the attribution.

[^j14-preserving-nature]: 成性 is explained here as nature already complete, not a nature manufactured by the practitioner. The repetition 存存 conveys continuous preservation. The source’s summary “This is Chapter Seven” belongs to Zhu Xi’s commentary, not to the canonical Appended Statements; the six such summaries in this juan are separately labeled.

[^j14-lu]: The source attributes this gloss only to 盧氏, “Master Lu.” No given name has been supplied conjecturally.

[^j14-gateway-reading]: The base’s 萬里, literally “ten thousand li,” is corrected to 萬理, “the myriad principles,” supported by the parallel Siku Quanshu transcription, revision 528603. The compilers deliberately disagree with interpretations that make the gateway only an exit: understanding and humaneness issue outward, while ritual practice and rightness complete an inward movement. The English preserves their argument rather than harmonizing it with the preceding glosses.

[^j14-ze]: 賾 can denote depth or intricacy, but Zhu explicitly argues for confused multiplicity, relating it to 嘖, noisy speech. “Intricate complexity” in the canonical passage leaves room for the meanings argued by the commentators; Zhu’s stronger “tangled multiplicity” is not softened within his gloss. His account of the character’s formation is reported, not offered as an independently verified palaeographic conclusion.

[^j14-image-correspondences]: The list draws on traditional trigram images. Its paired objects are retained in their transmitted order; it should not be read as assigning every first item to Qian and every second item to Kun. 奇耦 refers here to undivided and divided lines, not merely odd and even integers.

[^j14-huitong]: 會通 is translated through its two components, “convergences and free passages,” because the commentators carefully distinguish the gathering of relevant principles from the unobstructed course through them. Cook Ding’s ox-cutting is an allusion to the Zhuangzi: the joint and the opening represent complexity and passage, not a licence to disregard complexity. 典禮 is extended here beyond ceremonial rules to constant principles of fitting conduct.

[^j14-yao]: Yao 爻 denotes the line and, by extension here, its attached statement. The commentarial association with 效, “model” or “imitate,” explains the wording; this is not an assertion that the line is merely its written prognostication.

[^j14-complexity-movement]: The canonical wording leaves room for both “must not be disdained/disordered” and “does not become wearisome/disordered.” Zhu stresses the student’s obligation not to reject complexity, while Wu stresses how fitting exposition makes complexity intelligible. Their different emphases remain visible.

[^j14-model-deliberate]: 擬議 concerns reflecting upon images and statements to regulate actual speech and conduct. The seven following cases belong to this chapter’s own exemplification, not to an added modern practice manual. “Ponder” translates 玩 in the sense of sustained appreciative examination, not idle play.

[^j14-crane]: The base’s 嗚鶴 is corrected to 鳴鶴, “a calling crane,” supported by the parallel transcription. The line quotation follows the rendering in Juan Eight. 千里 is a conventional great distance, left as “a thousand li” rather than assigned a modern mileage. 樞機 concretely joins a door hinge and a weapon’s release mechanism: Bao Ba supplies their physical actions. 慎獨 is watchfulness over oneself when alone, not withdrawal from all human relations.

[^j14-one-heart]: 金 means metal here; translating “gold” would unnecessarily narrow the image of cutting through what divides people. 蘭 is retained as lan, a traditional fragrant plant: “orchid” can misleadingly impose a modern botanical identification. 出處 concerns public service and remaining out of office, not merely physical coming and going.

[^j14-white-grass]: 茅 is the white grass used as a protective or ritual underlay. 用可重 plays on substantial value and the weight borne; the compilers explicitly develop the latter. The translation therefore retains “carry great weight” rather than reducing the phrase to “be highly valued.” 錯 here is placing, not making a mistake.

[^j14-modesty]: 不德 does not deny virtue: it means not claiming one’s accomplishment as a merit owed recognition. 厚, “thickness,” gives moral substantiality its physical image in Yang Wanli’s comparison with bells and chime-stones. The rendering of the cited line follows Juan Three.

[^j14-dragon]: Zhu’s suggestion that this passage is repeated from the Wenyan 文言 is preserved but not used to delete it. Wenyan is rendered “Commentary on the Words,” distinct from the Judgment Commentary and Image Commentary. “Without a position” means without the effective office associated with the fifth line, despite being above it.

[^j14-discretion]: 幾事 refers to affairs at a delicate or incipient point. 密 conveys discretion and keeping matters close; it does not make every form of secrecy a virtue. The source applies an image of physical restraint to speech. 失身, “losing one’s person,” can encompass ruin or loss of life; the English does not reduce it to embarrassment.

[^j14-raiders]: The line quotation follows Juan Six. 上慢下暴 is intentionally translated without deciding its disputed syntax in advance: Chen takes it as the petty official insulting superiors and oppressing inferiors; Hu and the compilers emphasize the superiors’ failure in appointments. The historical comparison 冶容誨淫 assigns causal responsibility to seductive display. It is retained as the source’s social judgment, not endorsed as an excuse for sexual misconduct or an attribution of blame to its victims. 伐 carries the idea of attack accompanied by a stated charge, as Zhao explains.

[^j14-number-order]: The rearrangement discussed by Zhu is already part of the selected edition’s text. No further rearrangement has been made here. 簡, “strip,” is the traditional textual unit invoked to explain dislocation; translating it does not independently establish a specific physical manuscript accident. This juan follows the edition’s chapter numbering, Seven–Twelve.

[^j14-river-chart]: The River Chart 河圖 is a numerical arrangement, distinct from the Luo Writing 洛書 discussed later. The Four Images are old yang, young yin, young yang, and old yin, conventionally numbered nine, eight, seven, and six. Zhu distinguishes their positions from their numbers. “West and north” and “east and south” translate paired directions, not single northwest and southeast points. The commentary’s diagrammatic claims are preserved, not substituted with a modern reconstruction.

[^j14-five-phases]: Guo Yong explicitly rejects treating Five-Phase numerology as part of the Changes’ own Way; Zhu’s next gloss explicitly employs it. The disagreement is substantive and has not been reconciled by the translator. The Han treatise is cited as the source’s evidence, not as an independently collated edition in this project.

[^j14-five-positions]: Zhu distinguishes consecutive odd-even pairings from pairs separated by five. Gong and the compilers offer different spatial explanations. The arithmetic totals are straightforward: 1+3+5+7+9=25 and 2+4+6+8+10=30. The further cosmological meanings belong to the commentators. 變 and 化 remain “change” and “transformation” where their distinction is explicitly used.

[^j14-great-expansion]: 大衍 is “Great Expansion.” Its fifty, the forty-nine used stalks, and Heaven and Earth’s fifty-five are distinct totals and must not be conflated. The Two and Three mean the Two Modes and the Three Powers: Heaven and Earth; Heaven, Earth, and humanity. The canonical rendering here follows Zhu’s procedural reading. The source’s supplementary section below disputes what 奇 and 扐 denote and when the hanging is repeated; that dispute is translated in full.

[^j14-calendar]: The references to two intercalations in five years and intervals of roughly thirty-two months are the commentators’ calendrical explanation of the symbolism. They are not a modern rule for locating intercalary months in any particular five-year interval. Zhu counts one hanging, two countings, and two remainder-holdings as five operations for this analogy; the later “four operations” groups the procedure differently.

[^j14-remainder-totals]: The stated residual totals are consistent with subtracting the retained stalks from forty-nine: 49−36=13, 49−24=25, 49−28=21, and 49−32=17. Each complementary pair sums to 38; 38×6=228. This confirms the arithmetic, not the proposed cosmological necessity or the calendrical generalization.

[^j14-handling-stalks]: Zhu explicitly takes the hung stalk from the right-hand portion, whereas Kong’s preceding explanation speaks of Heaven’s portion. This difference is preserved. Zhu’s remainder is one through four: a portion divisible by four retains four, not zero. His finger numbering is retained rather than silently replaced with a modern hand diagram. “A change” here is one processing round; three such rounds form a line.

[^j14-counted-remainders]: 過揲 refers to stalks counted through and remaining in the working total after the hung stalks and remainders have been removed. 歸奇 refers to the set-aside remainders. Zhu’s family metaphor ranks proposed explanatory grounds; it does not describe physical generations. The English preserves his argument against restricting interpretation to retained-stalk arithmetic.

[^j14-zhang-procedure]: The base’s 劫 in Zhang’s definition is corrected to 扐, supported by the parallel transcription. This supplementary account hangs one only at the beginning of each line, not at every processing round. It is a genuine procedural disagreement, not an alternative description of an identical operation. Master Hengqu is Zhang Zai.

[^j14-le]: Guo argues from the distinction between 奇 and 扐 and from the related word 仂, a fractional share. His rejection of “spaces between the fingers” is preserved as his argument; the translator does not silently replace the preceding Zhu procedure with it. The source heading 附錄 marks this whole supplementary discussion: it is not a translator’s addition.

[^j14-compromise]: The compilers favor Zhu’s repeated hanging procedure but accept the Guos’ distinction between the hung odd stalk and the remainders, combining both in the image of intercalation. 氣盈朔虛 concerns the surplus associated with the solar cycle and the shortfall associated with lunar months. Their compromise is translated as stated, not converted into a new operational rule.

[^j14-year-total]: The text uses the retained stalks for old lines: 6×36=216, 6×24=144, total 360. Zhu himself identifies 360 as a rounded annual number and gives the traditional 365¼-day reckoning. The symbolic total is not a correction to the calendar, nor a claim that every casting of Qian or Kun must contain only changing lines; Zhu’s letter expressly denies that.

[^j14-circle-square]: The round-three and square-four analogy is the source’s traditional numerical model. It should not be mistaken for an exact geometrical assertion about circumference. The method assigns three to the lesser remainder-group and two to the greater: totals 9, 8, 7, 6 correspond to retained-stalk totals 36, 32, 28, 24. 太陽 and 老陽 designate old or great yang here, not the sun; likewise 太陰 and 老陰 are old or great yin.

[^j14-sixty]: The ten stems and twelve branches form the traditional sixtyfold cycle; this is not multiplication of ten by twelve. The next example does multiply five by twelve. Zhu’s argument for sixty as a universal measure is reported without extending the arithmetic examples into an independently established universal law.

[^j14-two-parts]: 二篇 means the Upper and Lower Classic, not the two parts of the Appended Statements. Using the old-line values, 192×36+192×24=11,520. 萬物, “the myriad things,” is a totality expression: the passage does not provide a census of 11,520 kinds of objects.

[^j14-four-operations]: The four 營 are procedure-types, counting the two hands together as one counting operation and their remainders together as one lodging operation. Zhu’s earlier fivefold calendrical analogy counts one hanging, two hand-countings, and two remainder-holdings separately. Three processing rounds give a line; nine give the inner trigram; eighteen give six lines. These counts are distinct from the number of line changes after a hexagram has been cast.

[^j14-4096]: The 4,096 are the 64×64 possible pairings of an initial and resulting hexagram. The compilers’ twelve-line representation stacks the two six-line figures; it does not replace the canonical sixty-four with a newly asserted canon of twelve-line figures. Their cun 寸, chi 尺, and zhang 丈 are successively larger traditional measures, in a tenfold progression. The units are retained rather than assigned inexact modern equivalents.

[^j14-assisting]: 神 is kept as “the numinous” when it names unfathomable efficacy, and as “spirits” where the wording invokes agents or their counsel. The range is present in the source itself. 酬酢 originally evokes answering hospitality and here means responsive exchange. Zhang’s contrast of danger becoming peaceful and ease being overturned concerns vigilance versus complacency, not a recommendation to seek harm.

[^j14-numinous-method]: The commentators argue that numerical and oracular operations disclose numinous transformation. These assertions are translated as their understanding of the Changes, not presented as independent empirical proof of predictive power. Zhu’s distinction between the Master’s saying and his disciples’ attribution is again retained without editorial deletion.

[^j14-introduction]: 啓蒙 denotes Zhu Xi’s 易學啟蒙, Introduction to the Study of the Changes, rather than an unspecified elementary primer. Its material is treated later in this compilation. The source’s comment on lost official details limits its own procedural account; the translation does not invent those details.

[^j14-four-ways]: 辭、變、象、占 are statements, changes, images, and prognostications. 卜筮 combines shell-divination and stalk-divination, but Zhu explicitly denies that the shell procedure uses the Changes’ prognostications. His answer and Kong’s broader analogy are both retained. Cai also insists that the four uses are not all occasions of casting.

[^j14-question-words]: 問焉而以言 is genuinely disputed within Zhu’s transmitted explanations: the Original Meaning takes it as consulting and then using the statements in speech and action, while the Conversations takes the words as the question addressed to the stalks. The canonical translation leaves the phrase open enough for both. 嚮 functions as 響, an echo. 命 here is the stated divinatory charge, not “fate.”

[^j14-three-five]: 參伍 may mean numerical operations with threes and fives or cross-comparison more generally. Zhu acknowledges its difficulty, gives both numerical explanation and historical usage, while the compilers emphasize interweaving. “Reckon by threes and fives” preserves the literal expression without presenting it as an independently reconstructed extra step in the stalk ritual. Counting five threes and three fives reaches fifteen; this elementary equality explains Zhu’s illustration, not the entire disputed term.

[^j14-number-crosschecks]: The citations from Xunzi, Han Fei, the Records of the Historian, and the Book of Han are translated as quoted within this compilation. They illustrate related senses of comparison and verification; their full original contexts have not been systematically collated here. “Interlace and gather” preserves the two distinct spatial actions Zhu explains, rather than reducing 錯綜 to a vague “calculate.”

[^j14-still-response]: The subject first identified by Zhu is the apparatus of stalks and hexagrams, not an injunction to make the practitioner insensible. The same pattern is then applied to the heart. 故 is the causes or intelligible grounds of affairs. 遂 has a repeated “thereupon” or immediate-consequence force, which Zhang notes explicitly; no claim of a modern physical transmission mechanism is implied.

[^j14-cheng-nonaction]: Cheng’s apparent “never advocated nonaction” followed by a quotation containing “no action” is deliberate: he distinguishes the Changes’ balanced account of stillness and response from what he treats as a one-sided doctrine. The English preserves his polemical distinction rather than harmonizing his interpretation with Laozi.

[^j14-depth-incipience]: 幾 is the incipient: a development at its first, barely perceptible movement. It is not an unspecified supernatural omen. 研 emphasizes close investigation, complementary to 極深, plumbing what is hidden in depth.

[^j14-swift]: Zhang’s body analogy concerns unified responsiveness. It is translated as his philosophical example, not as a modern physiological account. The shifting application of depth and incipience to the Changes and to the sage is explicit in Kong’s explanation and is retained.

[^j14-four-correspondences]: The compilers prefer one of Zhu’s two different assignments of the four uses to refinement and change. Their disagreement with the Original Meaning is consequential and remains visible. The bare 本義 label and the following split chapter summary occupy separate blocks in the base; all three blocks are retained without inventing additional commentary.

[^j14-opening-things]: 開物 is kept as “opens things” in the canonical passage because the commentators unpack it. Zhu explicitly interprets 物 here as people and opening as opening their understanding. The account of ancient simplicity is his historical conception, not an independently established description of early society. 冒 is encompassing or covering, not recklessness.

[^j14-round-square]: Round and square describe unbounded movement and determinate differentiation, not simply the physical shapes of stalks and written figures. 易以貢 is understood by Zhu as changing to inform people. The contrast of 神 and 知 is rendered numinous efficacy and discernment, without collapsing them into a single word “intuition.”

[^j14-determinate-affairs]: The base’s 享有定理 is corrected to 事有定理, “affairs have determinate principles,” supported by the parallel transcription. Zhu’s claim that the sage knows without casting is preserved as a characterization of sagehood; it is not a promise that readers can reliably foretell events without evidence. “Numinously martial” retains the source’s political and martial language, which Kong explicitly interprets through authority without killing.

[^j14-coming-past]: 藏往 does not mean merely remembering historical events. He Kai defines “what has gone before” as a pattern already present and determinate, contrasted with a development signaled but not yet arrived. His explanation is important for the text’s contrast of movable stalks with established hexagram configurations.

[^j14-purification]: 齋戒 retains ritual purification and alert restraint, rather than being reduced to meditation or simple relaxation. Zhu expressly explains it through 敬, reverent attentiveness, and distinguishes possessing the principle from consulting the oracle. 前民用 means guiding or opening the way for the people’s use, not placing the sage’s private benefit first.

[^j14-doors]: The sequence moves from activity and appearance to bodily form, made implements, methods, and shared use. 器 can mean a formed thing as well as a manufactured tool; the commentaries move between these senses. The closing/opening image is not itself a prescription for breathing or bodily energy practice. No such instruction has been added.

[^j14-taiji]: The base writes 大極 in the canonical passage and Zhu’s gloss, alongside 太極 elsewhere. Both are rendered “Supreme Ultimate”; no separate concept is inferred from the graphic variation. This is the Taiji of the cosmological commentary, not the name of a martial practice. Zhu’s Four Images are two-line figures; the commentators do not all explain the sequence identically.

[^j14-shao-images]: Shao defines the Four Images here as yin, yang, firmness, and yieldingness, and makes the Two Modes the “ancestors” of Heaven and Earth. This differs in formulation from Zhu’s account of successively drawn one-, two-, and three-line figures. The difference is retained, including the order in which Shao names the eight trigrams.

[^j14-order-not-separation]: Zhu expressly distinguishes explanatory priority from separation: when speaking of actual generation, principle and yin-yang arise together; when tracing their explanatory order, principle is prior. The translation preserves this distinction rather than turning the sequence into an unqualified chronological creation story.

[^j14-wuji]: 無極而太極 is rendered literally here, “Without an ultimate, yet the Supreme Ultimate,” and interpreted by Zhu’s immediately preceding contrast of a formed summit with a formless principle. It is not translated as a sequence in which one separate entity creates another. Master Zhou is Zhou Dunyi.

[^j14-chen-taiji]: Chen’s critique of the Han treatise, Laozi, and Zhuangzi belongs to his philosophical position. The quotations are translated as he cites them; the English does not present his characterizations as neutral summaries of those traditions. His central claim is the identity, under different descriptions, of the Way and the Supreme Ultimate.

[^j14-xu-images]: Xu’s 奇參耦中 and 耦參奇中 describe the undivided and divided entering among their opposites, linked to Kan and Li. His exposition develops the Four Images through Qian, Kun, Kan, and Li, then the other four trigrams, rather than repeating Zhu’s two-line construction. The English does not silently redraw his scheme or equate all commentators’ uses of “Four Images.”

[^j14-great-uses]: 亹亹 is persistent endeavor, not simply happiness or a prediction of success. Zhu suspects an omission after 立 in 立成器; no missing words are invented in the translation. His gloss on wealth and noble rank specifies sovereign power, a qualification developed by the compilers rather than erased for modern readers.

[^j14-yu-ze]: The base’s 頤 at the start of Yu Yan’s gloss is corrected to 賾, supported by the parallel transcription. The gloss explains the same word used in “probing complexity”; it is not a reference to the hexagram Nourishment.

[^j14-chart-writing]: The River is the Yellow River; the Luo is the river named in the Luo Writing tradition. The accounts of the Chart, Writing, dragon, and tortoise are the tradition’s narratives and competing interpretations, not archaeological findings asserted by this translation. They are not all identical to the numerical diagrams familiar from later presentations.

[^j14-river-witnesses]: Kong names divergent interpretations: Zheng Kangcheng is Zheng Xuan; Fusi is Wang Bi. The Nine Categories and Sovereign Ultimate 皇極 belong to the Great Plan 洪範 tradition. 皇極 is therefore distinguished from the Supreme Ultimate 太極, rather than translated as if the two expressions were identical. Baoxi is the form of Fuxi’s name used by Guo Yong.

[^j14-four-ways-summary]: The compilers explain why the following quotation from Great Possession belongs here, directly anticipating their disagreement with Zhu’s proposal to relocate it. The base’s 為聖為之道 is corrected to 為聖人之道, “ways of the sages,” supported by the parallel transcription’s 爲聖人之道.

[^j14-heaven-help]: Zhu proposes relocating this quotation to Chapter Eight; He Kai and the compilers explicitly defend its present location. It remains where the base places it, at the beginning of the material concluded as Chapter Twelve. Compliance 順 here is accord with the Way, not indiscriminate obedience. The promise of assistance is translated as the text’s claim.

[^j14-fifth-line]: Both the base and the parallel transcription read 六五 in Zhu Zhen’s gloss, although the quoted statement is Great Possession’s top nine. The English retains the fifth-line application rather than silently changing it to top nine. Whether this is a deliberate application to the ruler or an inherited inconsistency remains open.

[^j14-words-images]: 情偽 includes genuine and false conditions or intentions; it is not flattened into neutral “information.” 象 retains its concrete sense of configurations and images, not a free licence for personal symbolism. 鼓舞 literally evokes drumming and dancing, but Zhu explicitly explains its operation as prompting and awakening; “stirred and inspired” follows that explanation.

[^j14-two-master-saids]: Both occurrences of 子曰 are retained, as in the base, within a single primary block. Zhu questions one as a later addition and compares the editorial treatment of Zhou Dunyi’s 通書, here “Comprehensive Writings.” His conjecture is translated without silently adopting it.

[^j14-ouyang]: “Master Ouyang” is the source’s 歐公. Zhu’s account of his objection is translated as a reported controversy, not converted into an independently adjudicated claim about authorship. The passage is important evidence that the compilation itself preserves disagreement over both authorship and interpretive method.

[^j14-establishing-hexagrams]: Cui takes 設卦 as doubling the trigrams into sixty-four hexagrams; Wu takes it as King Wen’s establishing their names. The compilers prefer the trigram-image reading of 立象 over Zhu’s two-line reading. These different accounts of formation and naming remain distinct.

[^j14-yan]: The compilers refer to 焉 in 繫辭焉: the statements are attached there, to the images. This grammatical link supports their argument that words can convey the images’ inexhaustible meaning without doing so independently. “There” is their grammatical observation, not an extra word added to the Chinese.

[^j14-store]: 緼 is what is enclosed or stored; Zhu compares it with garment padding. “Inward store” retains that image without making the term an abstract “essence.” Zhu explains the passage through drawn figures, while the compilers insist that it first concerns creative transformation and human affairs. Their explicit disagreement is preserved.

[^j14-above-below]: 形而上 and 形而下 are retained as “above form” and “below form.” The commentaries explicitly reject treating them as two separated substances or worlds. 器 here extends beyond manufactured tools to formed, particular things; “implement” preserves continuity with the earlier discussion of useful forms, while the glosses state the broader sense. 化裁、推行 concern human shaping and enactment here, not merely changing line values.

[^j14-silent-completion]: 信 is rendered “being trustworthy,” retaining the relation of inward attainment to conduct rather than treating it solely as assent to a doctrine. The commentary’s culmination is not rejection of the text: it distinguishes embodied realization from verbal explanation alone. 人 and 德 prevent “the numinous” from becoming an impersonal technique detached from the practitioner’s character.

[^j14-cheng-final-reading]: The base’s 四人 in Cheng’s final sequence is corrected to 因人, “through the person,” supported by the parallel transcription. The repeated structure is therefore lines/images → transformation → numinous → person → virtue and conduct, not a reference to four people.

[^j14-hu-final-reading]: The base’s 得乎辭 in Hu’s closing citation is corrected to 存乎辭, supported by the parallel transcription and the canonical passage itself. His quotation is therefore translated consistently as “stirring the world’s movements rests in the statements.” No additional passage is inserted.

[^j14-end]: The closing contrast of images and figurative statements with literal reference develops the source’s claim of inexhaustible meaning. It does not warrant replacing the received images with arbitrary inventions. The final reference to mouth and ear criticizes merely verbal learning; the compilers have just affirmed the value of studying images and statements. This completes Chapters Seven–Twelve and therefore the upper Appended Statements as arranged in this edition.
